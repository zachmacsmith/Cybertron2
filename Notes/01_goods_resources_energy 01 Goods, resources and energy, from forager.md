# 01: Goods, resources and energy, from foragers to the organic economy and past it (15 Nov 2026)

Part 1 of the 02zzj economy research folder. It goes deeper than the first frame in 02zzi (§1, §2) and grounds it in numbers. It
covers what goods there are and how they behave, what land gives and how its uses compete, what energy a society runs on, how each
need climbs a ladder of substitutes, and which intermediate goods held economies together. The weight is on the organic (pre-coal)
economy, but the span runs from foragers to the 21st century. Oil, gas, electricity, nuclear and renewables are covered only
briefly (§3.8), because other parts of this folder treat those industries in depth.

## Summary

**Land set the limit on everything before coal.** Food, fodder, fuel, timber, fibre, fats, dyes and most building materials all
grew on the same ground, at a few tonnes of dry matter per hectare a year. Around 1300, England's arable land gave roughly 1,500
to 1,800 kcal of grain a day per person, from wheat yields of three or four times the seed. A working horse needed several acres
of oats and hay. Each person burned one to two tonnes of wood a year. An iron furnace and forge needed thousands of acres of
coppice, and a forest glassworks burned 150 to 200 kg of wood for every kilogram of glass. Wrigley's "organic economy" is the name
for this limit. Energy per head stayed near 20 GJ a year in England in the 1560s, and about half of it came from the muscle of people
and draught animals. Mills (6,000 in 1086, perhaps 10,000 by 1300) and peat in the Netherlands raised the ceiling but did not
remove it. Coal did: it gave half of England's energy by 1700 and over 90% by 1850. Each later carrier (oil, gas, electricity,
nuclear, wind and solar) changed which goods could be made and where, more than it changed how much energy a person used at home.

**The structure of an economy is held by a few intermediate goods, and by the properties of goods more than by their category.**
Fuel and charcoal, cord and cloth, leather, worked wood, containers, lime, salt, fats, malt and potash appear in almost every chain
of the organic economy. Each of them is a place where chains from different land covers meet, and each of them later gained an
industrial substitute that merged several old chains into one: coal for wood and charcoal, kerosene and then electricity for
tallow, whale oil and wax, synthetic soda for wood ash, kelp and barilla, the Haber-Bosch process for manure, guano and saltpetre.
What decides a good's social effect is a short list of properties: how fast it spoils, how much value it carries per kilogram,
whether it is bound to a place, whether it comes jointly with other goods, how much skill it needs, and whether one person's use
excludes or reduces another's. The engine's numbers are already close to history where it has them (a seed ratio of 4:1; charcoal
at 20% of the wood's weight). What it lacks is the intermediates and the land claims that make fuel, fodder and fibre compete with
food.

## 1. A taxonomy of goods

### 1.1 By material

Material decides which land cover, which skill and which fuel a good depends on. It is the axis the engine already uses.

| Material | Comes from | Examples, foragers → village → early modern → industrial → 21st century |
|---|---|---|
| **plant food** | field, garden, wild | roots, nuts, seeds → grain, beans, greens → potatoes, maize, sugar → wheat flour by rail → processed food, cooking oil |
| **animal products** | herds, game, fish | game meat, hides, bone, sinew → meat, milk, cheese, wool, tallow, dung → salt beef, butter → refrigerated meat → industrial poultry, whey protein |
| **wood** | forest, coppice, hedges | firewood, spear shafts → timber, poles, withies, bark, charcoal → ship timber, staves, potash → pit props, railway sleepers, paper pulp → engineered wood, pellets |
| **fibre** | wild bast, flax, hemp, wool, later cotton | sinew, bast cord → linen, wool cloth, rope → cotton, sailcloth → mill cloth → polyester (from oil) |
| **earth and stone** | rock, clay pits, chalk, sand | flint, ochre → stone, clay, lime, daub → brick, tile, glass, mortar → Portland cement → concrete (the most used made material today) |
| **metal** | ore, bog iron, placer gold | none, then native copper → copper, bronze, bloomery iron → charcoal blast-furnace iron, steel by cementation → coke iron, Bessemer steel → steel, aluminium, copper wire, lithium |
| **salts and chemicals** | brine, seawater, ash, urine, mines | ochre, salt from springs → salt, wood ash, lye, alum → potash, saltpetre, soda (kelp, barilla) → Leblanc and Solvay soda, sulphuric acid → ammonia, chlorine, polymers |
| **energy carriers** | all of the above | food, fire → firewood, charcoal, fodder → peat, coal → coal, town gas → oil, gas, electricity, uranium, hydrogen |
| **information** | people, then records | skill, songs, maps in memory → custom, tallies → books, almanacs, price currents → newspapers, telegraph → software, data |

### 1.2 By economic behaviour

The categories economists use. Each does something different to a society. Examples run across eras.

| Kind | The test | What it does to a society | Examples across eras |
|---|---|---|---|
| **necessity** | demand barely rises with income (low income elasticity) | sets the floor; when it fails, people die or move; its price is political (bread riots, grain laws) | game and plant food (foragers); grain, salt, fuel (village to 19th c.); grain, energy, housing, water (today) |
| **luxury and status good** | demand rises faster than income; worth carrying far | drives long-distance trade, specialist crafts and display; marks rank; sumptuary laws try to fix who may use it | shells, amber, obsidian (foragers); furs, wine, spices, silk, fine cloth (medieval); sugar, tea, porcelain, tobacco (early modern, then mass goods); designer goods, cars (today) |
| **durable** | used over years | stores wealth in material form; creates inheritance, repair and second-hand trades | tools, houses, the plough, a bed, pewter; furniture and clocks (18th c.); cars, appliances (20th c.) |
| **capital good** | its use makes other goods | creates thresholds (worth building only above a scale of use), owners and users, rent and credit | the mill, oven, smithy, loom, tan pit, ship; the blast furnace, steam engine; the factory, power plant, data centre |
| **intermediate** | used up in making others | the joints of the economy; makes specialisation between steps possible; a shortage spreads to every user | charcoal, flour, leather, cord, malt, lime, potash, bar iron; sulphuric acid, steel; chips, electricity |
| **service** | work done for someone, not a thing | trades without stock; wages; dependence on persons; cannot be stored | healing, midwifery, guarding, carrying, milling for multure, judging; banking, insurance, teaching; most of today's employment |
| **common-pool resource** | rival (use reduces what is left) but hard to exclude | overuse, rules, stints, disputes; Ostrom's commons institutions | game, fish, pasture, woods, peat bogs, the river's water; groundwater, fisheries, the atmosphere |
| **club good** | non-rival up to crowding; can exclude non-members | membership, fees, queues, quarrels over access | the manor mill and oven, a weir, a guild's hall; toll roads, canals; cable networks, software subscriptions |
| **public good** | non-rival and non-excludable | needs a tax, a levy, a patron or a custom; free riders | a watch, a beacon, a market's peace, drainage of a fen, a sea wall; lighthouses, standards of weight; basic research, defence, public health |
| **money** | held to exchange, not to use | lowers the cost of trading with strangers and across time; becomes a lever for whoever issues it | shells and beads; silver pennies; bills of exchange, bank notes; fiat money, bank deposits, digital payments |
| **information good** | costly to make, cheap to copy; value depends on who else has it | who knows prices, methods and people decides who can trade, make and govern | knowledge of the land; craft secrets; printed price currents, maps, almanacs; patents; software, data |
| **energy** | what all work runs on | the hardest physical limit before coal (§3) | food, fire; fodder, firewood, charcoal, water and wind; coal; oil, electricity |
| **labour** | people's time and skill | the scarcest input in most pre-industrial economies outside famines; its organisation (kin, serfdom, slavery, wages) is much of the social order | all eras |
| **land** | fixed in place, the source of most energy and materials before coal | its ownership is the main form of wealth and power until the 19th century | foraging territories; fields, woods, mills' sites; plantations; urban land (today the largest store of household wealth in rich countries [M]) |

### 1.3 The properties that matter more than the category

A good's category says what kind of institution grows around it. Its physical and economic properties say how. These are the
levers a world designer can actually set.

| Property | Low end | High end | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| **perishability** | grain (keeps a year or more dry), salt, metal | fresh fish, milk, meat in summer | whether a glut becomes wealth or waste; makes preservation (salt, smoke, drying, cold) an industry; local versus far trade |
| **value per kilogram** | firewood, stone, lime, grain | spices, silk, silver, furs | how far it can be carried before transport eats its value; grain by land rarely went more than a few tens of km, firewood about 18 to 20 km around London c.1300 [V], spices across Eurasia |
| **bound to place** | grain (grows widely) | salt springs, ore, limestone, clay, a mill site, a ford | dependence between villages; chokepoints; trade routes |
| **joint production** | a crop of beans | a slaughtered ox (meat, hide, tallow, bone, horn, offal); an oak (timber, bark, firewood); a coppice; a sheep (wool, meat, milk, dung) | whether the by-products decide if the act is worth doing; markets for each part |
| **skill needed** | gathering, carrying | smelting, tanning, brewing, glass, navigation | specialists, apprenticeship, secrets, guilds; losing a master loses a rung |
| **divisibility** | a house, a ship, a mill | grain, coin, cloth by the ell | who can buy it; whether shares, credit or rent are needed |
| **rivalry and excludability** | the market's peace (neither) | a loaf (both) | which of the institutional kinds in §1.2 it falls into |
| **time to produce** | a day's foraging | an oak for building (80 to 150 years [M]), a coppice cycle (7 to 20 years [V]), a skilled smith (years) | patience, rights over the future, inheritance |

## 2. Land: what it gives, and how its uses compete

### 2.1 The organic economy's land budget in outline

Every hectare gives either food, fodder, fuel, timber or fibre, or some mix of them over time. The yields are of the same order
whatever the use, because all of them are plant growth: a few tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year in a temperate climate.

| Use | Typical output per hectare per year | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| wheat, England c.1300 | about 0.5 to 0.8 t gross, of which a quarter to a third went back as seed | [C] from Ebony priory: 11.7 to 13 bushels an acre gross from 4 bushels of seed [V]; a bushel of wheat about 27 kg [M]; 1 ha = 2.47 acres |
| wheat, England c.1850 | about 1.7 to 2 t | [M] (roughly 28 bushels an acre), consistent with Overton's +25% in 1700 to 1800 and +50% in 1800 to 1850 [V] |
| potatoes | about 2.5 to 3 times the calories of wheat on the same land | [V] Overton (early 19th-c. yields); David Henry (1771) |
| broadleaf coppice | about 2.5 oven-dry tonnes of wood (about 45 GJ) | [V] mean annual increment; [C] at about 18 MJ per oven-dry kg [M] |
| hazel coppice cut at 10 years | about 25 t/ha a cut; 25,000 to 30,000 rods of 3 to 4.5 m at 8 to 9 years | [V] |
| hay meadow | enough hay for a horse's year from 1.5 to 2 acres (0.6 to 0.8 ha) | [V] a 19th-c. English text (date unclear) |

Food crops win on calories per hectare. Wood wins on storable heat with no tillage. Pasture wins where the soil is too wet, steep
or poor to plough. A person's needs added up across all of them is what makes land the binding limit (§2.8).

to 1430 [V].

**The seed ratio matters more than the yield.** At a ratio of 4, a quarter of the harvest is next year's seed; at 3, a third. A
bad year that halves the harvest at a ratio of 4 leaves only a quarter of a normal harvest to eat, after seed is set aside. This
is why medieval famines were so sharp (1315 to 1317), and why a household that eats its seed corn has given up its future. The
engine's plot already sits at this ratio: 64 grain from 16 seed, 4:1 (capacity.py, [[D4-38 The farming stages' work and returns are 02l §2's|D4-38]]).

### 2.3 Calories per acre by crop

| Crop | Relative food per acre | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| wheat | baseline (1) | the prestige grain; bread; needs good soil and manure |
| rye | close to wheat on poor soils [M] | the grain of sandy northern Europe; ergot risk |
| barley | similar to wheat [M] | ale and malt; pig and horse feed |
| oats | lower per acre in food value, but grows on poor wet land [M] | horse fodder; porridge in the north and west |
| beans and peas | lower calories, more protein; fix nitrogen [M] | part of three-course rotations; fodder; fertility |
| potatoes | 2.5 to 3 times wheat [V] | let Ireland and parts of northern Europe double their population in the 18th and early 19th centuries; one variety, one blight (1845) |
| maize (in Europe from the 16th c.) | high [M] | the Po valley, the Balkans; pellagra where eaten alone |
| grain versus fresh tubers, by weight | grain carries about five times the energy per kg of fresh tubers [V] (Smil) | grain stores and travels; this is part of why grain, not tubers, fed tax-collecting states |

A rough rule: a hectare of grain feeds several people; the same hectare as pasture for meat and milk feeds a fraction of one [M].
Diets shift towards meat when land is plentiful (after the Black Death) and towards grain and potatoes when land is short.

### 2.4 Fodder for draught animals

Draught animals multiplied a person's work several times (§3.5), but they ate land.

- **Oxen against horses.** On English demesnes oxen still outnumbered horses about two to one at the end of the 14th century;
  peasants took to the horse faster [V] (Langdon). The ox is cheaper: it eats hay and straw rather than oats, works on coarse
  fodder, and is eaten at the end of its working life. The horse is about half again as fast, better on light soils and the road,
  but needs grain [M].
- **A hard-worked 19th-century horse** was fed 12 to 16 lb of grain (oats and beans) and 12 lb of hay a day [V] (an 1856 guide).
  [C] That is about 2.0 to 2.7 tonnes of grain and 2 tonnes of hay a year. At oat yields of perhaps 1.5 to 2 t/ha [M], this is
  1 to 1.8 ha of oats plus 0.6 to 0.8 ha of hay [V]: **roughly 2 to 3 ha (5 to 7 acres) per working horse.** A light-working farm
  horse needed less. These figures are rough and rest on an [M] yield.
- **England's horses in the early 19th century** ate the produce of a large share of the farmland; estimates of the order of a
  fifth or more are common [M]. This is the hidden land cost of horse transport, and why the railway and then the tractor freed
  so much land.

### 2.5 Fuel per household

| Case | Figure | Note |
|---|---|---|

| London per head c.1300 | about 1.4 to 2.3 tonnes of wood a year | [C] from 141,000 tons and a population of 60,000 to 100,000 [M, contested]; includes brewing, baking and crafts |
| firewood carted by land around London | viable up to about 18 to 20 km in the early 14th century; less after the Black Death as wages and carriage rose | [V] |
| a working assumption used by historians | 1.5 m³ of firewood a person a year, and 3 m³ a year from a hectare of coppice: about half a hectare of coppice per person | [V] from a paper citing Galloway et al.; secondary |
| a cottager's family in a cool climate | a quarter to half an acre of 20-year coppice cut a year (6 to 12 cords) | [V] secondary, weakly sourced; [C] this means 5 to 10 acres held in a 20-year rotation |
| England and Wales 1561 to 1570 | firewood 21,490 TJ a year for 3.04 million people: about 7 GJ, or roughly 0.5 t, a head | [V] Wrigley's table; [C] per head |

The two estimates disagree by a factor of three or four. Both may be right: London burned fuel for crafts and bought it at a
price, while much of rural England by the 1560s was short of wood and burned straw, dung, furze and peat that national accounts
count poorly. **Read household fuel as 0.5 to 2 tonnes of wood a person a year, depending on climate, price and what else is
burned.**

[C] For London c.1300, 141,000 tons at about 3 tonnes of air-dry wood per hectare a year means some 45,000 to 55,000 ha (110,000 to
140,000 acres) of coppice under rotation, all within carting or boating distance. That is the size of the ring a medieval city of
under 100,000 needed for heat alone.

### 2.6 Timber for a house and a ship

| Building | Trees | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Grundle House, Stanton (Suffolk), a late-medieval timber-framed house | about 330 trees | [V] Rackham (1986), reconstructed from the frame |
| Mill Farm, Mapledurham | about 111 trees | [V] Miles (2014) |
| medieval building timbers | over 90% locally grown oak | [V] |
| a 74-gun ship of the line, late 18th century | of the order of 2,000 to 3,500 oaks [M] | contested; the navy's demand for "compass timber" (curved oak) shaped forest policy from the 17th century |

Rackham's point was that most of these trees were small: a medieval frame used many young oaks, squared whole, not a few giants.
Building timber came from standards grown among coppice, on 50 to 100-year cycles [M]. A house is therefore a claim on the
woods for decades, and a ship on a whole forest.

### 2.7 Industrial fuel: iron, salt, glass, lime

These were the trades that ran into the land ceiling first.

**Iron.**
- **Bloomery (to the late Middle Ages):** replica smelts take 10 to 20 kg of charcoal per kg of bloom; the best runs about 3.2 lb
  per lb of bloom; refining loses 25 to 40% of the bloom [V]. [C] So about 13 to 33 kg of charcoal per kg of bar iron, or roughly
  65 to 220 kg of wood per kg of bar iron at 15 to 20% charcoal yield. These are experimental figures, not medieval measurements.
- **Charcoal blast furnace and finery (16th to 18th centuries):** about 3 "loads" of charcoal per ton of bar iron (Hammersley
  1973) [V]; the load's weight varies by district.
- **The land a works needed:** a Wealden furnace and forge in the mid-17th century drew on 4,000 to 5,000 acres of coppice; the
  Forest of Dean reckoned 13,000 acres for the indefinite running of a furnace and forge; Backbarrow (Cumbria) needed about
  1,000 acres (1.5 square miles) of coppice cut each year [V].
- **Charcoal itself:** traditional earth kilns convert 10 to 21% of the wood's weight into charcoal, depending on the collier's
  skill more than the kiln's design [V]. [C] Air-dry wood holds about 15 MJ/kg and charcoal about 29 MJ/kg [M], so at 20% yield
  some 60% of the wood's energy is lost. Charcoal pays only where its heat, purity and lightness matter: smelting, smithing, and
  fuel carried far.

**Salt.**
- Droitwich's brine was nearly saturated: four tons of brine gave one ton of salt (17th-century Royal Society report) [V].
  Seawater, at about 3.5% salt [M], needs about seven times the brine, and about nine times the water boiled off, for the same salt [C], which is why coastal
  salt was made by the sun in pans, or by washing salt-rich sand ("sleeching") to make a stronger brine first.
- Leland (1530s) was told that Droitwich burned about 6,000 loads of wood a year; the manor of Martin Hussingtree owed 100
  cartloads of wood a year in return for a set quantity of salt; fuel was brought from Worcester, Bromsgrove and Alcester [V].
  The salt works was a chokepoint (salt) bound to a sink (woods) at a distance, tied by rent in kind.

**Glass.**
- Theophilus (c.1100): two parts beech ash to one part quartz. Beech gives about 1% ash, so ash alone takes about 63 kg of wood
  per kg of glass; with the furnace fuel, 150 to 200 kg [V].
- The Spiegelberg glassworks (1705 to 1822) needed about 800 centners of potash a year: about 40,000 m³ of wood [V]. [C] At 3 m³
  per hectare a year, that is some 13,000 ha of woodland for one works. Forest glass moved with the forest; English glass moved
  to coal after the 1615 ban on wood for glass furnaces [M].

**Lime.** Quicklime is burned from limestone or chalk at about 900°C; a kiln takes something of the order of a tonne of wood, or
less of coal, per tonne of lime [M, rough]. Lime went into mortar, plaster, tanning, soap and fields, so lime kilns sat where
stone and fuel met.

Campbell's 10.7 to 12 million acres of arable for 4.5 to 5.5 million people gives about 2 to 2.6 acres (0.8 to 1 ha) of arable a

organic economy needed of the order of **1.5 to 3 hectares of land of all kinds**. A forager, by comparison, needed of the order of
1 to 100 km² (100 to 10,000 ha) a person, at densities of 0.01 to 1 a km² [M] (Kelly). Farming fed perhaps a hundred times more
people per hectare than foraging, at the cost of more work for each calorie, at least at first [V] (Smil; Kraft et al. find
horticulture's return rates per hour higher than foraging's, so the evidence is mixed).

### 2.9 How the uses compete for the same ground

| Conflict | How it showed | How societies answered |
|---|---|---|
| **arable against pasture** | ploughing up the common removes grazing; fewer beasts mean less manure, so yields fall (the medieval "Postan" problem) | stints on the common; fold courses; convertible husbandry; later turnips and clover that are both fodder and fertility |
| **food against fodder** | oats for horses take land from bread | oxen on coarse fodder; horses only where speed paid; in the 20th century the tractor released the horses' land |
| **food against fuel** | woods ploughed into fields; fuel prices rise | coppice rotation; hedgerow wood; straw, dung and furze; peat; then coal |
| **fuel against timber** | coppice cut too often never makes standards; charcoal burners and shipbuilders want the same oaks | coppice-with-standards; forest laws; navy reserves; 1543 Act for the Preservation of Woods [M] |
| **fuel for the house against fuel for industry** | iron, salt, glass and lime works bid up the woods | works moved to the woods (Weald, Forest of Dean, Furness) or to coal; bans on wood in glass furnaces |
| **fibre against food** | flax and hemp need good land and manure, and foul water when retted | grown in small plots, or imported (Baltic hemp and flax) |
| **wool against grain** | enclosure for sheep in 15th- and 16th-century England ("sheep eat men") | statutes against depopulation; in practice, the market won where wool paid |
| **the river** | mills hold water, fish need runs, tanning and retting foul it | mill rights, weir laws, fixed fishing times, ordinances on where tanners may work |

**The historical way out was to import land.** A society short of ground bought goods that embodied other people's land: Baltic
grain, timber, hemp and tar for the Dutch and English; sugar and later cotton from slave plantations; Kenneth Pomeranz calls
these "ghost acres" [M]. The other way out was to mine stored land (peat, then coal, §3.7).

### 3.2 Foragers

Foragers run on human muscle and fire. Fire cooks food (which raises the energy a gut can take from it), warms, lights, drives
game and clears land. Energy per head is perhaps 5 to 10 GJ a year, food and firewood together [M] (Smil). Human foragers and
horticulturalists spend more energy on getting food each day than other great apes, and get more energy back per hour [V]
(Kraft et al. 2021). The ceiling is set not by land in a field but by the wild yield of a territory: game, roots, nuts, fish. The
density that follows (§2.8) is the root of the forager's other features: mobility, sharing, little stored wealth.

### 3.3 A pre-industrial household and village

[C] A household of five in lowland England c.1300, with a share of an ox team:

| Flow | Per year | Energy |
|---|---|---|
| food for five people (average 2,000 kcal a day) | about 0.9 to 1.1 t of grain-equivalent | about 15 GJ |
| fuel (1 to 2 t of wood a head in a cool climate, less if straw and dung are burned) | 2.5 to 10 t of wood | about 40 to 150 GJ gross; much less useful heat, since an open hearth delivers perhaps 10 to 20% of it to the room [M] |
| a share in two oxen | hay, straw, grazing | the oxen eat more food energy than the people [M] |
| mill power | grinding of 1 t of grain | a few hours of a mill's time |

The striking fact is that fuel, not food, is the largest energy flow in a household in a cool climate, and draught animals eat
more than people. A village of 50 households at this rate needs of the order of 40 to 170 ha of coppice or equivalent fuel
ground under rotation [C], besides its fields and grazing.

Wrigley's table, as reproduced in his VoxEU column (terajoules a year, England and Wales) [V]:

| Decade | Human | Draught animals | Firewood | Wind | Water | Coal | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1561 to 1570 | 14,860 | 21,100 | 21,490 | 200 | 550 | 6,930 | 65,130 |
| 1650 to 1659 | | | | | | | 116,820 |
| 1850 to 1859 | | | | | | | 1,835,300 |

- **[C] Per head in the 1560s:** about 21 GJ (3.04 million people), of which people's own food was 4.9 GJ, draught animals 6.9,
  firewood 7.1, wind and water together 0.25, coal 2.3. Muscle (people and animals) was over half the total, as Wrigley says [V].
- **Coal's rise:** half of all energy by the start of the 18th century; well over 90% by the mid-19th; coal per head roughly
  doubled every half-century for three centuries [V].
- **[C] Per head in the 1850s:** about 100 to 110 GJ, five times the 1560s, depending on the population base used.
- **Useful energy** (counting the efficiency of each use) rose from about 1 MWh (3.6 GJ) a person a year in the 1600s to over 3 MWh
  by the mid-19th century [V] (Thunder Said Energy's estimate; one secondary analysis).
- For comparison, the Netherlands used about 14 GJ a head around 1800 [V] (CBS); pre-industrial southern Europe was of the order of
  10 to 20 GJ [M] (Malanima).

### 3.5 The ceiling (Wrigley's organic economy)

Wrigley's argument [V]: in an organic economy, nearly all energy and most raw materials come from plants grown each year, and
plants capture only a small fraction of sunlight. Any growth in one use of land (more iron, more horses, more people) takes land
from another. Productivity can rise (better rotations, more mills, more trade), but each gain runs into the fixed area of land, so
growth is bounded, as the classical economists from Smith to Ricardo and Malthus expected. Coal broke this because it is stored
plant growth from millions of years, mined at a rate set by labour and capital rather than by acres.

The size of the gap: Wrigley reckoned that 2 million acres of woodland were needed to give each year the heat of 1 million tons of
coal [V]. How much land England's coal replaced in 1800 is contested: one secondary account says about a third of the land
surface; another gives 15 million acres for Britain [V]. [C] At 15 million tons of coal [M] and Wrigley's ratio, it would be
about 30 million acres, close to the whole area of England [M]. The spread comes from different assumptions about coal output and
woodland yield. On any estimate, coal gave 19th-century Britain a second country's worth of land.

### 3.6 Prime movers and mills

Power ratings are typical sustained useful outputs [M] (Smil, Langdon, Reynolds); individual machines varied widely.

| Prime mover | Sustained useful power | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| a person | about 50 to 100 W | everything; at the quern, a person grinds a few kg of grain an hour [M] |
| an ox | about 250 to 400 W | ploughing, carting heavy loads slowly; a team of 4 to 8 for heavy clay |
| a horse (with a collar harness) | about 500 to 750 W | ploughing, harrowing, carting, coaching; horse gins for pumps and mills |
| a medieval watermill | about 1.5 to 4 kW | grinding grain; then fulling cloth, forge hammers and bellows, sawing, crushing ore, oil, tan bark, paper |
| a post windmill | about 1.5 to 4 kW, when the wind blows | grinding; in the Netherlands, drainage, sawing, oil and paper |
| a large 18th-century water wheel | about 10 to 40 kW | textile mills, ironworks |
| a Newcomen engine (1712) | about 4 kW | pumping mines, at a large coal cost |
| a Watt engine (from 1776) | about 10 to 20 kW and more | pumping, then rotary power for mills |

Mill counts [V]:
- **Domesday (1086):** about 6,000 mills recorded (estimates 5,624 to 6,500), all water or animal powered; likely a count of pairs of
  stones rather than buildings; none recorded in Lancashire, Cumbria, Northumberland or Durham.
- **By the end of the 12th century:** some 56 known windmills in England, the earliest before 1137.
- **c.1300:** Langdon's best guess is about 10,000 mills in England, wind and water together, grinding most of the grain and doing
  industrial work; the number fell after 1348 and windmills were often given up for watermills.
- **The Netherlands:** the Zaan district had 600 to 1,000 windmills at its peak, over 200 of them sawmills, after Cornelis
  Corneliszoon's wind sawmill of 1592; by about 1920 some 20 were left. One source claims that as late as 1850 wind gave 90% of the
  power used in Dutch industry (weakly sourced).

[C] 6,000 mills for a population of perhaps 1.5 to 2 million [M] in 1086 is roughly one pair of stones per 50 to 60 households.
The mill was the first capital good that almost every village either owned, shared or paid a lord to use (multure, often a
sixteenth to a twentieth of the grain [M]), and the lord's monopoly on it (suit of mill) was one of the main points of friction
between lords and tenants.

**What water and wind did not do:** they gave mechanical power at a fixed site and could not be carried. They did not give heat,
which is most of what a pre-industrial society needs. Wind and water together were well under 1% of England's energy in the 1560s
(750 TJ of 65,130) [C from V].

### 3.7 The fuel ladder: wood → charcoal → peat → coal

| Fuel | Where and when it led | What it needs | What it is good and bad for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **dead wood, brushwood** | everywhere, all eras | labour, a right to gather | gathered by anyone; bulky; low heat per load |
| **firewood from coppice** | organised woodland, from the Neolithic in places; the medieval norm | rights over years; restraint | renewable; heavy to carry beyond 20 km by land [V] |
| **straw, dung, furze, heath** | where wood is short: open-field villages, steppe, uplands | a by-product of farming | burns fast; dung burned is manure lost to the fields |
| **charcoal** | metalworking from the Bronze Age; glass, salt, smithing | a collier's skill; 5 to 10 tonnes of wood a tonne [C from V]; time | high, clean heat; light to carry far; wastes 60% of the wood's energy [C] |
| **peat** | the Netherlands in the 16th and 17th centuries; Ireland, Scotland, Frisia, the Fens | bogs near water, cut in spring and dried in summer; canals for transport | stored land: many years of growth in each cut; the Dutch Golden Age ran on it [V] (de Zeeuw's thesis, contested as a sufficient cause); output fell about 20% in the 1660s and Dutch energy per head fell 15 to 20% by 1750 as cheap peat ran out [V, weakly sourced] |
| **sea coal** | London from the 13th century for lime-burning and smithing; household fuel from the later 16th century; half of England's energy by 1700 [V] | outcrops near water (Tyne to London by sea); pumps for deeper pits; ships | dense, abundant, dirty; needed new grates, chimneys and processes (glass, brewing, salt, brick) before it could replace wood; iron needed coke (Darby, 1709 [M]) and puddling (Cort, 1784 [M]) |

**When the better rung pays:** charcoal pays where heat must be intense and pure, or the fuel carried far. Peat pays where wood is
gone and bogs lie by water. Coal pays where wood is dear, coal lies near water, and the user can change their process to take its
fumes. The transition to coal was driven more by process heat (lime, salt, glass, brewing, smithing, then iron) and household
hearths than by steam engines [V] (Jones, softmachines.org, summarising this literature).

### 3.8 Past coal, briefly

| Carrier | Industrial start | What it changed in goods | Where it stands now |
|---|---|---|---|
| **town gas (from coal)** | London's Gas Light and Coke Company, 1812 [M] | street and house lighting; cooking; coal tar, which became dyes and drugs | displaced by natural gas and electricity |
| **oil** | Titusville well, 1859 [M]; kerosene for lamps first, then petrol and diesel for engines | ended whale oil; made the car, the tractor, the aeroplane; feedstock for plastics, synthetic fibres, solvents | about 199 EJ in 2024, the largest single source [V] |
| **natural gas** | town and industrial use in the late 19th century; pipelines after 1920s to 1960s [M] | heating, power, and above all ammonia for fertiliser by the Haber-Bosch process (1913 [M]) | about 149 EJ in 2024 [V] |
| **electricity** | central stations from 1882 (Edison's Pearl Street, Holborn Viaduct) [M] | a carrier, not a source: it moved power from a site to every machine and lamp; made aluminium, the electric motor in every workshop, refrigeration, telecommunications, computing | about 30,000 TWh a year generated [M] |
| **nuclear** | Obninsk 1954, Calder Hall 1956 [M] | very dense, place-bound power; needs a state | about 31 EJ in 2024 [V] |
| **hydro, wind, solar** | hydro from the 1880s; modern wind and solar at scale from the 2000s [M] | electricity without fuel; the return of place-bound power (sunny and windy sites, rivers) | renewables excluding hydro about 33 EJ in 2024 [V]; renewables in all about 14.6% of consumption in 2023 [V] |

World primary energy in 2023 was 620 EJ, of which fossil fuels were 81.5% [V]. [C] At about 8 billion people, that is about 77 GJ
a head; the world average is often quoted as 74 GJ, with a range from about 4 GJ in Afghanistan to over 700 GJ in Iceland [V].
Smil finds that gains in health and education from energy flatten above 50 to 70 GJ a head; the United States at about 300 GJ
shows no clear gain over Japan at about 150 [V].

### 3.9 Energy per head across eras

| Society | Energy per head a year | Mostly from |
|---|---|---|
| foragers | about 5 to 10 GJ [M] | food, firewood |
| medieval and early modern farming societies | about 10 to 25 GJ [M]; England 1560s about 21 GJ [C from V]; Netherlands c.1800 about 14 GJ [V] | food, fodder, firewood; a little wind and water |
| England 1850s | about 100 GJ [C from V] | over 90% coal [V] |
| world 2023 | about 77 GJ [C from V] | oil, coal, gas |
| rich countries today | about 150 to 300 GJ [V] | oil, gas, electricity |

The jump from 20 to 100 GJ was the industrial revolution. Most of it went into making things and moving them (iron, steam,
railways), not into the home.

## 4. Substitution ladders and trade-offs, by need

Each need has several ways to meet it. A better rung costs inputs, capital or skill, and pays only above some scale or price.
Rungs often coexist: the hand quern went on beside the mill, often illegally, for centuries. "Pays when" is the condition under
which people historically moved up.

### 4.1 Food (getting it)

| Rung | Inputs | Pays when | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| foraging, hunting | labour, skill, territory | land is plentiful per person | high return per hour, low per hectare |
| hoe horticulture, slash and burn | labour, axes, fire, long fallow | forest regrows faster than people grow | Boserup's first stage |
| ox plough, two- and three-field rotation | oxen, plough (iron share), meadow for hay | people are numerous enough to need more ground worked | the medieval norm |
| convertible husbandry, legumes, turnips and clover | seed, labour, livestock housing | land is scarce and markets pay for meat and grain | Norfolk four-course; nitrogen from clover |
| new crops (potato, maize) | the plant itself, a little skill | land is very scarce; the poor need calories | 2.5 to 3 times wheat's calories an acre [V]; brittle (blight) |
| mineral fertiliser, then machines | guano, nitrate, Haber-Bosch ammonia; tractors and diesel | fossil energy is cheap | yields of 8 t/ha of wheat today in north-west Europe [M] |

### 4.2 Heat and cooking

| Rung | Inputs | Pays when |
|---|---|---|
| open hearth, wood | wood, a roof hole | always; lowest capital |
| hearth with straw, dung, furze | farm by-products | wood is short; costs fertility |
| chimney and enclosed fireplace | brick or stone, skill | houses get upper floors; fuel is dear (spread in England in the 16th century [M]) |
| peat in a hearth | bogs and canals | wood gone, bogs near |
| coal grate | coal, iron grate, tall chimney | coal is cheap by water (London from the late 16th century [V]) |
| iron range, then gas and electric cookers | iron castings, gas mains, electricity | urban networks exist |

### 4.3 Light

| Rung | Inputs | Pays when |
|---|---|---|
| the hearth's glow | none extra | always |
| rushlight (pith dipped in tallow) | rushes, kitchen fat | any household with fat |
| tallow candle | tallow from cattle and sheep, wick of cotton or linen | a chandler's trade in towns; smoky |
| beeswax candle | bees, wax | the Church and the rich |
| oil lamp | olive oil (south), rapeseed and linseed (north), whale oil (17th to 19th c.) | oil cheaper than tallow; whaling as an industry |
| gas light | coal gas, mains | 1810s on, in towns [M] |
| kerosene lamp | crude oil, refineries | 1860s on [M]; ended whaling's lamp market |
| electric light | power stations, wires, bulbs | 1880s on [M] |

Nordhaus's study of the price of light found the cost of a unit of light fell by a factor of the order of thousands to hundreds of
thousands between the Middle Ages and the late 20th century [M]. The evening was, for most of history, rationed by fat.

Iron's value to a farming economy was as an edge on wood: the ploughshare, the axe, the scythe, the sickle. A medieval plough was
mostly wood with a little iron [M]; the engine's wooden plough (4 timber, 1 copper) has the same shape.

## 5. Sources, sinks and intermediates

### 5.1 The intermediates that held the organic economy together

| Intermediate | Inputs | Main uses | Bound to | Its later substitute |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **firewood** | coppice, hedges, dead wood | every hearth; baking, brewing, dyeing | woods within 20 km by land | coal, gas, electricity |
| **charcoal** | wood, a collier's skill, time | smelting, smithing, glass, salt, gunpowder | woods; carried far | coke (1709 on [M]) |
| **potash (wood ash, lye)** | wood ash, leached | glass, soap, bleaching, dyeing | woods; later Baltic and North American forests | Leblanc soda (1790s), Solvay soda (1860s) [M], from salt and coal |
| **cord and rope** | bast, flax, hemp, wool, withies | nets, snares, lines, well ropes, ships' rigging, lashing | flax and hemp land; retting water | wire rope, synthetic rope |
| **cloth and yarn** | wool, flax, hemp; spinning, weaving, fulling (water mills from the 12th c. [M]), dyeing | clothing, sails, sacks, bedding | sheep country, water for fulling, dyes (woad, madder, kermes) | mill-spun cotton, synthetics |
| **leather** | hides from herds and game; oak bark; lime; time (many months in pits [M]) | harness, shoes, bellows, buckets, bags, saddles, belts for machines | herds; old oak woods for bark; water | rubber, synthetics |
| **worked wood** (boards, staves, poles, withies) | timber, a saw pit or saw mill, cleaving skill | building, barrels, carts, ploughs, boats, furniture, tools' handles | woods, standards | sawn softwood imports, then steel and plastics |

| **salt** | brine springs, seawater pans, rock salt; fuel | preserving fish and meat, cheese, butter, tanning, glazing, diet | a few sites; salt roads | salt remains, but refrigeration displaced its main use; it became the feedstock of soda and chlorine |
| **fats** (tallow, lard, oil, wax) | cattle, sheep, pigs, olives, rape, linseed, whales, bees | light, soap, grease for axles and machines, cooking, dressing leather | herds, oil crops, whaling grounds | kerosene, electricity, petroleum lubricants, palm oil |
| **malt** | barley, water, a floor and kiln, fuel | ale and beer (a staple drink and much of a household's calories [M]), vinegar, distilling | barley land, water | industrial brewing, hops (from the 15th c. in England [M]) for keeping beer |
| **bar iron** | ore, charcoal, a furnace and forge, water power | every edge, nail, hoop, horseshoe, tyre, hinge | ore and woods together | coke iron, steel |
| **saltpetre** | nitre beds of dung and urine, earth scraped from stables; later Indian imports | gunpowder, preserving meat | state monopolies (saltpetre men) | Chilean nitrate, then synthetic ammonia |

### 5.2 How the chains formed and merged: traced cases

**Salt.** Foragers took salt from springs and from meat. Settled farmers needed it for cattle and for keeping food. Iron Age
Droitwich boiled brine in clay pots ("briquetage") [M]; medieval Droitwich boiled it in lead pans over wood, with woods in other
manors owing carts of fuel in return for salt [V]. Lüneburg, Halle and Salzburg grew on salt; the Hanse moved Lüneburg salt to
pack Baltic herring [M]. Atlantic sea salt from the Bay of Bourgneuf, made by the sun, undersold boiled salt for fish-curing [M].
From the 17th century, rock salt in Cheshire and coal-fired pans joined the salt chain to the coal chain [M]. In the 19th century,
salt became the feedstock of the alkali industry (Leblanc, then Solvay soda) and chlorine, merging the salt chain with the chains
for glass, soap, textiles and bleach that had run on wood ash [M]. Refrigeration then took away its oldest use.

**Iron.** Bloomeries in many villages used bog ore and local charcoal. From the 14th to 16th centuries, the water-powered blast
furnace and finery forge concentrated iron in woodland districts with streams: the Weald, the Forest of Dean, Furness [V for the
acreages]. Iron was bound to charcoal and so to coppice; ironmasters bought coppice rotations for decades. Coke smelting (1709 [M])
and puddling (1784 [M]) cut iron loose from the woods and bound it to coal fields. Steel (Bessemer 1856 [M]) and then electric arc
furnaces moved it again, to ore ports and cheap electricity.

**Light.** Tallow from the herds, wax from the bees, olive oil in the south. Whale oil joined light to a global hunt from the 17th
century [M]. Coal gas (1810s [M]) joined light to the coal chain and to city pipes. Kerosene (1860s [M]) joined it to oil and
ended the whale-oil market. Electric light (1880s [M]) joined it to power stations, which by then also drove motors and trams. Four
separate chains (herds, bees, whales, coal) collapsed into one network.

**Cloth.** Wool from sheep, spun on distaffs and wheels, woven at home, fulled in water mills (12th century on [M]), dyed with
plants from far away. English wool fed Flemish and Italian looms through the 13th and 14th centuries; then England wove its own
cloth for export [M]. Putting-out tied rural households to merchants (02zzi §3.6). Cotton from the Indian Ocean trade and then
American slave plantations, spun by water frame and mule from the 1770s, merged cloth with water power, then steam and coal [M].
Synthetic fibres (20th century) merged it with oil.

**Alkali (soap, glass, bleach).** Wood ash and potash from forests; kelp from Scottish and Irish shores; barilla from Spanish
saltwort [M]. Glassworks and soap boilers competed with hearths and ironworks for wood. The Leblanc process (1790s) made soda from
salt, sulphuric acid and coal; Solvay (1860s) from salt, limestone and ammonia [M]. Three land-based chains became one chemical
chain fed by salt, coal and stone, and the old ash trades died.

**Fertility.** Dung from beasts and night soil from towns, so fertility was tied to fodder and therefore to land. Legumes and
clover (17th and 18th centuries) made nitrogen on the farm. Peruvian guano and Chilean nitrate (1840s to 1910s) joined fertility to
ocean shipping and mining [M]. The Haber-Bosch process (1913 [M]) joined it to coal and then natural gas: food production now runs
on fossil energy, and the same chemistry makes explosives, which merged the fertiliser chain with the gunpowder (saltpetre) chain.

**Containers.** Baskets, skins and pots; barrels for two thousand years; glass bottles from coal-fired furnaces in 17th-century
England [M]; tin cans from 1810; paper and cardboard from wood pulp; plastics from oil. Each step joined containers to a bigger
chain (cooperage to woods, glass to coal, cans to tin mining and steel, plastics to oil).

**The pattern:** organic chains were many, separate and bound to different covers of land. Each industrial substitute joined
several of them to one mineral source (coal, salt, oil, gas) and one network (pipes, wires, rails). That is why industrial
economies are both more productive and more exposed to a single shock (an oil embargo, a gas cut-off).

### 5.3 Sinks

What absorbs surplus in the organic economy:
- **building:** houses, barns, churches, castles; the largest store of labour and timber;
- **beasts:** herds as walking capital, which grow and can be eaten in a famine;
- **stores:** grain in barns and granaries; salt meat and fish; cheese; wine and ale;
- **land improvement:** marling, hedging, draining, terracing, breaking new ground;
- **status consumption:** feasts, fine cloth, plate, horses, retinues;
- **the Church:** buildings, endowments, masses;
- **war:** the largest single sink of states' surplus from antiquity to the 20th century.

Industrial sinks add machines, infrastructure (rails, canals, pipes, grids) and financial claims.

## 6. What a simulator could take from this

No cost limits assumed here. Ordered from what matters most.

### 6.1 Model as first-class constraints

1. **A land budget that every use draws on.** Food, fodder, fuel, timber and fibre should all be claims on the same parcels, at
   yields of the same order (a few tonnes of dry matter per hectare a year), so that more horses, more ironworks or more people
   take land from something else. This is the organic economy's defining limit and the reason most of its institutions exist
   (commons, stints, forest law, enclosure). The engine has covers and flows; what it lacks is fodder for draught beasts and fibre
   crops competing with grain, and industrial fuel demand competing with hearths.
2. **Seed ratio and storage.** Keep the 4:1 ratio (it is right for 1300). Raising it should be the agricultural revolution: through
   rotations, legumes, manure, lime and marl, not through a tool. A famine should be able to make households eat their seed.
3. **Fuel as the largest household energy flow in a cool climate,** and the woods' regrowth as its limit. Firewood within carrying
   distance (the 20 km limit around London c.1300 is a good test), coppice rotations as time capital, and the fall-back fuels
   (straw, dung, furze, peat) with their costs (manure lost, labour in summer).
4. **Draught animals that eat land.** An ox team or a horse should cost hay, grazing and, for the horse, oats; the choice between
   them is a real trade-off (cheap and slow against fast and grain-hungry).
5. **Industrial fuel demand.** Iron, salt, glass and lime should each burn many times their weight in wood: charcoal at 5 to 10 t
   of wood a tonne, iron at tens of kg of charcoal per kg of bar in a bloomery, glass at 150 to 200 kg of wood per kg. This puts
   works in the woods and makes ironmasters and salt-boilers rivals of villages for the coppice.
6. **Place-bound chokepoints:** salt (brine springs, saltmarsh), ore, limestone, clay, mill sites, fords, bogs. Each should be
   rare and far from the others, so that villages need each other.
7. **The intermediates of §5.1,** at least: charcoal, potash or lye, cord, cloth, leather, boards and staves, barrels, lime, salt,
   fats, malt and bar iron. These are where chains meet and where specialists form. The proposed tranches in 02zzh §7 and
   economy_web.md already include most of them.
8. **Perishability and value per kilogram as the main properties of goods.** These two decide trade distance and the need for
   preservation; the engine has both (spoil, weight). Value per kilogram becomes active once transport has a cost per weight and
   distance.
9. **Mills as the first shared capital,** with the lord's monopoly and multure as the first rent. Water power should be bound to
   specific stretches of river, with fixed capacity, and in conflict with fish runs and tanning.
10. **Energy per head as a metric.** Sum food, fodder, fuel and mill work per person per year, in GJ. An organic village should sit
    near 10 to 25 GJ; anything above that without coal or peat means the world's yields are too generous.

### 6.2 Model when the world grows past the village

- **Peat** as the first "stored land" fuel, with its depletion and the canals it needs (the Dutch case is the model).
- **Coal** as the boundary: it should require changed processes (grates, chimneys, coke) before it substitutes for wood, and it
  should be bound to a few fields near water.
- **Imported land** ("ghost acres"): a region that buys grain, timber or hemp from a frontier is importing land. A multi-region
  world could show this directly.
- **The merges of §5.2:** each industrial substitute should replace several organic chains at once and tie them to one mineral
  source and one network. A simulator that reaches the 19th century should show that dependence and fragility, not only the gain.
- **Past coal:** model oil, gas and electricity mainly as carriers that change where things can be made and what goods exist
  (plastics, fertiliser, aluminium, refrigeration), not as more of the same heat. Electricity in particular should be a network
  good: a public or club good with a natural monopoly.

### 6.3 Safe to aggregate or leave out

- **Individual crop species** beyond a few kinds with different trade-offs: wheat (prestige, demanding), rye or oats (poor land,
  fodder), barley (ale), legumes (fertility, protein), and later potatoes (calories, fragility). More species add little.
- **Minor wild products** with a single use: fold them into "wild food" or into one by-product of an act, as 02zzh §6 already says.
- **Exact prime-mover outputs:** a person, an ox, a horse, a water wheel and a windmill in ratios of roughly 1 : 4 : 8 : 30 : 30
  [C from M ratings] are enough; the precise watts do not change decisions.
- **Chemistry of tanning, glass and dyes:** keep their inputs, times and fuel; leave out the recipes' detail.
- **Grades of quality:** let skill change yield and waste, as the engine does, rather than adding graded goods.
- **Containers as items holding items:** keep them as fittings or as a property of a store (02zzh §6), and model the barrel's
  importance as a needed input for salting, brewing and shipping.
- **Energy conversions** inside a household (the efficiency of a hearth): one number per fuel per use is enough.
