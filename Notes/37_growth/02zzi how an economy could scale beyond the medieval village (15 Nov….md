# 02zzi: how an economy could scale beyond the medieval village (15 Nov 2026)

*note · `docs/review/37_growth/02zzi_economies_scaling.md`*


Zach (15 Nov): "How could you see the economy scaling from medieval to further and more complex societies? ... a deep dive and
analysis into the types of different goods and resources and an economics analysis of different societies ... not as 'this is
what we should build' but this is what we could potentially build to support."

An analysis to think with, not a plan. It draws on economic history in broad strokes (named authors and ideas are standard ones;
numbers are rough orders of magnitude, flagged as such), and ties each part to what the engine has (E), what is proposed in this
week's work (P: economy_web.md, 02zzh, the briefs), and what it would need (N). It complements 02zzh (layers and trees) and
economy_web.md (chains of different shapes).

## 1. Kinds of good, as economists sort them

The economy so far is sorted by material (food, timber, metal). Economics sorts goods by how they behave, and each kind does
something different to a society.

| Kind | What it is | Examples (E, P, N) | What it does to a society |
|---|---|---|---|
| **necessities** | demand barely rises with wealth | grain, fuel, salt (E) | set the floor; famine when they fail |
| **luxuries and status goods** | demand rises faster than wealth; worth carrying far | fine bread, cheese (E), furs (P), wine, spices, fine cloth (N) | drive long-distance trade and specialist crafts; mark rank |
| **durables** | used over years | houses, tools, the plough (E) | savings in material form; inheritance; wear and repair trades |
| **capital goods** | make other goods | the anvil, the mill, the oven (E), tan pits (P), looms (N) | thresholds, owners and users, rent |
| **intermediates** | consumed in making others | charcoal, flour (E), leather, cord, withies (P) | the joints of the economy; specialisation between steps |
| **services** | work done for others, not a thing | milling, carrying, teaching, guarding (E as acts); healing, judging (N) | trades without goods; wages; dependence on persons |
| **common-pool goods** | shared, used up by use | woods, game, fish, pasture (E) | overuse, rules, disputes (the commons) |
| **club goods** | shared by a group who can exclude others | the mill, the oven, the weir (E, B) | membership, fees, quarrels over access |
| **public goods** | shared, not used up, hard to exclude | roads, a bridge, defence, drainage, a market's peace (N) | need taxes, levies or a patron; free riders |
| **money** | a good held to exchange, not to use | coin (E); later credit, notes (N) | lowers the cost of trade across strangers and time |
| **information** | knowledge of prices, methods, people, places | known methods (E), signs (E), records (N) | who knows what decides who can trade, make and govern |
| **energy** | what all work runs on | food (people), fodder (beasts), wood and charcoal (heat), water (the wheel) (E); wind, peat, coal (N) | the hardest limit on any pre-industrial economy |
| **labour** | people's time and skill | units and skill (E) | the scarcest input in every run; its organisation is the society |
| **land** | the source of most energy and materials | parcels, covers (E) | its ownership is the main form of wealth and power until very late |

Two facts follow. **Every economy before coal is an organic economy** (E. A. Wrigley's term): almost all its energy and most of its
materials come from what grows on land, so land sets a ceiling on everything. And **most of a society's structure lies in the
non-material kinds** (services, club and public goods, money, information): what people do for each other, what they share, how
they coordinate. The engine is rich in materials and has the start of the rest (agreements, households, coin, signs).

## 2. What limits every pre-industrial economy

| Limit | What binds | What relaxes it |
|---|---|---|
| **land and energy** | food for people and beasts, fuel, timber, fibre all compete for the same ground | better farming (more per acre), better fuel (charcoal, peat, then coal), trade for land-hungry goods |
| **labour** | each household's hours; skill takes years | specialisation, tools, animal and water power, more people |
| **transport** | moving bulk by land costs many times moving it by water (a commonly cited ratio is ten to one or more) | rivers and coasts, pack beasts, carts and roads, ships; places near water grow first |
| **trust and enforcement** | dealing with strangers needs a way to make promises hold | kin, reputation, witnesses, written contracts, courts, lords, guilds, states |
| **information** | not knowing prices, methods or who has what | markets in a known place, fairs, travelling traders, records, letters |
| **risk** | one bad harvest can starve a village | stores, preservation, diversified crops, trade, credit, charity, lords' granaries |
| **the Malthusian loop** | more food leads to more people, which eats the gain | in the long run, only steady growth in productivity escapes it |

The runs already show four of these binding: labour (every run), transport (Stanmoor's two-day road, Coppice Hill never reached),
risk (the famines) and trust (agreements kept by the engine, not by reputation, 38.68).

## 3. A sequence of societies, with branches

Not a fixed path. Each type below is a different answer to the limits above; societies move between them in both directions, and
there are side branches (§3.9). For each: how it is organised, what it makes and trades, where its surplus goes, what moves it on,
and how it fails.

### 3.1 Foragers (bands)
- **Organised by** small mobile groups; sharing as the rule; no stored wealth to speak of.
- **Goods:** wild food, game, simple tools; gifts.
- **Surplus:** almost none is stored; feasts when a big kill comes in.
- **Moves on when** numbers grow beyond what wild land yields, or storable food (grain, nuts) appears.
- **Engine:** gathering, hunting and sharing work (E); the runs' agents behave nearly like this when farming fails.

### 3.2 Settled farming villages (where the engine is)
- **Organised by** households farming their own land, with kin and neighbours for big jobs; a little exchange; gifts and help.
- **Goods:** grain, beasts, wild food, timber, simple tools; a few things from outside (salt, metal).
- **Surplus:** stored against the lean season; given to kin; spent on building.
- **Moves on when** surplus allows specialists, or someone gains control of land or a chokepoint.
- **Fails by** harvest failure, raids, disease, too many people for the land.
- **Engine:** this is the runs (E).

### 3.3 Chiefdoms and lordship (redistribution)
- **Organised by** a lord, chief or temple who takes a share of the surplus (rent, labour days, tribute) and gives back protection,
  justice, feasts, and big works (the mill, the granary, the road). Karl Polanyi called this **redistribution**, beside
  **reciprocity** (gifts) and **exchange** (markets).
- **Goods:** the same, plus the lord's capital (the mill, the oven, the smithy, the hall) and luxuries for status.
- **Surplus:** flows to the centre and back out unevenly.
- **Moves on when** the lord's need for cash (for war, for luxuries) turns labour dues into money rents, and markets grow.
- **Fails by** over-extraction, war, a lord's death without heir.
- **Engine:** claims, rights, agreements and acting for others (E) can express a lord; the institutions work (37.x) and the
  thresholds of economy_web.md (P: the mill, the weir as club goods) give a lord something to own. **N:** rents in labour and
  kind as standing obligations; a lord's claim on a share of a harvest.

### 3.4 Market towns and regional specialisation
- **Organised by** a market in a known place on a known day, where households from many villages sell surplus and buy what they
  lack; craftsmen settle there; a town grows with walls, a church, a charter.
- **Goods:** villages specialise by their land (wool, grain, timber, salt, ore); towns specialise by trade (smiths, tanners,
  weavers, bakers, brewers). Adam Smith's point: **the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market**, so wider
  markets mean finer specialisation.
- **Surplus:** turned into money and spent at market; invested in shops, tools, stock.
- **Moves on when** roads, rivers and money join markets into regions; merchants appear who buy in one place and sell in another.
- **Fails by** war on the roads, plague, a lord's tolls.
- **Engine:** offers and agreements (E), coin (E), roads (E), carriers (the region run's pack-ox traders). **N:** a market as a
  place and a day where offers meet; prices that people can see and compare (information as a good); a town as a node that grows
  by drawing people and food from its region.

### 3.5 Merchant and commercial economies
- **Organised by** merchants who carry goods between regions; credit across distance and time; partnerships; guilds that control
  entry to trades; written contracts and courts.
- **Goods:** luxuries and bulk staples carried far (cloth, wine, grain by water, salt, metal); money and bills of credit.
- **Key ideas:** **comparative advantage** (David Ricardo): regions gain by each making what it is relatively best at, even if one
  is better at everything; **transaction costs** and **institutions** (Douglass North): trade grows as the cost of making and
  enforcing deals falls.
- **Surplus:** merchant capital, lent and reinvested; buildings; patronage.
- **Moves on when** merchants start organising production, not just trade (§3.6).
- **Fails by** default chains, war, debasement of coin.
- **Engine:** agreements with credit legs (E), ledgers at institutions (E: [[D2-33 Ledgers|D2-33]]). **N:** partnerships and firms as parties;
  enforcement by courts or guilds rather than the engine (OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]]); bills of exchange; reputation that third parties can see.

### 3.6 Proto-industry (putting-out)
- **Organised by** a merchant who gives raw material (wool, flax) to rural households, who spin and weave at home for piece wages
  between farm work, and collects the product. Jan de Vries called the rise of households working harder for the market to buy
  more goods the **industrious revolution**.
- **Goods:** cloth, nails, cutlery, lace, made in homes; bought goods (sugar, tea, pottery) in return.
- **Surplus:** the merchant's working capital; households' new consumption.
- **Moves on when** gathering the work under one roof, with power, pays (the mill, the factory).
- **Engine:** **N:** wage labour as a standing relation (work for another for a wage, not just a day's agreement); piece rates;
  materials owned by one party worked by another; home production competing with farm work for units.

### 3.7 States and fiscal power
- **Organised by** a state that taxes, borrows, keeps an army, makes law and coin, and builds public goods (roads, harbours,
  courts); **the fiscal-military state** of early modern Europe.
- **Goods:** taxes in money; public goods; armies and their supply.
- **Moves on or fails** by its ability to raise revenue without crushing producers.
- **Engine:** institutions acting on each other (37 growth work), claims and enforcement (E, partly). **N:** taxation as a rule
  that takes a share; public goods paid by levies; a currency issued by an institution.

### 3.8 The edge: from organic to mineral energy
- **What changes:** coal (and then steam) replaces wood and muscle as the source of heat and power, breaking the land ceiling of §1.
  Wrigley's argument is that this, more than any single invention, made sustained growth possible.
- **What it needs before it:** dense markets, capital, skills in metal and machines, transport, and a reason (expensive labour,
  cheap fuel: Robert Allen's high-wage explanation for Britain).
- **Engine:** this is past what the engine is for; noted as the boundary. Peat (economy_web.md, L) is the organic economy's own
  step in the same direction: a fuel from stored land, not this year's growth.

### 3.9 Branches the sequence leaves out
- **Pastoral nomads:** wealth in herds, mobility, trade with settled neighbours, raiding as an economic strategy.
- **River-irrigation economies:** a single large public work (canals) and the organisation it demands.
- **Maritime trading cities:** little land, much shipping; wealth from carrying and credit.
- **Frontier and colonial economies:** land plentiful, labour scarce; very different institutions follow.

The engine's frontiers (wild land, the region's three frontiers) could host the last; pastoralism and irrigation would need herds
and water works at a scale it does not have.

## 4. How societies move: the mechanisms

| Mechanism | What it says | In the engine |
|---|---|---|
| **intensification under pressure** (Ester Boserup) | more people per acre push farmers to work land harder: shorter fallows, more manure, more labour per acre | land per household and the capacity ladder (E: [[D4-102 Carrying capacity is measured, by level, from the engine's own rules|D4-102]]) |
| **market growth** (Smithian) | wider markets allow finer specialisation, which raises output | specialists form only when transport and trade let them sell |
| **capital deepening and thresholds** | big works pay only above a scale of use | the mill, the weir, the oven (E, B); economy_web.md's switches |
| **energy transitions** | each new energy source lifts the ceiling | wood → charcoal → peat (E, P, L); coal is the edge |
| **institutions** (North) | secure property and enforceable contracts lower the cost of dealing | agreements (E), and the open question of who enforces ([[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]]) |
| **useful knowledge** (Joel Mokyr) | methods spread and accumulate through teaching, writing and contact | methods known by upbringing and teaching (E, 4c §3.3); records (N) |
| **urbanisation** | towns concentrate specialists and markets but must be fed from the land | a town node drawing food (N) |
| **the Malthusian check** | gains turn into people unless productivity keeps rising | births and land (E); the peoples run's famine |

**What sets them going is usually a combination:** enough people, a surplus, peace, transport, and someone (a lord, a merchant, a
village) able to gather capital. The runs have none of the last three for long, which is why they stay at §3.2.

## 5. What each step would need the world to hold

A catalogue of what could support each kind of society: goods, chains, works and rules. Not a build list.

| To support | Goods and chains | Works | Rules and relations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **villages that last** (§3.2) | the stage 1 web: leather, withies, cord, bone; keeping food; fuel | coppice, tan pits, rack, smokehouse | commons rules; sharing; teaching |
| **lordship** (§3.3) | luxuries (furs, fine bread, cheese); a hall's goods | the lord's mill, oven, smithy, hall | rents in labour and kind; rights over woods, game, mill; justice |
| **market towns** (§3.4) | regional staples (wool, grain, salt, ore); craft goods; coin | a market place, roads with tracks, carts, a bridge or ford | a market day; visible prices; weights and measures; tolls |
| **merchants** (§3.5) | goods worth carrying (cloth, salt fish, metal, wine); credit | warehouses, boats, inns | partnerships; enforceable contracts; reputation seen by others; bills of credit |
| **proto-industry** (§3.6) | flax and wool, yarn, cloth, nails | the loom, the spinning wheel, the fulling mill | wage labour; piece rates; materials put out and collected |
| **states** (§3.7) | money in quantity | roads, harbours, walls | taxation; public works by levy; coinage by an institution; law |

## 6. What scaling would ask of the engine itself

**People:**
- Each agent is a model call a day, so a society of thousands cannot be thousands of agents. A larger society needs **people who
  are not agents**: populations in households or villages that eat, work and breed by rule, with agents as heads of households,
  lords, merchants and officials (the peoples benchmark and [[L-104 MAJOR the descendants benchmark|L-104]] already point this way).
- Specialists could then be a rule-driven population that an agent employs or taxes, rather than each one deciding.

**Goods and acts:**
- Agents see only the methods they know ([[D4-24 The world grows by data, and agents explore and learn it|D4-24]]), which scales well: a merchant sees trade, a smith smithing.
- Aggregation becomes necessary: a town's bakers as one supply of bread at a price, not each loaf.

**Time and place:**
- A year is 32 days; a society's change takes generations. Runs of hundreds of days, or a history run before the agents arrive
  (as the ecology already does), would let institutions and capital exist at the start.
- Regions of tens of nodes with roads, rivers and a town are within the engine's model of space ([[D4-35 Everything happens in a place|D4-35]]); a kingdom is not yet.

**Prices and markets:**
- Today offers are seen only at the place they are made. A market needs **prices that travel**: heard from traders, posted at
  a market day, written in records. That is an information good (§1), and the senses guide's rules apply.

**Enforcement:**
- Today the engine settles agreements itself. A deeper economy needs enforcement to be something people provide (witnesses,
  courts, guilds, lords): OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]] is the gate for §3.3 to §3.7.

## 7. Questions the project could study with it

- **When does a village specialise?** Measure how far trade and transport have to fall before households give up self-sufficiency.
- **Who captures a threshold?** When a mill or a weir becomes worth building, who builds it, who pays to use it, and what rules
  follow?
- **Does a market emerge?** Given carriers and coin, do agents converge on a place and day to trade, and do prices converge across
  villages?
- **Does credit form without the engine enforcing it?** ([[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]].)
- **Does a commons find its rules?** The coppice, the river, the game.
- **Does a lord emerge,** and does redistribution or exchange win?
- **What does a famine do to depth?** Does it push a specialised economy back to the floor, and how fast does it climb again?

## 8. Limits of this analysis

- It leans on Western European, mostly English, medieval and early modern history; the branches in §3.9 are as real.
- The numbers are orders of magnitude, not calibrations.
- The sequence is a frame, not a law: real societies skip, combine and reverse these types.
