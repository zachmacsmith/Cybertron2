## Summary

Through almost all of history the unit of production was not a person or a firm but a **household**: a few adults, children and
often servants, with a labour budget that rose and fell with the seasons, a harvest peak several times the winter's demand, and
by-employments to fill the gaps. Around it, societies built answers to the same few problems: who may use land and its produce
(property), who gets the surplus (lordship, rent, tithe, tax), how promises are made to hold (kin and gossip, then courts, guilds,
merchant law, states), and how people survive bad years (stores, kin, credit, charity, poor relief, later insurance and the welfare
state). Labour relations moved from sharing among foragers, through slavery, serfdom and labour dues, to servants and day
labourers, guild crafts, factory wage work with unions and labour law, and the modern market of professions, licences and
platforms. Each step needed something before it (a storable surplus, a way to record and enforce claims, money, a market wide
enough to live from) and each changed what people produced and how they exchanged.

Population sat underneath. Before about 1800 more food mostly became more people, and real wages moved against numbers: the Black
Death roughly halved England's people and raised real farm wages by about two thirds. Famines were frequent, but Amartya Sen's
point holds back to the middle ages: people starve when they lose the means to command food, not only when food is absent. Late
marriage and service made the western European household a flexible labour and savings unit; the demographic transition then broke
the link between income and numbers. For a simulator the lesson is that **social structure is mostly made by people, around a few
physical facts the world must hold**: the labour calendar, land and its yield, who is present and sees what, records made by
deliberate acts, and bodies that are born, starve and die. The engine should hold those facts honestly and leave courts, guilds,
lords, rents, poor rates and unions for agents to build, as OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and sepa|L-106]] already points.

**On the figures.** *Checked*: confirmed against a source in this session. *Rough*: an order of magnitude or a range.
*Contested*: scholars disagree materially. *Unchecked*: a standard figure this session could not re-verify because the shared web
search budget ran out part-way. The labour budget in §1.3 is an illustrative construction, not a sourced series.

## 1. Labour and skill

### 1.1 Foragers and the first farmers

- **How much foragers work** is a long argument. Marshall Sahlins's "original affluent society" (1966, 1972) drew on Richard Lee's
  !Kung studies (often quoted: about 12 to 20 hours of food-getting a week, around 40 with tools and housework: *contested,
  unchecked*). Later work counted processing, travel and childcare and found longer days.
- **Kraft and others (2021)** compared apes, the Hadza (foragers) and the Tsimane (forager-horticulturalists): humans gain far more
  energy per hour than apes but did **not** turn it into leisure; the gain fed more dependants. Horticulture returned more than
  foraging for about the same time (*checked*, qualitative).
- **The first farmers worked harder.** Ester Boserup's argument: people intensify (shorter fallows, more weeding and manuring) under
  population pressure, raising output per acre and lowering it per hour. Early farmers' skeletons show more wear and disease than
  foragers' (*rough*).
- **What changed was storage.** Grain can be seen, measured, stored, stolen and taxed (James C. Scott, *Against the Grain*, 2017).
  Owning a surplus, holding dependants and extracting from others begin here, and with them slavery, tribute and the state.

### 1.2 The household as the unit of production

- **Chayanov's peasant household** (A. V. Chayanov, 1925): the family works its land until the drudgery of one more day's work
  balances what that day adds to the family's consumption. The ratio of consumers to workers drives how hard it works; a young
  family with many small children works hardest per worker. Output is shaped by the family life cycle, not only by prices.
- **Western European households were small and nuclear** (Peter Laslett's English listings: a mean household size around 4.75,
  steady from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century: *unchecked*). They grew by taking **servants** rather than by extended kin
  living together.
- **The household was a firm with many products** (grain, beasts, brewing, baking, spinning, fuel, building, care), most of it
  invisible to markets and much of it women's work.
- **Its labour was flexible:** it took a servant when children were small, sent its own into service when grown, hired at harvest
  and sold its own days in slack seasons. That was the pre-industrial labour market: households adjusting their size and time.

### 1.3 The peasant family's year

An **illustrative** budget for an English customary tenant of about 1300 with a yardland (about 30 acres in open fields, around 20
sown each year in a three-field rotation), two adults, a youth, small children and sometimes a servant. The ranges are built from
the orders of magnitude in Clark (1991), Campbell (2000) and Dyer (1989); treat them as a sketch of shape, not as measurements.

| Task | When | Person-days a year (rough) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ploughing and harrowing (crop land and two or three fallow ploughings) | autumn, spring, early summer | 40 to 70, with a team | about half to one acre a day for a team; teams often pooled between households |
| Sowing, harrowing in, clod-breaking | autumn and spring | 10 to 20 | |
| Weeding, hoeing, bird-scaring | late spring, early summer | 15 to 30 | children's work in part |
| Haymaking | early summer | 10 to 25 | a first peak |
| Harvest: reaping, binding, stooking, carting | 3 to 5 weeks in late summer | 60 to 100 | **the peak**: three or more times the winter's demand on hands |
| Threshing and winnowing | autumn and winter | 50 to 100 | spread out; the main winter task |
| Beasts: herding, milking, feeding, mucking out | all year | 100 to 200 | often children and women |
| Fuel, fencing, ditching, building, repair | winter and slack times | 30 to 60 | |
| Labour services owed to the lord (a villein yardlander) | through the year, with boon days at harvest | 0 to 150 | two or three days a week on some manors, often sent by a son or a servant; much less where commuted (§3) |
| Housework: food, brewing, baking, spinning, washing, childcare | every day | 300 or more | mostly women's; spinning filled the spare hours |
| Market, church, holy days, sickness | | Sundays and about 40 to 50 holy days | |

What the table shows:
- **Slack in winter, too few hands at harvest.** Every pre-industrial labour institution answers this: yearly servants, day
  labourers, boon work, neighbours' help repaid in kind, migrant harvest gangs.
- **By-employment fills the slack:** spinning, weaving, carting, quarrying, fishing, charcoal-burning, brewing for sale. Many
  smallholders were half craftsmen (*rough*); putting-out (§1.9) later rested on this slack.
- **How many days were worked** is contested. The older assumption was a working year of about 250 days. Humphries and Weisdorf
  (2019), estimating from the pay of workers on annual contracts, argue that casual workers' labour input rose from **under 150
  days a year in the medieval period to well over 300 by the Industrial Revolution** (*checked*; *contested*: others argue that
  medieval labourers worked 250 days or more, and that the low figure measures underemployment, not chosen leisure).

**For the engine:** a year is 32 days with 5 to 7 work units a day by season (spec/agents/work.md). The seasonal shape (a sharp
harvest peak and slack winters) is the part that matters; it is what creates demand for hired hands, for exchange of labour
between households, and for winter crafts.

### 1.4 Specialisation: how many specialists a village or town supported

- **A medieval English village of a few hundred people** typically had a smith, a miller (the lord's), a carpenter or wright, and
  part-time brewers (mostly women, brewing for sale in turns), bakers, weavers and tailors; a priest; and a reeve and a hayward
  chosen from the tenants (*rough*). Full-time specialists were few; part-time ones many.
- **Towns:** perhaps 15 to 20% of England's people lived in towns around 1300 (*rough*, Dyer, Britnell). Between 1200 and 1349 the
  crown granted well over 1,500 market charters (*rough, unchecked*: the Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs lists about 2,000 markets
  and fairs before 1516). Most people lived within a day's walk of a market.
- **What limits specialists** is the size of the market (Adam Smith) and the cost of moving food to them. A specialist must be fed
  by others' surplus: if a farming household feeds itself and a fraction more, the number of non-farmers is bounded by that
  fraction. With seed yields around four to one for wheat on medieval English demesnes (*rough*), the surplus per farmer was thin.
- **The long shift:** the share of the English workforce in agriculture fell from perhaps three quarters in the middle ages to
  about half by 1700 and under a quarter by 1850 (*rough*, Broadberry and others 2015; Wrigley); in rich countries today it is 1 to
  3% (*rough*).

### 1.5 Unfree labour: slavery and serfdom

**Slavery** appears wherever there is a surplus to guard, war to make captives, and estates or mines needing hands more than skill.
- **Roman Italy** at its height may have held one to two million slaves among six to seven million people (*rough, contested*,
  Scheidel); manumission was common.
- **Domesday England (1086):** slaves (*servi*) were just under one in ten of the recorded people (*checked*); slavery faded within
  about two generations as slaves were settled as smallholders.
- **The Atlantic trade:** about **12.5 million** people embarked from Africa, 1525 to 1866, and about **10.7 million** arrived
  (*checked*, Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database). The United States held nearly four million enslaved people in 1860
  (*unchecked*).

**Serfdom** binds the worker to the land and a lord rather than making him property.
- **Domesday:** villeins were about **41%** of the recorded population, holding about 45% of the land; bordars and cottars about
  **32%**, holding about 5% (*checked*); the rest free men and sokemen.
- **The English villein** around 1300 held a yardland or half for **week-work** (often two or three days a week on the great church
  estates), **boon works** at harvest, heriot at death, merchet to marry a daughter, and needed leave to leave; he could not sue his
  lord in the king's courts over his land (*rough*; dues varied by manor).
- **Its end in England** came after the Black Death: tenants could leave for better terms and lords gave way. The Peasants' Revolt
  of 1381 demanded the end of villeinage; it faded within a century.
- **The second serfdom** went the other way: as western grain demand rose in the sixteenth century, lords in Poland, Bohemia,
  Prussia and Russia raised labour dues (*robot*, *barshchina*) to three or more days a week in places (*rough, unchecked*). Russia
  abolished serfdom only in 1861.

Evsey Domar's hypothesis (1970): **free land, free labour and a landowning class cannot coexist.** Where land is plentiful and labour
scarce, landowners bind labour or get no rent. The Black Death made labour scarce east and west; western peasants won, eastern ones
lost, depending on the strength of lords, towns and states.

### 1.6 Servants, wage labour and day labourers

- **Servants in husbandry:** unmarried young people hired by the year, living in their employer's house, paid in board, lodging and
  a small yearly wage, changing masters at a hiring fair (often Michaelmas or Martinmas). In 63 English parish listings from 1574 to
  1821, servants were **13.4% of the whole population**, and around **60% of people aged 15 to 24** were in service (*checked*,
  Kussmaul 1981). Service was a life stage, not a class: it moved young labour to where it was needed, trained it, and let it save
  towards marriage.
- **Day labourers and cottagers:** households with little or no land who lived by wages, with a garden, common rights and the wife's
  spinning. In 1086 the bordars and cottars were the start of this class; by the seventeenth century perhaps a third or more of rural
  households lived mainly by wages (*rough*).
- **Wage regulation:** after the Black Death the Ordinance (1349) and Statute of Labourers (1351) tried to hold wages at pre-plague
  levels (the base year is given as 1346 or 1345) and to compel the able-bodied to work (*checked*). Enforcement was patchy; by the
  1370s the statute had largely lapsed in effect, though it was revived and replaced by the Statute of Artificers (1563), which let
  justices of the peace set wages and made yearly hiring the norm (*unchecked*).
- **Payment in kind** was everywhere: meals at work, a share of the harvest, gleaning rights, a cottage tied to the job. A medieval
  harvest worker's pay was often half food and drink.

### 1.7 Apprenticeship and guilds

- **Apprenticeship:** an indenture in which the master gives board, lodging and training, often for a premium, and the youth gives
  his labour for a term. The Statute of Artificers (1563) made **seven years** the minimum for crafts in England (*unchecked*).
- **Rules and reality:** a high proportion of London apprenticeships ended early, with early departures, late arrivals and absences
  common (Wallis 2008; Minns and Wallis 2012, *checked*, qualitative; the share completing is often put under half, *unchecked*).
  The master's cost came first and the apprentice's useful work last; leaving early was the apprentice's exit.
- **Guilds** regulated entry (apprenticeship, fees, a masterpiece, often being a master's son), quality (searches, seals on cloth),
  prices, the number of apprentices per master, and members' welfare.
- **What guilds were for is contested.** Stephan R. Epstein: they enforced training contracts, signalled quality and spread skills.
  Sheilagh Ogilvie (*The European Guilds*, 2019): mostly rent-seeking, excluding outsiders, women and Jews, raising prices and
  resisting new methods, with benefits shared with the authorities that sold them privileges. They withered or were abolished from
  the late eighteenth century (France 1791).

### 1.8 Wages and their real value over time

| Period (England) | Day wage, unskilled | Day wage, craftsman | Real wage, as read by the literature |
|---|---|---|---|
| about 1300 | 1 to 1.5d | about 3d | low; land-hungry, before the famine of 1315 to 1317 |
| about 1450 | about 4d | about 6d | high: real farm wages about **64% above** pre-plague by the fifteenth century (Clark, *checked*); builders' roughly doubled (Phelps Brown and Hopkins, *rough*) |
| about 1600 | about 8d | about 12d | fallen back after the price rise and population recovery |
| about 1750 | about 12d | 18 to 24d | rising; London and Amsterdam the highest in Europe |

(Money wages *rough, unchecked*, from the Farmer, Munro and Clark series; read the trend, not the pennies.)

- **Robert Allen's welfare ratios** divide annual earnings by the cost of a family's "bare-bones" basket. London and Amsterdam
  workers in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries earned roughly **three to four times** it; Delhi and Beijing workers little
  more than one (*rough*; Allen 2001, 2009). Dear labour and cheap coal are Allen's reason Britain mechanised.
- **Gregory Clark** reads near-stagnation from 1200 to 1800, with the plague era a high point (*A Farewell to Alms*, 2007).
- **The day count matters** (§1.3): a daily wage times 250 days overstates medieval incomes if casual workers found fewer days.

### 1.9 The industrious revolution

- **Jan de Vries** (1994, 2008): from the mid-seventeenth century north-west European households worked more days and more for the
  market (women and children in cottage industry, men giving up "Saint Monday" and holy days) to buy new goods: sugar, tea, tobacco,
  pottery, printed cotton, clocks. Demand changed before supply.
- **Hours:** Hans-Joachim Voth (2001), from witnesses in criminal trials, finds male annual hours rose about 30% between the 1750s
  and 1800: in London from about 2,288 to 3,666 as Saint Monday and about 53 holy days went, in the North from about 2,860 to 3,366
  by 1830 (*checked*, from reviews).
- **The other reading:** Allen and Weisdorf (2011) find two industrious episodes among farm labourers, both from hardship (working
  more for the same basket), not a consumer revolution; London workers had the margin for one (*checked*).
- **Putting-out:** a merchant gives wool or flax to rural households, who spin and weave at home for piece wages in the farm's slack
  season (§1.3). Franklin Mendels argued it loosened the link between land and marriage (*contested*).

### 1.10 Factory labour, unions and labour law

- **The factory** gathered workers under one roof with water and then steam power, supervision and a clock; its gains came from
  power, machines and control of work. Early mills employed many women and children for twelve hours a day and more.
- **Labour law** in Britain came by stages: the Factory Act 1833 (children, inspectors), the Ten Hours Act 1847, repeal of the
  Combination Acts 1824 to 1825, the Trade Union Act 1871 (*unchecked*, standard dates).
- **Unions** began as craft and friendly societies (sick pay, funerals), raised wages where they could restrict supply or stop
  production, and became political. Across the OECD density halved from about **30% in 1985 to about 15% in 2023 to 2024**; US
  density fell from 20.1% (1983) to **9.9% (2024)**; UK membership peaked in 1979 and density is about 22% (*checked*).
- **Hours fell** from around 3,000 a year in western Europe around 1870 to about 1,400 to 1,800 in rich countries today (*rough,
  unchecked*, Huberman and Minns 2007; OECD).

### 1.11 Modern labour markets: professions, licences and platforms

- **The employment contract** became the main relation: open-ended, with the employer directing the work (Ronald Coase: some
  transactions are cheaper to direct than to contract each time); careers, pensions and health insurance tied to the job.
- **Professions** are the guilds' heirs: entry by education and examination, self-regulation, often a state licence. In the United
  States the share of workers needing a licence rose from about **5% in the 1950s to over a quarter by the 2000s** (*unchecked*,
  Kleiner and Krueger 2013). The Epstein-Ogilvie argument (§1.7) recurs.
- **The gig economy** is piece rates and day labour with a digital hiring fair and a reputation score. It is small relative to its
  attention (about 1% of US employment as main work in the 2017 BLS survey, *unchecked*); whether its workers are employees is
  fought over in many countries.
- **What persists from the village:** households pool income and time; unpaid work is still divided by sex; reputation still
  governs much hiring.

### 1.12 Labour: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Relation | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| household production | land, tools, division of labour by age and sex | flexible labour over the life cycle | sets the floor of output; exchange of surplus only |
| slavery | surplus, captives or a slave trade, coercive law | gang labour on estates, mines, plantations | depresses free wages; tied to long-distance trade |
| serfdom and labour dues | a lord with force; labour scarcer than land | a demesne worked without wages | the lord's peak is also the tenant's; dues commute as markets grow |

## 2. Property and rights

### 2.1 Property as a bundle of rights

Edella Schlager and Elinor Ostrom (1992) split property into rights of **access**, **withdrawal** (taking produce), **management**
(deciding how it is used), **exclusion** (deciding who may use it) and **alienation** (selling or leasing the other rights). Their
point is that most historical property was **split**: a tenant had access and withdrawal; the lord had exclusion and alienation;
the village set rules (management) for fallow and grazing; the king had rights over game. The engine already uses exactly this
bundle (spec/world/holding.md, [[D4-23 Holding is a recognised claim|D4-23]]), with claims recognised by the claimant, by custom or by an institution's register, and
enforced only by people.

### 2.2 From use rights to tenure

- **Foragers:** territories used by a band, visitors asking leave; personal tools; **demand sharing** (Nicolas Peterson: those who
  have must give when asked).
- **Early farmers:** land held by lineages and households; herds as movable wealth; inheritance starts to matter.
- **Medieval England:**
  - **freehold:** held for fixed, often nominal, services; heritable and saleable; protected by the king's courts from Henry II's
    assizes (1160s to 1170s);
  - **villein or customary tenure:** "at the will of the lord according to the custom of the manor", protected only in the manor
    court; a transfer was a surrender and regrant, entered on the court roll;
  - **copyhold:** customary tenure after serfdom, the title a copy of the roll entry, protected by royal courts by the sixteenth
    century; terms for lives, by inheritance or at will, with entry fines as the lord's lever;
  - **leases** for years at money rent: the demesnes were leased from the late fourteenth century, making the tenant farmers of the
    early modern period.
- **A peasant land market** shows in court rolls from the thirteenth century: small plots bought, leased and mortgaged between
  villagers to balance land with family size.
- **Inheritance shaped holdings:** primogeniture kept them whole and pushed younger children into service, crafts and towns;
  partible inheritance (Kent's gavelkind, much of France and western Germany) supported denser, poorer populations with more
  by-employment.

### 2.3 Commons and their regulation

- **The open-field village** had arable in strips scattered across two or three great fields, meadow allotted for hay, and common
  pasture and waste; after harvest, and on the fallow, everyone's beasts grazed the arable.
- **By-laws** made in the manor court set when fields opened and closed, how many beasts each holding might graze (the **stint**,
  usually in proportion to arable held), who might glean (often only the poor, old and young, after the sheaves were carried), the
  hayward's duties, and fines for strays and for wood or turves beyond one's allowance (Warren Ault).
- **Why strips were scattered** is argued: equity at founding; ploughing in common; insurance (Donald McCloskey: strips across
  soils cut the variance of a household's harvest at some cost in output, *contested*).
- **Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons"** (1968) assumed open access. Medieval commons were regulated common property with
  known users. Elinor Ostrom's **design principles** for lasting commons (1990):
  1. clearly defined boundaries of the resource and its users;
  2. rules that fit local conditions, with costs in proportion to benefits;
  3. those affected help make the rules;
  4. monitoring by users or people answerable to them;
  5. graduated sanctions;
  6. cheap, local conflict resolution;
  7. outside authorities recognise the right to organise;
  8. nested layers for larger systems.
  Medieval by-laws meet most: the stint (2), the court and its tenant jury (3, 6), the hayward and reeve (4), rising amercements (5),
  the lord's and king's recognition (7). Ostrom's own cases include Törbel's Swiss alpine meadows, governed by village rules since at
  least 1224.

### 2.4 Rights in game, water and mills

- **Game:** the Norman kings put large areas under forest law (perhaps a quarter to a third of England at its widest, *rough*),
  reserving deer to the king; small game needed a grant of free warren. Poaching was among the commonest and most resented
  offences; game laws stayed strict into the nineteenth century.
- **Water:** fisheries and weirs were valuable lords' rights; a weir blocked boats, and Magna Carta (1215) ordered fish weirs
  removed from the Thames and Medway.
- **Mills and mill soke:** tenants were bound to grind at the lord's mill (**suit of mill**) and pay **multure**, from about one
  twelfth to one thirtieth of the grain (one source: one thirteenth for serfs, one twenty-fourth for free men: *checked*). Hand
  querns were confiscated: the abbots of St Albans paved a floor with tenants' querns, and the townsmen dug them up in 1381. Domesday
  records about 6,000 mills (*unchecked*); John Langdon puts England's mills at over 10,000 by 1300 (*rough, unchecked*). About 43%
  of fourteenth-century powered mills were the church's (Adam Lucas, *checked*).

### 2.5 Enclosure

- **What it was:** ending common rights and open fields, consolidating strips into compact fields under one holder, often hedged. It
  was done by agreement among holders, by a lord's purchase or pressure, or from 1750 onwards mostly by private act of parliament.
- **Timing** (J. R. Wordie 1983): England was perhaps **45% enclosed by 1500** and **47% by 1600**; about **24%** of England was
  enclosed in the seventeenth century; parliamentary enclosure in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries took in about 7.25 to 7.35
  million acres, some **22%** of England's 32.5 million acres (*checked* acreages; percentage computed here).
- **Effects** (*contested*): Robert Allen (*Enclosure and the Yeoman*, 1992) found that the open fields had already adopted most
  improvements (the "yeoman's revolution" of the seventeenth century), and that eighteenth-century enclosure raised rents more than
  output, redistributing income to landowners. Others find higher yields and more investment. For the landless, losing grazing,
  fuel and gleaning rights cut real income (Jane Humphries on women and children's earnings from commons).
- **The general lesson:** turning a split bundle of rights into a single holder's full bundle pays off where there is a market for
  the output, a way to compensate (or override) the holders of small rights, and a strong institution to register and enforce the
  result.

### 2.6 Corporations, intellectual property and modern property

- **Corporations** hold property, sue and outlive their members: medieval towns, guilds, monasteries and colleges; then chartered
  companies with tradable shares (English East India Company 1600, Dutch VOC 1602); general incorporation and limited liability in
  the nineteenth century (England 1855 to 1856, *unchecked*), which let strangers invest without risking everything.
- **Intellectual property:** Venice's patent statute (1474), England's Statute of Monopolies (1624), copyright from the Statute of
  Anne (1710) (*unchecked*). Ideas are non-rival: property in them is wholly an institution.
- **Registers and titles** make land collateral; Hernando de Soto (2000) argues the poor of many countries cannot borrow against
  customary, unregistered claims.
- **Modern property is mostly claims on claims:** shares, bonds, pensions, deposits, policies, kept by institutions, enforced by
  courts.

### 2.7 Property: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Form | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| use rights, demand sharing | small mobile groups | access for all; insurance | little investment in land |
| split tenure (lord, tenant, village) | a lord with force; a court; custom | rent; order on shared land | transfers recorded; custom sets dues |
| regulated commons | known users; a meeting; a monitor; sanctions | lasting use of pasture, wood, fish | grazing in proportion to arable; gleaning for the poor |
| rights in game, water, mills | lordship backed by law | monopoly rent | dearer milling and food; poaching and querns as resistance |
| enclosure, full private holding | a market for output; registration; power over small rights | consolidation, investment | landless labour; higher rents |
| corporations, intellectual property | charters or general law; courts; registers | pooled capital from strangers; rewards for invention | firms as parties; monopoly for a term |

## 3. Lordship and rent

### 3.1 The manor

- **Parts:** the lord's **demesne** (worked directly, by servants, hired labour and tenants' dues), the tenants' holdings (free and
  villein), the commons, and the lord's monopolies (mill, oven, sometimes the brewhouse, the press in wine country, the fold where
  tenants' sheep had to lie to manure the lord's land).
- **Officers:** a steward (the lord's man, often over several manors), a bailiff, and from the tenants a **reeve** (chosen yearly to
  run the demesne and account for it), a hayward (crops and fences), and the manor court's jurors. The yearly **account roll**
  recorded every bushel and penny; these rolls are the main source for medieval agriculture.
- **Not every village was one manor** and not every manor was one village; many villages had several lords, and many tenants held
  of more than one.

### 3.2 Labour dues to money rents

- **Commutation** (replacing services with money) went in waves: much in the twelfth century, some return to services in the
  thirteenth as demesne farming paid well under high grain prices, then commutation and leasing of demesnes from the later
  fourteenth century (M. M. Postan's argument, *contested* in detail). By 1300 money rents were already the largest part of what
  most tenants paid (*rough*).
- **Why lords preferred money** when they could: services were worked badly ("the work of a villein is worth half that of a hired
  man" was a common lords' complaint, *rough*), monitoring cost a reeve's time, and money could be spent anywhere. **Why lords kept
  services:** when labour was scarce and wages high, a claim on labour was worth more than its money value.
- **After the Black Death,** lords first tried to keep services and raise fines (the "seigneurial reaction" behind 1381), then gave
  way. By the fifteenth century the demesnes were leased, rents had fallen, and the tenants who stayed held more land on better terms.

### 3.3 The lord's monopolies and the tithe

- **Banalités** (the French term; *bannum*, the lord's power to command): the mill, the oven, the wine press, sometimes the bull and
  the boar. The tenant paid in kind each time, so the lord's share rose with output.
- **Tithe:** a tenth of the produce to the church: the great tithes (grain, hay, wood) usually to the rector (often a monastery or a
  college that had taken the church over), the small tithes (lambs, wool, milk, eggs, garden produce) to the vicar. Collection in the
  field, by counting stooks, was a constant source of dispute. Tithes survived in England, commuted to a money rent charge in 1836
  (*unchecked*).

### 3.4 How the surplus was extracted and spent

**The burden** on a villein yardlander around 1300 has been put at anything from a fifth to a half of gross output, adding rent,
services, tithe, mill tolls, entry fines, heriot, merchet and royal taxes (*contested*: Kosminsky and Postan high, Kanzaka and
Campbell lower; free tenants paid far less). Orders of magnitude only:

| Claim | Share of gross output (rough) | Paid how |
|---|---|---|
| seed for next year | a fifth to a quarter of the grain | kept back in the household |
| tithe | a tenth | in kind, in the field |
| rent and services | a tenth to a quarter | money, labour, some kind |
| mill tolls, fines, heriot, merchet | a few percent, lumpy | kind and money |
| royal taxes | a few percent in tax years | money and goods |

**Lords spent it** on households and retinues (dozens to hundreds fed), building (castles, halls, churches, colleges), war, pious
gifts and distant luxuries (wine, spices, fine cloth); much less on improving estates (*rough*). Lordship turned rural surplus into
urban demand.

### 3.5 Lordship: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Element | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| demesne and labour dues | tenants who owe labour; officers to direct and count | grain and wool at scale; the lord's household fed | competes with tenants' land for labour at the peak |
| money rent | markets; coin | lords' spending anywhere; tenants' freedom of time | pushes tenants to sell |
| monopolies (mill, oven) | capital beyond a household; power to forbid alternatives | mills built early; steady income | a toll on every loaf; evasion |
| tithe | a church with legal power | parishes, clergy, some relief | a tenth off the top, in kind |

## 4. Law and enforcement

### 4.1 Before courts

- **Foragers and small villages** keep promises by repeated dealing, gossip and ridicule, sharing withdrawn, and exit (leaving the
  band). Christopher Boehm's "reverse dominance hierarchy": the group levels anyone who tries to dominate.
- **Feud and compensation:** among early farmers and in early medieval Europe, kin groups answer injury with vengeance or a payment
  (the Anglo-Saxon *wergild*, a price on each rank's life). Order rests on kin being able to retaliate.

### 4.2 Village and manor courts

- **The manor court** met every three weeks or so (*rough*), with a **view of frankpledge** once or twice a year. It handled land
  transfers and heirs, by-laws and their breach, trespass and debt between tenants, failed services, and the assize of bread and ale
  (fines on brewers and bakers that worked as a licence).
- **Frankpledge:** every man over twelve was in a **tithing** of about ten, bound to produce any member who offended: policing by
  neighbours.
- **Juries of tenants** found the facts and declared the custom, which was written into the rolls. Deals were made before
  witnesses and backed by **pledges** (sureties who paid if the principal did not); people paid to have agreements enrolled.
- **Village credit:** most villagers both borrowed and lent, and the court was a key reason they could (Chris Briggs, 2009).

### 4.3 Guild courts, merchant law and fairs

- **Guilds** judged their members' disputes and offences against craft rules; their sanctions were fines, loss of the right to
  trade, and expulsion.
- **The law merchant** (*lex mercatoria*): customs among traders, applied in fair and borough courts with merchant judges and fast
  procedure ("piepowder" courts at English fairs: for travellers with dusty feet). Milgrom, North and Weingast (1990) model the
  Champagne fair courts as a cheap record-keeper: a merchant could ask a judge whether a trading partner had unpaid judgments
  before dealing, and judges' rulings were enforced by exclusion from the fairs. This is a reputation system with a third party
  keeping the record.
- **Communal responsibility:** Avner Greif describes how medieval towns made all their merchants liable for one member's debts
  abroad, which let strangers from different towns trade; it worked while towns were few and members identifiable.

### 4.4 Reputation: the Maghribi traders

- **Avner Greif** (1989, 1993, 2006), from eleventh-century letters in the Cairo Geniza: Jewish merchants in the Mediterranean hired
  agents in distant ports, shared news of agents' honesty by letter, and collectively refused to employ an agent who cheated any of
  them. The value of future employment exceeded the gain from cheating.
- **The Genoese** relied more on bilateral relations, contracts and courts; Greif argues the collectivist system did not scale to
  strangers while the court-based one could. Edwards and Ogilvie (2012) find the Maghribis used courts and contracts too
  (*contested*).
- **It needed** a closed group, a channel of information, a shared rule of punishment and repeated dealing. **The runs lack the
  channel** (38.68): witnesses are recorded but never perceive defaults, and nobody tells a third party.

### 4.5 Royal courts, the common law and Douglass North

- **Royal justice** in England grew from Henry II's assizes, with travelling justices and juries; Common Pleas and King's Bench
  heard debt and contract; the action of assumpsit (sixteenth century) made informal promises enforceable.
- **The economy of obligation:** Craig Muldrew (1998) shows late sixteenth- and early seventeenth-century households living by long
  chains of informal credit, with debt litigation at extraordinary levels (most households party to a suit at some time, *rough,
  unchecked*). "Credit" meant both a loan and a reputation; suing was the last resort that made the threat credible.
- **Douglass North:** institutions are "the rules of the game", formal and informal; **transaction costs** (finding partners,
  measuring, negotiating, monitoring, enforcing) are a large share of all activity, and economies grow as institutions lower them
  (1990). North and Weingast (1989): after 1688 parliament's control of taxes and independent courts made the crown's promises
  credible, so it could borrow cheaply. North, Wallis and Weingast (2009): "limited access" against "open access" orders.
- **Weights, coin and writing** are enforcement too: assized measures, coin of guaranteed weight, sealed bonds with a penalty for
  default that replaced living witnesses as the record.

### 4.6 Modern enforcement

- Contract law, company law, police and courts at scale; regulators (for banks, food, drugs, labour); credit bureaus and ratings
  (reputation kept by third parties as a business); platforms' ratings. The cost of enforcing a contract still varies hugely between
  countries (the World Bank's Doing Business measured times from under a year to several years, *rough*).

### 4.7 Enforcement: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Mechanism | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| kin, gossip, exit; feud | small groups, talk; kin able to retaliate | help and order without a state | trade with strangers barely possible |
| manor courts, juries, pledges | a regular meeting, a record, an authority | land market, local credit, by-laws | cheap dealing among neighbours |
| guild and fair courts, merchant law | a body that can exclude; fairs; merchant judges | quality rules; trade among strangers | long-distance commerce; entry barriers |
| reputation coalitions | a closed group and a channel of information | agency across distance | trade confined to the group |
| royal courts, contract law, written bonds | judges, records, sheriffs, literacy | enforceable promises among anyone | credit chains; impersonal markets |

## 5. The state and taxation

### 5.1 From chiefs to early states

- **Chiefdoms** collect tribute (food, labour, prestige goods) and give back feasts, protection and judgement: Karl Polanyi's
  **redistribution**.
- **Early states** (Mesopotamia, Egypt, China) rested on grain, which can be seen in the field, measured, stored and carried (Scott
  2017). They took taxes in grain and labour (corvée for canals, walls and tombs), kept granaries, counted people and fields, and
  wrote, first of all, to keep accounts.
- **What states provided:** defence, order, irrigation and storage, standard measures, sometimes famine relief (Chinese state
  granaries in the eighteenth century, *rough*), and the cult.

### 5.2 Medieval taxation

- **Feudal obligations:** knights' service for land, scutage (money in place of service), the lord's aids at fixed occasions.
- **Taxes on movables:** the English lay subsidy (a fraction, often a fifteenth in the countryside and a tenth in towns, of the value
  of movable goods, assessed by local juries; fixed as quotas per vill from 1334, *unchecked*), the poll taxes of 1377 to 1381 (the
  third of which set off the Peasants' Revolt).
- **Purveyance:** the king's officers buying (or seizing) food and carts for the royal household and armies at low prices: a tax in
  kind and a constant grievance.
- **Customs** on wool exports became the crown's most reliable revenue from 1275.
- **The Church** taxed too: the tithe and papal taxes.

### 5.3 The fiscal-military state

- **The military revolution** (standing armies, new fortifications, navies) made war far dearer; states that could raise and borrow
  money won.
- **Britain after 1688** (John Brewer, 1989): parliament's control of taxation, a professional excise, the Bank of England (1694)
  and a funded debt raised taxes from a few percent of national income in the late seventeenth century to the low teens by the late
  eighteenth and near a fifth in the Napoleonic wars (*rough, unchecked*, O'Brien 1988). Most was indirect: excise on beer, malt,
  salt, candles, leather, soap, and customs (taxes on traded goods were over half of indirect taxes by about 1800, Dal Bó and others
  2025, *checked*). The Dutch Republic pioneered the model.
- **Collection needed capacity:** measurement, records and paid officials (excise men gauging casks).

### 5.4 Public goods

- **Roads:** in England, parishes had to maintain roads with statute labour (six days a year per householder from 1555,
  *unchecked*); turnpike trusts (from 1663, most from the 1700s) charged tolls and borrowed to improve roads; canal and railway
  companies came later under private acts.
- **Defence:** the single largest public spending item before the twentieth century.
- **Justice:** courts and their records; policing was local and voluntary (constables chosen in turn) until the nineteenth century.
- **Coinage:** the crown's mint and its standard; debasement as a hidden tax (the Great Debasement of 1544 to 1551 in England).
- **Poor relief:** from the parish (§6.5).

### 5.5 State capacity

- **What it is:** the ability to tax, to enforce law and contracts, and to provide public goods across a territory (Besley and
  Persson, *Pillars of Prosperity*, 2011). It needs measurement (surveys, censuses, cadastres), records, officials who are paid and
  monitored, and consent or coercion.
- **Its history:** early modern European states with fiscal centralisation and limited government (parliaments controlling taxes)
  raised more revenue per head (Mark Dincecco, 2011). Competition through war drove the build-up (Charles Tilly: "war made the
  state, and the state made war").

### 5.6 The modern regulatory and welfare state

- **Size:** average public spending in industrial countries was about **8 to 11% of GDP around 1870**, about **13% in 1913** and
  nearly **46% by 1996** (*checked*, Tanzi and Schuknecht). The United States went from 19.7% (1937) to 27.0% (1960) and 31.4%
  (1980); France from 29.0% to 34.6% and 46.1% (*checked*).
- **What drove it:** wars (taxes that did not fall back), the franchise and **social spending** on pensions, unemployment, health and
  schooling. Peter Lindert (2004): transfers were tiny before 1880 and grew with democracy and ageing; high-spending welfare states
  did not grow more slowly, because they chose taxes that did little harm.
- **Steps:** Bismarck's social insurance (1883 to 1889), British pensions (1908) and National Insurance (1911), US Social Security
  (1935), the Beveridge Report (1942).
- **Regulation** (food, drugs, labour, banks, competition, environment, zoning) now sets much of the terms of every contract.

### 5.7 The state: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Form | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| tribute and redistribution | prestige; a storable surplus | feasts, alliance, big works | surplus to the centre and out |
| taxes in grain and labour | measurable crops; records; force | granaries, canals, armies | farmers grow what can be taxed |
| taxes on goods and trade; funded debt | coin, markets, officials; credible promises | revenue that grows with trade; war finance | prices raised; smuggling; capital drawn to bonds |
| public goods | taxes, tolls or levies | lower transport and transaction costs | wider markets, finer specialisation |
| welfare and regulation | income taxes, votes, bureaucracy | insurance against age, sickness, unemployment | labour supply, saving and fertility change |

## 6. Population and risk

### 6.1 Forager and early farming demography

- **Foragers** keep births spaced three to four years apart (long breastfeeding, mobility), with high infant and child mortality;
  population grows very slowly.
- **Farmers** have shorter birth intervals (weaning foods, sedentism) and more children, but also more infectious disease (density,
  animals, stored food that draws pests). The Neolithic demographic transition raised numbers far faster than health.

### 6.2 Malthusian dynamics and the evidence

- **The model** (Thomas Malthus, 1798): with land fixed and technology slow, more people mean less land each and falling real wages;
  falling wages raise deaths (the **positive check**) or delay marriage and reduce births (the **preventive check**); population
  and wages move inversely around a long-run equilibrium.
- **England's evidence:**

| Date | England's population (rough) | Real wages |
|---|---|---|
| about 1300 to 1315 | about 4.5 to 6 million (the medieval peak; one estimate 5.8 million before the Great Famine) | low |
| 1348 | about 4.5 to 5 million | low |
| 1440s to 1450 | about 2 to 2.5 million (one estimate 2.3 million) | high; farm wages about 64% above pre-plague |
| 1550 | about 3 million | falling |
| 1650 | about 5.3 million | low |
| 1750 | about 5.8 million | fairly high, then squeezed late in the century |
| 1851 | about 16.7 million | rising, and no longer bound to population |

  (Medieval figures *contested* within about a third; checked ranges from Broadberry and others and summaries of their work; later
  figures *rough*, from Wrigley and Schofield 1981.)
- **The Black Death** (1347 to 1351) killed perhaps 30 to 50% of Europe's people (*contested*; Ole Benedictow argues about 60%);
  recurrent plague kept English population down until the early sixteenth century. It is the clearest natural experiment in the
  Malthusian model: fewer people, more land per head, higher wages, better diets (more meat, ale and wheat bread), the end of
  serfdom, and, in the west, a shift from grain to pasture.
- **Escaping the trap:** after about 1800 in England, population and real wages rose together. Explanations: energy from coal
  (Wrigley), high wages inducing mechanisation (Allen), cultural and demographic change (Clark), useful knowledge (Mokyr), and
  institutions (North). Unified growth theory (Oded Galor) models the transition as technology raising the return to education,
  which led parents to choose fewer, better-educated children.

### 6.3 Famine

- **Frequency:** pre-industrial European harvests were poor about one year in four (Slicher van Bath, *checked*), and bad years
  clustered. A famine with heavy mortality came perhaps once or twice a century in a region (*rough*). England's last famine
  mortality was in the 1620s; France still had them in 1693 to 1694 and 1709 to 1710 (*unchecked*).
- **The Great Famine of 1315 to 1317:** rain ruined harvests across northern Europe; England lost perhaps 10 to 15% of its people,
  possibly more, towns worst (*checked* range, estimates "far from secure"). A murrain of cattle and sheep followed (1319 to 1321),
  killing ploughing oxen and so the next harvests.
- **Amartya Sen's entitlements** (1981): a famine is a collapse of some groups' ability to command food by what they own, grow,
  earn or are given. In Bengal in 1943 (around 2 to 3 million deaths, *rough*) food was not unusually short, but war inflation put
  it beyond rural labourers' wages while the state bought for Calcutta and the army. Labourers, fishermen and artisans starved, not
  landholders. Sen also observed no substantial famine in a functioning democracy with a free press.
- **In medieval terms,** cottagers and craftsmen starved first; smallholders with stores survived; lords with granaries profited.
  The peoples run (38.70) has the same shape: Brockley held about 110 food and did not answer a starving visitor.
- **Storage was costly:** rats, damp and tied-up capital. McCloskey and Nash (1984) read high storage costs and interest from the
  seasonal rise of grain prices (*rough*). Peasants rarely carried grain over a year; storage at scale (granaries, China's
  ever-normal granaries) was a public good.

### 6.4 Disease

- **Epidemics** (plague, smallpox, typhus, dysentery, influenza) were the largest killers in most years, and plague was largely
  independent of food supply. Towns were **graveyards**: before the nineteenth century urban deaths exceeded births, and towns grew
  only by migration from the countryside (London in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries absorbed a large share of England's
  natural increase, *rough*, Wrigley 1967).
- **Life expectancy at birth** in England was about 35 to 40 years from the sixteenth to the early nineteenth century, much of it
  infant and child deaths; those who reached 20 often lived into their fifties or sixties (*rough*, Wrigley and Schofield).
- **The end of epidemic mortality** came from quarantine (plague left western Europe by the 1720s), smallpox inoculation and
  vaccination (1796), and from the 1850s clean water and sewers, then germ theory, then antibiotics and vaccines.

### 6.5 Insurance: kin, credit, charity and poor relief

| Means | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **stores and diversification** | grain and beasts held back; scattered strips, several crops, by-employments | losses in store; a two-year failure; lower output in good years |
| **kin** | help from kin; retirement contracts (land to an heir for maintenance, on the court roll) | English kin networks were thin and households nuclear (Laslett, Richard Smith) |
| **neighbours** | food gifts, help at harvest, loans in kind | fails when everyone is short |
| **credit** | grain or money against the next harvest or land | default; distress sales of land |
| **charity, gilds, friendly societies** | alms from monasteries, lords, wills; members' sick and burial funds | uneven; much went with the monasteries (1536 to 1540) |
| **poor relief** | the parish taxes householders and relieves the old, sick, widowed, children, and in bad years the able-bodied | settlement rules limited who a parish relieved |

- **The Old Poor Law** (1597, 1601) made every parish raise a rate. In 1696 relief cost about £400,000, just under **1% of national
  income** (*checked*); real spending per head more than doubled between 1748 to 1750 and 1803 (*checked*), reaching about 2% of
  national income around 1800 to 1830 (*rough, unchecked*, Boyer). Formal relief in western Europe could reach about 3% of GDP
  before the welfare state (van Bavel and Rijpma 2016, *checked*).
- **What it did:** Peter Solar argues a reliable, local, tax-funded insurance let English labourers give up land for wage work
  without risking starvation, helping explain England's early escape from famine (*contested*). The New Poor Law (1834) put the
  able-bodied in the workhouse to make relief "less eligible" than the worst job.
- **Later insurance:** marine insurance (fourteenth-century Italy), life insurance and annuities (seventeenth century), then state
  social insurance (§5.6) doing the work of stores, kin and the parish.

### 6.6 Household formation: the European marriage pattern

- **John Hajnal (1965)** found west of a line from Trieste to St Petersburg **late marriage** (women about 24 to 26, men 26 to 28),
  **many never marrying** (10 to 20%) and **new households on marriage**; east of it three quarters of women were married by 25
  (*checked*).
- **England:** women's mean age at first marriage was about 25 to 26 in the seventeenth century, falling to about 23 by the early
  nineteenth (*checked*). Marriage followed real wages with a lag: Malthus's **preventive check** working through an institution.
- **It needed** service as a life stage, a norm that a couple set up its own household, inheritance late in the parents' life, and
  the church's rule of consent.
- **It enabled** (*contested*) fertility below the biological maximum, long youth labour on the market, savings before marriage,
  some bargaining power for women (de Moor and van Zanden's "girl power"), and more investment in children. Voigtländer and Voth
  (2013) tie it to the Black Death's shift to pasture, where women had better work.

### 6.7 The demographic transition and modern urban population

- **The transition:** death rates fell first (from the late eighteenth century in north-west Europe), then birth rates fell (from
  the 1870s in most of Europe; France earlier). In between, population grew fast: England grew from about 5.8 million in
  1750 to about 16.7 million by 1851 (*rough*).
- **Today:** the world's total fertility rate is about **2.25 births per woman**, one child fewer than a generation ago, projected to
  fall to 2.1 by the late 2040s; life expectancy at birth reached about **73.3 years in 2024**; and nearly **58%** of the world's
  people live in towns and cities (2024), up from about 44% in 1994 (*checked*, UN World Population Prospects 2024 and DESA). Many
  rich countries have fertility far below replacement and ageing populations; the dependency burden shifts from children to the old,
  and from kin to the state.
- **Towns stopped being graveyards** with clean water and medicine; they now grow by their own natural increase and by migration, and
  urban households are smaller, with fewer children, more single people and more women in paid work.

### 6.8 Population and risk: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Element | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| storage | buildings, dry grain, protection | survival of a bad year | stocks smooth prices; hoarding raises them |
| kin, credit, charity, relief | kin nearby; lenders; a parish that can tax | a floor under the poor; labour mobility | distress sales concentrate land; relief floors wages |
| late marriage | service; neolocal norms; consent | lower fertility; savings; youth labour | ties population to wages |
| the demographic transition | falling mortality; schooling; returns to skill | growth per head | smaller families; more capital per child |

## 7. How these interact with production and exchange

- **The harvest peak creates the labour market:** boon work, hiring fairs and migrant gangs all sit on it.
- **Labour scarcity against land decides who wins** (Domar): freedom in England after the Black Death, the second serfdom in the east.
- **Rights in chokepoints become rents,** from the mill and the weir to the guild, the patent and the licence, and each draws evasion.
- **Enforcement scales with records and third parties.** The runs are stuck at the bilateral stage (38.68).
- **Extraction shapes markets:** money rents and taxes push peasants to sell; lords' and states' spending makes urban demand.
- **Whoever carries risk gains a claim on the poor:** land in distress sales, labour for relief.
- **Population answers wages,** through deaths and marriage, until the transition.

## 8. What a simulator could take from this

### 8.1 Which labour relations, institutions and population dynamics matter most for LLM agents to face

1. **A seasonal labour calendar with a sharp harvest peak.** It is the single fact that makes households need each other, and it
   should be strong enough that a household cannot reap its fields alone in time. Ripe grain that is not reaped spoils or shatters.
   The spec already varies units by season (work.md); the crop's harvest window should be narrow enough to bind.
2. **Households with more land than hands and households with more hands than land,** at the same time, in the same place. That is
   what creates servants, day labourers, tenancies and lordship. Seed both kinds; vary them by generation as the peoples worlds age.
3. **A chokepoint someone can hold:** a mill (with querns slower but possible), a weir, an oven, a ford, a seed store. Mill soke and
   its evasion is the archetype: the rule is made by people, the physics (querns grind slowly, a mill fast) by the world.
4. **Commons that can be over-used,** with their users able to see who used them: a coppice, a pasture, a fishery, game. Ostrom's
   principles are a checklist for whether agents built a working commons; the engine should only provide the resource dynamics and
   the witnessing.
5. **Credit and default where defaults can be seen.** 38.68's finding (relational credit appears only where the engine leaves a gap)
   is the strongest single argument for [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and sepa|L-106]]'s direction. Reputation economies need third parties to learn of defaults.
6. **Famine as entitlement failure.** The interesting famine is not "no food in the world" but "food in one household's store and
   a starving neighbour with nothing to trade" (38.70's Brockley). Prices that rise in dearth, labour whose demand falls, and stores
   that can be shared or hoarded give agents the real choice.
7. **Births, ageing and death with lags:** children who cost food for years before they work, adults whose prime ends, elders who
   need care. Marriage and household formation should be agents' choices with real economic weight (a new household needs a house,
   land and a store).
8. **Surplus that can be taken:** rent, tithe, levies, tolls. Agents should face the choice to extract, and the costs of extraction
   (monitoring, resistance, flight).

The rule, consistent with holding.md and [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and sepa|L-106]]: **the engine holds physics, presence, perception and records written by deliberate
acts; people make the institutions.**

| chokepoints | capital goods with real thresholds (mill against quern, oven against hearth, weir against net) | soke, tolls, monopolies, licences, evasion |

| state | institutions with members, offices, a store and a register (membership.md); levies as legs an institution's code creates (agreements presets) | taxes, public works, defence, coin, poor relief |
| risk | weather and harvest variance that clusters; disease; spoilage and pests in stores | storage strategy, insurance, charity, poor rates |
| population | birth, ageing, death, hunger, fertility as choices with lags | marriage norms, household formation, migration |

What this research suggests the engine should **not** do ([[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and sepa|L-106]] is not decided; each change would first be a world option read
against the null arm, as [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and sepa|L-106]] proposes):
- settle obligations automatically, which removes the need for enforcement and so the institutions that provide it;
- make every member of a household know every agreement, which removes the household as a site of negotiation;
- keep witnesses who never perceive, which removes the third party;
- impose a wage, a rent or a tax rate; these are what agents should bargain over.

### 8.3 What is beyond a village engine, and how to reach it

- **Scale:** factories, unions, national courts, the fiscal-military state and the welfare state need thousands to millions of
  people. Per 02zzi §6, those layers need **populations that are not agents** (households that eat, work, breed and pay by rule),
  with agents in the roles that decide: heads of households, lords, masters, merchants, officials, union leaders. Rule-driven
  populations should still respond to wages, prices and relief as the history says (Chayanov's drudgery balance, Malthusian
  marriage, Sen's entitlements), so that agents face a believable labour supply and tax base.
- **Time:** institutions take generations. The demographic transition and enclosure took centuries. Long runs, or a history run
  before agents arrive, are needed for these to exist at the start.
- **Records:** every step up the arc (court rolls, guild books, bonds, registers, tax lists, censuses) adds a record kept by an
  institution. Written documents (spec/information/letters_and_documents.md) and ledgers ([[D2-33 Ledgers|D2-33]]) are the engine's way in.

### 8.4 Experiments this suggests (to read against a null arm)

- **The harvest peak:** narrow and wide harvest windows; does a narrow one produce hired labour, boon work or labour swaps?
- **Mill soke:** a lord's mill with querns allowed against querns slow; does a monopoly rule appear, and is it evaded?
- **Visible defaults** (already suggested in 38.68): defaults perceived by witnesses as a sign; does a reputation economy form?
- **Dearth:** one bad harvest in a mixed village of landed and landless; who starves, and does a poor relief or a lord's dole appear?
- **Labour scarcity:** after a mortality shock (a Black Death arm), do the survivors' terms of work improve, or do holders of land try
  to bind labour (Domar)?
- **Marriage and land:** with houses and land scarce, do agents delay household formation?

Each should be read with the qualitative end-state reading the scenario guide asks for, not by a count alone.
