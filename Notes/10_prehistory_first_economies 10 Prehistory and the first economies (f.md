# 10: Prehistory and the first economies (from foragers to the first cities and palaces)

Part of the economy research folder (02zzj). It reads against `02zzi_economies_scaling.md` (§3.1 foragers, §3.2 villages, §3.3
redistribution, §3.9 the irrigation branch) and uses the chain shapes of `docs/cybertron2/proposals/economy_web.md` §1 (shallow,
accretive, paradigm switch, substitutes, joint products, assembly, chokepoint, long skill, time capital, pulse, commons,

## Summary

For more than 99% of the human story the economy was foraging: bands of twenty-five or thirty moving across territories, taking
wild food with tools of stone, wood, bone and fibre, and sharing most of what came in. That economy was not simple. It already
had long skill (knapping, tracking), chokepoints (good stone, ochre, water), time capital (traps, weirs, stored nuts and fish),
pulses (salmon runs, nut falls, migrating herds), commons (game, shellfish beds) and long-distance exchange of a few light,
valued goods. Ethnographic return rates show its logic: big game pays ten to thirty thousand kilocalories an hour once found,
small seeds a few hundred, so people take the best and work down the list only as the best runs short. The fall to seeds and
nuts after large game declined, together with storage and settling down, prepared the ground for farming. Farming itself did
not at first pay better per hour (Bowles 2011 puts early cereal cultivation at about 63% of the wild-food mean); it won because
it fed more people per hectare, could be stored and defended, and raised birth rates.

From that start the chains of the first economies formed and joined in a recognisable order. Domestication, in at least seven
centres between about 11,500 and 4,000 years ago, gave grain and herds; herds later yielded milk, wool, traction and transport
(Sherratt's secondary products, which now look piecemeal rather than a single revolution). Fire, once it could be made at will,
became a ladder of temperatures: cooking, heat-treated stone, lime, pottery kilns, and copper smelting by about 5000 BC.
Exchange networks for obsidian, flint, shell, amber and salt were in place before metal, and bronze turned them into a
chokepoint economy: copper and tin almost never occur together, so every bronze-using society depended on routes a thousand
kilometres or more long, carried by donkeys and ships and held together by partnerships, credit and treaties. The first cities
and states (Uruk, Egypt, the Indus, the Aegean palaces) were machines for gathering grain and wool, paying workers in rations,
and trading cloth for the timber, metal and stone their plains lacked; writing began in Mesopotamia as their bookkeeping. §6
gives the dependency trees: what each step needed and what it opened.

**How to read the numbers.** *ka* is thousands of years ago, *Ma* millions, *BP* before present; dates are calibrated.
**[firm]** means widely agreed; **[rough]** an estimate or order of magnitude; **[contested]** disputed. Where a number comes
from a secondary summary and was not checked against the original, the text says so. Sources are listed at the end.

## 1. Foraging economies

### 1.1 Stone tools and their raw materials

| Industry | From | What it is | Sourcing | What it enabled |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lomekwian** | ~3.3 Ma, Kenya **[contested as an industry]** | big cores struck on an anvil | local cobbles | pounding, crude cutting |
| **Oldowan** | ~2.6 Ma (Ledi-Geraru, Gona); perhaps 2.9 Ma (Nyayanga) | sharp flakes knocked off cobbles | mostly within a few km; at Nyayanga chosen cobbles carried **up to 13 km** (Science Advances 2025) **[firm]** | butchery of large carcasses; marrow and fat |
| **Acheulean** | ~1.76 to 0.2 Ma | the hand-axe: a planned shape worked on both faces | usually local (under ~20 km) but chosen for quality | an all-purpose tool, unchanged for a million years: shallow in form, high in skill |
| **Prepared core (Levallois), Middle Stone Age** | ~0.3 Ma | a core shaped so one blow gives a planned flake; points for hafting | at Olorgesailie, Kenya, obsidian from **25 to 95 km** ~300 ka (Brooks et al. 2018) **[firm]** | **hafting**: composite tools |
| **Blades, Upper Palaeolithic / Later Stone Age** | ~50 to 40 ka (earlier in Africa) | long thin blades from prismatic cores; burins for bone and antler | good flint moved 100 to 200 km and more **[rough]** | more edge per kilo; needles, harpoons, spear-thrower hooks |
| **Microliths** | sporadic from ~71 ka (Pinnacle Point); general after ~20 ka | tiny geometric pieces set in rows in a shaft | small nodules suffice | arrows, barbed spears, sickles; replaceable parts |
| **Ground stone** | grinding slabs from ~30 ka; polished axes with farming | shaped by pecking and grinding | tough rock from a few quarries, traded far | felling forest, milling seed |

**Shapes.** Knapping is **long skill** (years to master; failures waste stone) on a **chokepoint** (fine-grained stone is
patchy), and it is **accretive**: hafting, heat treatment, pressure flaking and microliths each added to what came before.
Microliths are a **substitute** that relaxed the chokepoint: poor small stone sufficed once the tool was a composite of many
parts. The distance stone travelled grew by orders of magnitude, from 13 km in the Oldowan to tens of km in the Middle Stone
Age, hundreds in the Upper Palaeolithic, and nearly two thousand with farming (§2.4). Early long moves were probably people
walking to an outcrop; later ones were exchange between groups.

### 1.2 Fire and cooking

| Step | When | Evidence | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| fire used | ~1.0 Ma **[contested]** | burnt bone and ash, Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa | warmth, protection |
| repeated hearths | ~790 ka | Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel | the hearth as a camp centre |
| habitual fire | ~400 to 300 ka | Qesem Cave; many European sites | cooking as normal |
| **making fire at will** | **~400 ka** | Barnham, Suffolk: a heated hearth and iron pyrite, foreign to the site, for striking sparks on flint (Davis et al., *Nature*, Dec. 2025); the previous oldest firm evidence was ~50 ka | fire no longer has to be carried or found |
| heat-treating stone | ~164 ka, Pinnacle Point | silcrete heated to ~350 °C flakes better | the first use of fire to change a material |
| adhesives | birch tar from ~200 ka (Neanderthals, Italy); gum-and-ochre glues ~70 ka, South Africa | residues on tools | hafting: stone, shaft and glue **assembled** from three chains |
| landscape burning | late Pleistocene and Holocene (Australian "firestick farming") | charcoal records, ethnography | fire on the land: fresh growth draws game; a managed **commons** |

**Cooking as an energy technology.** Wrangham (2009) argues that cooking raised the energy people get from the same food
(gelatinised starch, denatured protein, softened fibre, fewer pathogens), allowing smaller guts and larger brains; Carmody et al.
(2011) found mice gain more on cooked food of the same weight **[firm for the effect; its size in humans rough]**. Fire
multiplies food, and every hearth is a fitting that every other food chain passes through. Its cost is fuel: wood-gathering
grows longer the longer a camp stays, one push to move on.

**The pyrotechnology ladder.** Each rung needed the one below in fuel and furnace handling:

| Temperature | Process | First |
|---|---|---|
| ~350 °C | heat-treating stone | ~164 ka |
| 500 to 800 °C | open fire: roasting, drying, smoking | Palaeolithic |
| ~750 to 900 °C | lime for plaster (heavy fuel cost) | Levant, ~9000 BC |
| 600 to 1,000 °C | pottery, then kilns | ~20 ka (China) |
| ~900 °C | faience and glazes | 5th to 4th millennium BC |
| 1,085 to ~1,200 °C | **smelting copper**: closed furnace, charcoal, forced draught | ~5000 BC |
| 1,150 to 1,300 °C | iron bloomery | ~1200 BC (outside this file) |

### 1.3 Hunting technologies

| Technology | Earliest firm evidence | Effect | Inputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| scavenging, clubs, stones | >2 Ma | carcasses taken from predators | stone |
| **wooden spears** | Schöningen, Germany: eight or more spears, long dated ~300 ka, re-dated in 2025 to ~200 ka **[contested date]**; Clacton tip ~400 ka | thrust, or thrown 10 to 20 m | straight young trees; a stone tool |
| **stone-tipped spears** | ~500 ka, Kathu Pan **[contested]**; common by ~300 ka | deeper wounds | stone, shaft, glue, binding: **assembly** |
| **spear-thrower (atlatl)** | firm finds ~20 to 17 ka (Solutrean and Magdalenian France); probably older **[contested]** | darts to 30 to 50 m with more force | antler hook, light darts |
| **bow and arrow** | Sibudu, South Africa, bone point ~61.7 ka **[contested]**; Grotte Mandrin, France, tiny points with bow-shot damage, ~54 ka (Metz et al. 2023) | accurate at 20 to 50 m; one person can hunt alone | stave, **cord**, feathers, small points, glue: five chains |
| **nets** | cord impressions in clay at Pavlov and Dolní Věstonice ~29 to 26 ka; a Neanderthal three-ply cord, Abri du Maras, ~50 to 40 ka; cordage at Ohalo II, 19.4 ka | communal drives of hares and small game; fish | cord in quantity |
| **traps and snares** | inferred in the Upper Palaeolithic; wooden fish traps from the Mesolithic (~7000 to 5000 BC) | gear works while people do other things: **time capital** | cord, withies, stakes |
| **drives and kills** | buffalo jumps (Head-Smashed-In, used ~5,800 years); Near Eastern "desert kites", stone funnels kilometres long, many ~9,000 to 5,000 BP | hundreds of animals in a day: a **pulse** | crowds, leaders, years of building; meat processed at once |
| **dogs** | well before 15 ka **[contested in detail]** | tracking, holding game at bay | food for the dogs |

The bow made hunting a one-person task and brought small fast prey (birds, rabbits) into reach. Nets and drives went the other
way: they made hunting a crowd's work, the first occasions where a group had to be organised, led and fed in one place.

### 1.4 Fishing and shellfish

| Step | When and where |
|---|---|
| shellfish as a staple | Pinnacle Point, South Africa, ~164 ka (Marean et al. 2007) **[firm]**: predictable, low risk, gathered by anyone |
| barbed bone points | Katanda, DR Congo, ~90 ka **[contested date]** |
| open-sea fishing | Jerimalai, East Timor: tuna ~42 ka; shell hook ~23 to 16 ka (O'Connor et al. 2011) |
| shell fish-hooks | Sakitari Cave, Okinawa, ~23 ka (Fujita et al. 2016) |
| weirs and fixed traps | Mesolithic Europe and the Holocene everywhere: **time capital** and a **commons** downstream |
| great shell middens | Holocene coasts (Jōmon, Ertebølle, California, Brazil's sambaquis) |
| salmon economies | North-west coast of North America, ~5,000 BP on: plank-house villages, ranked chiefs, slaves, potlatch |

Fish and shellfish are the forager resources most like crops: **dense, predictable, seasonal**. Where rich, foragers settled,
stored large surpluses and developed property in fishing stations, specialists and rank without farming. These "complex
hunter-gatherers" show that **storage, not farming, opened the door to wealth and rank** (Testart 1982).

### 1.5 Food storage

| Way | Where and when |
|---|---|
| caching meat in cold ground | Upper Palaeolithic Eurasia; Arctic ethnography |
| pits beside mammoth-bone houses | Mezhyrich, Ukraine, ~15 ka |
| drying, smoking, rendering, pemmican | everywhere: turns a **pulse** (a kill, a run) into months of food |
| stored nuts | California acorns (leaching tannins takes hours per kilo), Jōmon chestnut pits, Mesolithic hazelnut roasting |
| **granaries for wild grain** | Dhra', Jordan, ~11,300 cal BP: raised floors against damp and rodents, between houses, perhaps shared; **about a thousand years before domestic cereals** (Kuijt and Finlayson 2009) **[firm]** |

Woodburn's (1982) distinction is the key one. **Immediate-return** foragers (Hadza, !Kung) eat within days what they get, own
little and level hard. **Delayed-return** foragers (anyone with stores, traps, boats or weirs) hold assets that must be guarded
and inherited, so they develop property, binding kin rules and often rank. Storage turns sharing from a norm about food into a
question of who owns the store.

### 1.6 Sharing and exchange norms

| Norm | How it works | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **demand sharing** (Peterson 1993) | food is shared because others ask; refusing shames | spreads big, chancy kills |
| **tolerated theft** (Blurton Jones 1984) | defending a kill costs more than letting others take some | meat flows without a rule |
| **meat pooled, plant food kept** | among the Ache most of a hunter's kill is eaten outside his family (Kaplan and Hill 1985) | **risk pooling**: the band is an insurance pool |
| **show-off hunting** (Hawkes 1991) | men target big game for prestige even when small game pays better | status as a currency |
| **hxaro** partnerships (Ju/'hoansi San) | delayed gifts (beads, arrows) to on average **16 partners** (range 2 to 42) within **~200 km**, ~24 in mid-life, ~12 in old age; in drought a family lives with a distant partner for months (Wiessner 1977, 1982) **[firm]** | a regional safety net; goods move far as a side effect |
| **Aboriginal exchange routes** | ochre (Wilgie Mia, Parachilna), pituri, greenstone axes from Mount William, Victoria, carried hundreds of km along kin and ceremonial routes **[distances rough]** | quarries owned by one group; goods valued for distance |
| **Sahlins's spectrum** (1972) | generalised (no accounts, close kin), balanced (equivalents, neighbours), negative (haggling, theft, strangers) | the kind of exchange follows social distance |

Forager sharing is insurance with known rules, and it weakens once food can be stored or a household's returns become
predictable. The first goods moved far were **light, durable, valued and hard to get**: pierced Nassarius shell beads (~100 to
75 ka, Skhul and Blombos), ochre, fine stone, ostrich-eggshell beads moved ~100 km and more in southern Africa from ~33 ka
(Stewart et al. 2020).

### 1.7 Band size and territory

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| residential band | **~25 to 30 people** (Birdsell's "magic number 25"; Marlowe 2005) **[firm]** |
| regional group (dialect, marriage pool) | **~500** **[rough]** |
| nested levels | families ~4 to 5 → camps ~15 → bands ~50 → ~150 → ~500 → ~1,500, each level ~3 to 4 times the one below (Hamilton et al. 2007) **[rough]** |
| density | ~0.01 people/km² (deserts, Arctic) to over 1/km² (rich coasts) (Binford 2001; Kelly 2013) |
| band territory | under 100 km² (rich coasts) to over 10,000 km² (deserts) **[rough]** |
| moves a year | a few (coastal, stored food) to 30 or more (arid zones) |
| work | !Kung of Dobe: ~12 to 19 hours a week getting food; ~40 to 45 hours counting tool-making and housework (Lee 1979) **[firm; the "original affluent society" reading (Sahlins 1972) contested]** |

A band is too small to breed alone (it needs spouses from the regional group) and too small to insure itself. **Exchange began
as a way to get spouses and insurance, not goods.**

### 1.8 Foraging returns in numbers

Optimal foraging theory's **diet-breadth model** ranks resources by **post-encounter return** (calories per hour of pursuit and
processing once found). A forager takes a resource if it beats the average rate of searching for better ones; as top resources
become scarce the average falls and lower ones join the diet.

**Great Basin, USA, experimental returns** (Simms 1985, 1987, as tabulated in Kelly 1995/2013; pinyon confirmed in Simms's
pinyon study, other values quoted from Kelly's table as commonly reproduced) **[rough: wide experimental ranges]**:

| Resource | kcal/hour |
|---|---|
| deer, bighorn sheep, pronghorn | ~16,000 to 31,000 |
| jackrabbit | ~13,000 to 16,000 |
| cottontail | ~9,000 to 10,000 |
| ground squirrel | ~5,000 to 6,000 |
| cattail pollen (seasonal) | ~2,700 to 9,400 |
| ducks | ~2,000 to 2,700 |
| pinyon nuts | ~850 to 1,500 |
| shadscale seed | ~1,200 |
| grass and mustard seeds (ricegrass, bluegrass, tansy mustard, sunflower) | ~300 to 1,300 |
| cattail roots | ~130 to 270 |

**Ache, Paraguay, observed** (Hawkes, Hill and O'Connell 1982; Hill et al. 1987) **[firm; depends on how tracking time is
counted]**: white-lipped peccary ~5,300 kcal/hour with tracking, ~8,800 after encounter; feral oranges ~4,400; honey ~3,200;
palm larvae ~1,800; palm starch ~1,000; men's hunting overall ~750; mean of all foraging ~1,340 (women ~1,220). The model
predicts the Ache ignore anything returning under ~870 kcal/hour, however often met. A rough survival threshold is **~370
kcal per hour of foraging** (from Kelly's and Binford's data).

Three things carry over to any model:
1. **The spread is two orders of magnitude.** Big game beats small seeds fifty to a hundred times per hour of handling. People
   eat seeds because big game is found rarely, and grows rarer under pressure.
2. **Seeds are low-ranked because of processing, not gathering.** Threshing, winnowing, parching and grinding are slow; grinding
   stones, baskets, pots and querns are tools that raise a low-ranked resource's rate. Jack Harlan (1967) cut wild einkorn in
   Turkey with a flint sickle and argued a family could gather in about three weeks more grain than it could eat in a year
   (often summarised as ~1 kg of clean grain an hour) **[widely quoted; not re-checked against Harlan here]**.
3. **The broad spectrum revolution** (Flannery 1969; Stiner 2001): after ~20 ka in the Near East and Europe diets widened from
   large game to small fast animals, fish, shellfish and seeds, as the model predicts under growing numbers. Cultivation began
   from that base.

**Foraging against early farming.** Bowles (2011), from 15 cultivars like the first crops and five foraging peoples, estimated
early cereal cultivation at **about 63% of the wild-food mean in calories per hour**, storage and processing included **[rough;
large uncertainties, as the author says]**. Farming spread because it paid more per hectare, could be stored, and came with
private property in fields and stores that households could defend (Bowles and Choi 2019).

## 2. The Neolithic package

### 2.1 Centres of domestication

Domestication was slow. In the Near East non-shattering wheat and barley took **2,000 to 3,000 years** to dominate after
cultivation began (Tanno and Willcox 2006; Fuller 2007), against experiments suggesting 20 to 200 years under strong selection
(Hillman and Davies 1990).

| Centre | Plants | Domestic forms from about | Animals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fertile Crescent** | einkorn, emmer, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch, flax | ~10,500 to 9,500 BP **[firm]** | goat, sheep, cattle, pig (~10,500 BP) |
| **North China** | foxtail and broomcorn millet | ~10,000 to 8,000 BP | pig, dog |
| **South China** (Yangzi) | rice | cultivation ~9,000 BP; domestic forms dominant ~7,000 to 6,000 BP **[contested pace]** | pig, later water buffalo |
| **New Guinea** (Kuk) | taro, banana, yam | drained mounds ~7,000 to 6,500 BP; perhaps from ~10,000 BP **[contested]** | none until later |
| **Mesoamerica** | squash (~10,000 BP), maize from teosinte (~9,000 BP), beans, chili | maize cobs grow over millennia | turkey, dog |
| **Andes and Amazonia** | potato, quinoa; manioc, peanut, squash, cotton | ~10,000 to 5,000 BP | llama, alpaca (~6,000 BP), guinea pig |
| **Eastern North America** | squash, sunflower, goosefoot, marsh elder | ~5,000 to 4,000 BP | |
| **Sahel and West Africa** | pearl millet (~4,500 BP), sorghum, African rice, cowpea, yams | **herding came before crops** | cattle (Saharan herding ~8,000 BP), donkey |
| **South Asia** | millets, mung bean, indica rice, cotton (Mehrgarh ~7,000 BP, earliest known) | | zebu (~8,000 BP) |

Farming spread into Europe at about **1 km a year** on average (Ammerman and Cavalli-Sforza 1971) **[firm in outline]**, carried
mainly by migrating farmers, as ancient DNA has since confirmed.

| chicken | ~1650 to 1250 BC, Ban Non Wat, Thailand, tied to rice farming (Peters et al. 2022) | Southeast Asia | eggs, meat |

### 2.3 The secondary products revolution

Andrew Sherratt (1981, 1983) argued that for three or four thousand years herds were kept for meat, and that a **package of
secondary products** (milk, wool, the plough, the cart, riding) arrived together in the 4th millennium BC, changing land use
(heavier soils opened by the plough), the division of labour (ploughing as men's work) and wealth (herds as inheritable capital).

| Product | Sherratt's date | Evidence now | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **milk** | 4th millennium BC | milk fats in pots from **~7000 BC**, north-west Anatolia (Evershed et al. 2008, >2,200 vessels); farmers at Barcın Höyük, 6600 to 6000 BC, arrive already dairying (2024) | **far earlier**: dairying is nearly as old as herding |
| **lactase persistence** | | spread in Europe mostly in the last ~3,000 years (Evershed et al. 2022) | milk was first eaten as cheese, yogurt, butter (little lactose) |
| **wool** | 4th millennium BC | woolly fleeces with selection; big wool flocks in 4th-millennium Mesopotamian texts | **about right**, gradual |
| **traction** | 4th millennium BC | ard marks under a long barrow at South Street, England, ~3500 BC; draught pathologies in cattle earlier in places | **about right**, earlier starts |
| **wheel and cart** | 4th millennium BC | Bronocice pot (Poland) cart image ~3500 BC; Ljubljana marsh wheel ~3150 BC | **right**: within a few centuries in several regions |
| **riding** | 4th millennium BC | firm riding and chariots late 3rd to early 2nd millennium BC | **later** |

The beast became a bundle of **joint products** (meat, milk, hide, dung, fibre, power) taken over its life rather than at one
killing: **time capital**. A milked and worked herd must be kept alive through winter, so fodder, folds and inheritance follow.
Traction was an **increasing-returns** threshold: an ox team paid only on enough land, which concentrated land in families that
owned teams. Wool made a commodity gathered from many herders, spun by many hands, stored and carried far: what the first
cities ran on (§5).

### 2.4 Pottery, weaving and ground stone

**Pottery** was invented by foragers several times: Xianrendong Cave, China, **~20,000 cal BP** (Wu et al. 2012) **[firm]**;
Jōmon Japan ~16,000 BP; Ounjougou, Mali, ~11,800 BP. In the Near East it came **after** farming, ~7000 BC, following two thousand
years of plaster and stone vessels. Pottery is a **paradigm switch for cooking**: boiling (porridge, soup, fat and bone grease)
extracts more food from low-ranked resources, and a pot stores grain, oil and milk against pests. Clay is everywhere but good
clay, temper and fuel are local and potting is long skill; it becomes a specialist trade (the wheel, ~4th millennium BC in
Mesopotamia) once demand is concentrated.

**Fibre and weaving.** Cord is Palaeolithic (§1.3); possible wild flax fibres at Dzudzuana Cave, Georgia, ~30 ka **[contested]**;
woven linen at Nahal Hemar, Israel, ~8,500 BP; textiles at Çatalhöyük ~7000 BC; spindle whorls and warp-weighted looms with the
Neolithic. **Spinning is the bottleneck**: with a hand spindle a spinner makes roughly 50 to 100 m of yarn an hour, and a square
metre of cloth needs one to several kilometres, so a garment takes **hundreds of hours** **[rough; experimental archaeology,
e.g. Copenhagen's Centre for Textile Research]**. Cloth was among the most labour-dense goods of the ancient world, hence a
store of value and the first cities' main export.

**Ground stone.** Querns with farming (rotary querns only ~5th century BC). Polished axes for clearing woodland fell a medium
tree in hours, many times slower than steel **[rough]**. Axes became exchange goods: **Alpine jadeitite axes** from Monte Viso
and Monte Beigua, 5300 to 4000 BC, reached Brittany, Britain, Ireland and Denmark, up to **~1,800 km** away (Projet JADE,
Pétrequin et al. 2012) **[firm]**; many were never used, and were laid in bogs and graves.

### 2.5 Sedentism

| Site | When | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Ohalo II, Sea of Galilee | 23,000 BP | brush huts; wild barley ground on stone; cord |
| Natufian villages | ~15,000 to 11,700 BP | **settled foragers**: stone houses, cemeteries, sickles glossed from cutting grass, mortars |
| Dhra', Jordan | ~11,300 cal BP | granaries before domestication |
| Göbekli Tepe, Turkey | ~9600 to 8200 BC | monumental pillars raised by people still largely foraging; feasting on a large scale (wild cereal beer **[contested]**) |
| Çatalhöyük, Turkey | 7100 to 5950 BC | dense mud-brick town, **3,500 to 8,000 people** **[rough]**; each household its own store; no visible central authority; obsidian from 140 km |

**What it cost.** Early farming skeletons show shorter stature, more caries, more anaemia and infection than late foragers
(Larsen 1995) **[firm in general, varies]**. Births rose: in the **Neolithic demographic transition** (Bocquet-Appel 2011) birth
intervals fall from ~4 years among mobile foragers to ~2 to 3 among farmers **[firm in outline]**. More people, worse health:
the Malthusian trade-off at its origin.

### 2.6 Labour and yields

| Quantity | Figure |
|---|---|
| early cultivation per hour | ~63% of the wild-food mean (Bowles 2011) **[rough]** |
| Neolithic cereal yields | of the order of **1 to 2 t/ha** on intensive, manured garden plots **[rough; Bogaard 2004's models]** |
| manuring | stable isotopes show **manuring from the start** among Europe's first farmers (Bogaard et al. 2013) **[firm]** |
| manure needed | ~30 to 100 t/ha for intensive manuring **[rough]**: grain needs dung, dung needs beasts, beasts need fodder |
| land | perhaps 0.5 to 1 ha of cereal per head **[rough]** |
| for comparison | medieval English wheat ~0.5 to 0.7 t/ha |

A cereal field yields many times the edible energy of wild land per hectare, so farmers reached ten to a hundred times
foragers' density, and outnumbered and displaced them (clear in European ancient DNA), whatever the return per hour.

## 3. Early specialisation and trade

### 3.1 The goods

| Good | Sources | Earliest long moves | Distances | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **obsidian** | a few volcanic fields: central and eastern Anatolia, Melos, Lipari, Sardinia | Melian obsidian in Franchthi Cave, Greece, ~11,000 BC: the earliest proof of sea travel for a material in the Mediterranean **[firm]** | central Anatolian obsidian **700 to 800 km** into the southern Levant | **chokepoint** |
| **mined flint** | chalk and limestone seams | Spiennes, Belgium, ~4400 BC; Krzemionki, Poland, ~3900 to 1600 BC (thousands of shafts); Grimes Graves from ~2650 BC | tens to hundreds of km | threshold work; quality chokepoint |
| **shell** | Spondylus (Aegean, Adriatic); cowries; Turbinella (Gujarat) | Spondylus ornaments in central European Linearbandkeramik, ~5500 to 5000 BC | up to **~1,500 km** | status good |
| **amber** | Baltic coast | Baltic amber in Mycenae's shaft graves, ~1600 BC | **~2,000 km** | status good, river routes |
| **salt** | brine springs, rock salt, sea | Poiana Slatinei, Romania, ~6000 BC; Provadia-Solnitsata, Bulgaria, 5600 to 4350 BC; Hallstatt rock-salt mines from ~1500 BC | regional, tens to hundreds of km | **chokepoint** for keeping and herds |
| **lapis lazuli** | Badakhshan, Afghanistan | Mesopotamia and Egypt from the 4th millennium BC | **~2,500 km** | status good |
| **carnelian** | Gujarat | Indus beads in Ur's Royal Cemetery, ~2600 BC | ~2,500 km by sea | craft product |
| **nephrite** | Fengtian, Taiwan | across Southeast Asia from ~3000 BC | ~3,000 km | status good |

### 3.2 Grimes Graves

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| shafts | **at least 433** **[firm]** |
| depth | up to ~12 to 14 m, to the best seam ("floorstone") |
| dates | deep galleried shafts from about the third quarter of the 27th century BC (Healy et al. 2018); intermittent mining to ~1400 BC |
| per medium shaft | **~60 t of flint nodules** (the figure most sources repeat) **[rough]** |
| site total | **~16,000 to 18,000 t** by extrapolation **[rough; method not found]** |
| tools | red-deer antler picks, hundreds per shaft: a **joint product** of herds and hunting |

A shaft was a crew's work for a season, moving hundreds of tonnes of chalk with antler picks **[rough]**: a **threshold** work,
with labour fed months ahead of any return. The first industrial sites in Europe were mines of a good that had substitutes (local
flint) but no equivalents (large, flawless nodules for prestige axes).

### 3.3 Obsidian, and how networks are read

Renfrew, Dixon and Cann (1964 to 1968) traced Near Eastern obsidian to source by trace elements and found:
- a **supply zone** near each source (roughly 300 km, as usually quoted **[rough]**) where obsidian is most of the chipped stone;
- a **contact zone** beyond, where its share falls **exponentially with distance**;
- read as **down-the-line exchange**: each community keeps some and passes the rest on, and no one walks the whole route.

Later work adds **directional trade**: some southern Levantine sites 700 to 800 km away hold more obsidian than down-the-line
passing could deliver. Sources also shift over time (central Anatolian early, eastern Anatolian in the Pottery Neolithic); Tell
Mozan in Syria took 97% of its obsidian from eastern Anatolia (Frahm 2014).

| Network | How goods move | Signature | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **down-the-line** | hand to hand between neighbours | smooth exponential fall-off | most Neolithic obsidian |
| **directional** | carried by specialists to a central place | peaks far from source | some Levantine obsidian; Uruk colonies (§5.1) |
| **prestige chains** | gifts between leaders, held not used | rich graves and hoards far away | jade axes, amber, Spondylus |

### 3.4 Salt

Humans and herbivores need sodium, and salt is the main means of keeping meat, fish and later cheese; grain-eating farmers need
added salt more than meat-eating foragers **[firm in outline]**. At **Provadia-Solnitsata** brine was boiled in ceramic vessels in
domed kilns (briquetage). Output rose from ~25 kg of dry salt a load around 5500 BC to perhaps **4 to 5 t** a cycle by 4700 to
4500 BC **[rough; reports differ]**, and the site grew a stone wall. Some 40 km away lies the Varna cemetery, the richest
gold-bearing burial ground of its age (§4.1): salt wealth and metal wealth in one coastal region. Salt-making joins the **fuel**
chain (boiling) and the **clay** chain (vessels).

## 4. The first metals

### 4.1 From native copper to smelting

| Step | When and where |
|---|---|
| **cold-hammered native copper**, malachite beads | Anatolia (Çayönü), ~9th to 8th millennium BC; the Old Copper Complex by Lake Superior, ~4000 to 1000 BC, which never went on to smelting |
| **smelting** | **Belovode, Serbia, ~5000 BC** (49th century BC; ~5 g of slag, the earliest secure smelting known) (Radivojević et al. 2010) **[firm]**; Tal-i Iblis, Iran, 5th millennium BC |
| **mines** | Rudna Glava, Serbia, ~5400 BC; Ai Bunar, Bulgaria, ~5000 BC, open trenches tens of metres long |
| **metal as rank** | Varna, Bulgaria, ~4600 to 4300 BC: ~3,000 gold objects, ~6 kg in all, most in a few graves **[rough]**: the first sharp inequality in Europe's graves |
| **lost-wax casting, arsenical copper** | Nahal Mishmar hoard, Judaean desert, ~3700 BC: over 400 objects |
| **the Iceman's axe** | ~3300 BC, Alps: 99.7% copper from Tuscan ore (Artioli et al. 2017) |

Smelting needs ore, **charcoal** (a fuel chain), a furnace or crucible at ~1,100 to 1,200 °C (pottery skills), and **forced
draught** (blowpipes, later bellows of leather). **Arsenical copper** (2 to 5% arsenic, from mixed ores, perhaps first by
accident) is harder than pure copper and casts better; it dominated the 4th and early 3rd millennia.

### 4.2 Bronze and the tin trade

Tin bronze (~10% tin) is harder, casts well and follows a recipe. The problem: **tin and copper rarely lie near each other**, and
the Near East has almost no tin.

| Tin source | Status |
|---|---|
| central Anatolia (**Kestel** mine, **Göltepe** smelting village; Yener) | small, low-grade, 3rd millennium BC **[contested scale]** |
| Central Asia (**Mushiston**, Tajikistan; **Karnab**, Uzbekistan) | Bronze Age mines; part of Uluburun's tin matched here (2022 isotope study) **[firm for mining]** |
| Iran and Afghanistan (**Deh Hosein** in western Iran; the Herat region) | the likely origin of the tin reaching Assur from the east **[rough]** |
| **Erzgebirge** (Saxony, Bohemia) | tin isotopes point to it for Central European bronzes (Powell et al.) |
| **Cornwall and Devon** | ingots from wrecks off Israel and southern France matched to south-west Britain (Williams et al. 2025) **[contested but growing]** |
| Brittany, Iberia | placer tin on Atlantic routes **[rough]** |
| Southeast Asia (Thailand, Yunnan) | bronze from the 2nd millennium BC |

Tin bronze is common in Mesopotamia by ~2600 BC (Ur's Royal Cemetery); full tin bronze reached central Europe and Scandinavia
by ~1800 BC and the Aegean and Egypt by ~1500 BC **[firm in outline]**.

**The Old Assyrian trade (~1950 to 1750 BC)**, from ~23,000 letters and contracts in the merchants' quarter (*kārum*) of
**Kanesh** (Kültepe), central Anatolia:
- **Route:** Assur to Kanesh, ~1,000 km, by black donkey caravans, about six weeks.
- **Out:** **tin** bought in Assur from the east, and **woollen textiles** (some woven by merchants' wives in Assur, some
  Babylonian). **Back:** **silver and gold**, which were money.
- **Loads and volume:** a donkey carried ~130 minas (~65 kg) of tin with about four textiles packed round it **[rough]**; a
  working estimate of **~1,000 donkey loads a year** in the busiest decades, a third tin and two thirds cloth, with estimates
  ranging from a few hundred to several thousand; **~100,000 textiles over thirty years** in one sample-based estimate **[rough;
  contested]**.
- **Margins:** roughly 100% on tin, more on textiles, before costs and taxes **[rough]**.
- **Institutions:** the ***naruqqum*** ("bag"), a ten- to twelve-year investment fund pooling gold from a dozen or more
  investors into one merchant's hands for a share of profits; family firms with agents abroad; a merchants' court; **treaties**
  with Anatolian rulers fixing taxes (a share of cloth at entry, a right of first purchase) and guaranteeing caravans. Long-distance
  credit, partnerships and merchant law in the early 2nd millennium BC (Larsen 2015; Veenhof).

**The Uluburun ship (~1320 BC)**, wrecked off south-west Turkey (Pulak 2008): **~10 t of Cypriot copper** in about 350 oxhide
ingots (counts of 348 and 354 are given) plus bun ingots; **~1 t of tin**, the 10:1 ratio of bronze, as if the ship carried a
recipe; ~175 glass ingots; ebony, ivory, resin, Baltic amber; goods of half a dozen cultures.

**Specialists.** Smelting was done near ore and fuel, casting and finishing near the buyer. Hoards of scrap and moulds point to
recycling (bronze can be remelted, so metal circulated as a stock) and to smiths who travelled **[contested: itinerant, or attached
to elites]**.

### 4.3 The Bronze Age palace economies (Linear B)

The Mycenaean palaces (Knossos ~1400 BC; Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes to ~1200 BC) kept accounts in **Linear B**, an early Greek
(deciphered by Ventris, 1952). The tablets survive because the palaces burned; they cover each palace's last months.

| Chain | What the tablets show |
|---|---|
| **wool** | Knossos tracks flocks by place and shepherd, with a wool target per flock and the "deficit" owed: **at least ~80,000 sheep**, perhaps ~100,000, mostly wethers kept for wool **[rough; Killen]**; a wool unit of ~3 kg, about four sheep a unit **[rough]** |
| **textiles** | women workers in named groups (by place, some captives from the east Aegean) on monthly rations of wheat and figs; Pylos lists ~750 women with ~1,000 children **[rough]** |
| **bronze** | the Jn series lists **bronzesmiths** in some 25 places, each group with or without an allocation (***ta-ra-si-ja***) of metal, ~0.75 to ~4 kg per group on single tablets; ~270 smiths **[rough]**, many "without allocation": a sign of metal shortage |
| **ta-ra-si-ja** | the palace issues raw material and takes back finished goods, the worker working at home: a **putting-out system** three thousand years before proto-industry (02zzi §3.6) |
| **luxuries, war, feasts** | perfumed oil, chariots, furniture, banquets |

The palaces were **selective redistributors**: they tracked a few high-value chains closely and left most farming and exchange to
villages that appear only when they owe something (Halstead 1992). They depended on imported copper, tin, ivory, gold and glass,
and on dependent labour. Around **1200 to 1150 BC** the eastern Mediterranean system (Mycenaean palaces, the Hittites, Ugarit)
collapsed within decades and Greece lost writing for four centuries. Causes are debated (drought, earthquakes, raiders, revolt,
broken routes), but one reading fits the supply chains: **an economy built on several long, interlocking chokepoints is brittle
when they fail together** (Cline 2014) **[contested causes]**.

## 5. The first cities and states

### 5.1 Uruk and Mesopotamia

**Setting.** Flat alluvium between the Tigris and Euphrates: rich soil, too little rain to farm without irrigation, and **no
stone, no metal, no good timber**. Floods come in spring, before harvest, and rivers shift. Marshes rich in fish and reeds;
date palms; sheep and goats on the steppe edges.

| Phase | When | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Ubaid | ~6500 to 4000 BC | villages and towns with temples; small canals off river levees |
| **Uruk** | ~4000 to 3100 BC | Uruk reaches ~250 ha, and perhaps **~600 ha with 40,000 to 50,000 people by ~2900 BC** **[rough; contested]**; the Eanna temple precinct; **writing**; colonies upriver |
| Early Dynastic | ~2900 to 2350 BC | city-states (Ur, Lagash, Umma, Kish); wars over canals and fields |
| Akkad | ~2350 to 2150 BC | the first territorial empire |
| **Ur III** | ~2112 to 2004 BC | a centralised state with the densest record of the ancient world (~100,000 tablets) |

**Great households.** The core institutions were temples and palaces (*e2*, "house") that owned land, herds and workshops and fed
dependants in **rations** (Gelb 1965). Many families farmed their own land too; the great households were not the whole economy,
but they are nearly all the records.

**Ur III rations, barley a month** (granary records) **[firm]**: adult man (*guruš*) **60 sila** (~60 litres, ~1.3 kg a day);
woman **30 sila**, sometimes 40 to 60 for valued work; children 10 to 25; plus oil, a few minas of wool a year, sometimes beer,
fish and dates. At ~3,400 kcal/kg, 60 litres (~38 kg) is ~4,000 kcal a day: a worker's needs and some to share **[rough
arithmetic]**.

**Bevelled-rim bowls**: crude, mould-made bowls of near-standard size, found by the hundreds of thousands from Iran to Syria and
often thrown away after one use. The usual reading is a daily ration of grain or bread **[contested: also bread moulds, yogurt]**;
if so, they mark **labour paid in standard measures by an institution**.

**Writing as accounting.**
- **Tokens** in simple shapes from ~8000 BC, sealed in clay envelopes (*bullae*) in the 4th millennium BC, then impressed on
  them: Schmandt-Besserat's theory of writing's origin **[contested in strong form; bullae and numerical tablets firm]**.
- **Numerical tablets** (~3500 BC): numbers and a seal.
- **Proto-cuneiform** (Uruk IV, ~3350 to 3200 BC): ~5,000 to 6,000 archaic tablets from Uruk, the great majority
  **administrative** (grain, beer, bread, sheep, workers, fields, labour), the rest lexical lists for training scribes (Nissen,
  Damerow and Englund 1993) **[firm in outline; ~85 to 90% often quoted]**.
- **A different number system for each kind of good**: discrete objects, grain rations, grain capacities, areas, time. Counting
  was tied to the commodity before it was abstract.
- Writing let institutions plan a year's rations against a harvest, carry debts and deficits over years, hold a worker to a
  quota and audit an official: **the information good that let a redistributive household scale to thousands**.

**Textiles, the export.** Wool from the households' flocks, spun and woven by dependent women. In Lagash province in Šulgi's 48th
year (Ur III) one text records **over 10,000 people in the weaving establishments, about two-thirds women weavers** (Waetzoldt,
via secondary summary) **[rough]**; Guabba, a coastal town, supplied the largest group (one summary gives ~4,300 women and ~1,800
children **[unverified]**).

**Irrigation and yields.** Canals were dug and cleaned by labour gangs; Lagash and Umma fought over a canal and fields for
generations (the Stele of the Vultures, ~2450 BC). Early field texts have been read as giving very high seed-to-yield ratios
(20:1 and more) **[contested readings]**; at Larsa around 1700 BC yields averaged ~**900 litres per hectare** (Jacobsen and Adams
1958; Jacobsen 1982). They argued irrigation without drainage salted the fields, pushing a shift from wheat to salt-tolerant barley
and a fall in yields; **Powell (1985)** disputed the readings and argued barley was preferred on its merits and that leaching and
fallow kept salt in check **[contested]**. Either way, intensification can degrade its own base.

**Imports.**

| Lacked | From | Route |
|---|---|---|
| timber | Lebanon, Amanus, Zagros | rafts downriver |
| copper | **Magan** (Oman), Anatolia | the Gulf; the Euphrates |
| tin | Iran, Afghanistan, Central Asia | overland via Elam |
| stone (diorite, limestone) | Magan, the Zagros | sea, rivers |
| lapis, carnelian, gold, ivory | Badakhshan; **Meluhha** (the Indus); **Dilmun** (Bahrain) as entrepôt | the Gulf |

**The Uruk expansion** (~3500 to 3100 BC): Uruk-style settlements planted far upriver (Habuba Kabira, Jebel Aruda, Hacınebi),
read by Algaze (1993) as a **world system** in which the alluvium's cities traded cloth and manufactures for the periphery's
metal, timber and stone through colonies on the routes **[contested as a model; the colonies are firm]**.

### 5.2 Egypt: the Nile and redistribution

**Setting.** A narrow floodplain in desert; a river that floods **every year at a predictable time** (late summer, from Ethiopian
monsoon rain) and leaves new silt. **Basin irrigation**: the flood filled earth-banked basins, soaked them and drained; one winter
crop (emmer, barley, flax) was sown in the mud. Little canal-cleaning, little salt. The risk was a low or a too-high flood, which
the state measured with **nilometers** to set taxes. Old Kingdom Egypt had perhaps **1 to 1.5 million people** (Butzer 1976)
**[rough]** along ~1,000 km of river, and **the river was the road**: grain moved by boat cheaply from any field to any granary.

**Redistribution.** Taxes in grain, flax and animals flowed to royal and temple granaries; out came **bread and beer** for
workers, officials and priests.

| Case | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| **Giza pyramid builders** (~2550 BC) | a workers' town (Heit el-Ghurab) with bakeries, breweries and fish-processing; perhaps **8,000 to 10,000** workers at a time **[rough]**; one estimate of food per ten days for 10,000 workers: ~3,800 kg barley, ~3,900 kg emmer, 10 cattle, 36 sheep and goats, ~1,700 kg fish (AERA) **[rough]**; cattle came in from Delta estates |
| **Merer's diary** (Wadi al-Jarf papyri, ~2560 BC) | the log of an inspector whose crew of ~40 shipped limestone from the Tura quarries to Giza along flood canals: the oldest papyrus known is a work log |
| **Deir el-Medina** (New Kingdom) | a royal tomb-builder's monthly wage of **4 khar of emmer and 1.5 khar of barley** (a khar is ~76 litres, so ~420 litres: enough for a household and surplus to trade) **[khar counts firm; litres rough]**, plus fish, vegetables, oil, clothing |
| **the first recorded strike** | year 29 of Ramesses III (~1157 BC): the Deir el-Medina crew stopped work and sat in at temples when rations were weeks late (Turin Strike Papyrus) **[firm]** |

**Exchange outside the state.** Village barter ran throughout, priced in units of account: in the New Kingdom the **deben** (~91 g
of copper) and the sack of grain priced goods paid in a mix of other goods (Janssen 1975) **[firm]**. **Money as a measure of value
came long before coin.** Imports came mostly by **royal expedition**: cedar from **Byblos**, copper and turquoise from **Sinai**,
gold from Nubia, incense and ebony from **Punt** by Red Sea ships.

### 5.3 The Indus civilisation

**Setting and scale.** The Mature Harappan phase (~2600 to 1900 BC) along the Indus and the now-dry Ghaggar-Hakra, over
~800,000 km², the largest of the first civilisations; over 1,000 known sites. **Mohenjo-daro** covered ~200 to 250 ha with perhaps
40,000 people **[rough]**; Harappa, Rakhigarhi, Dholavira. Grid streets, covered drains, wells, baked bricks in a **1:2:4** ratio.
Winter wheat and barley, summer millets and rice in places; cotton; zebu.

**Weights.** Cubes of banded grey **chert**, cut and polished, consistent across sites hundreds of km apart: a **binary** series
**1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64** (smallest ~0.856 g; **commonest, the "16", ~13.7 g**), then **decimal** multiples (160, 200, 320,
640, up to ~100 times the 13.7 g weight) **[firm]**. Probably for trade, taxing goods entering cities and allocating craft
materials (Kenoyer) **[rough reading]**.

| Craft | What it involved |
|---|---|
| **long carnelian beads** | blanks heated to redden, drilled end to end with a special hard-stone drill: one long bead could take a week or more **[rough; Kenoyer's experiments]**; exported to Mesopotamia |
| **shell bangles** | *Turbinella* conch from the Gujarat and Makran coasts, sawn into rings |
| **stoneware bangles** | high-fired controlled ceramic, perhaps restricted production |
| **steatite seals** | carved, fired, with the undeciphered script; sealings show corded packages |
| **copper** | from Rajasthan (Khetri) and Oman; tin scarce, much metal copper or arsenical |
| **cotton cloth** | the earliest known |

**Trade.** The Indus is probably the **Meluhha** of Mesopotamian texts, sending carnelian, ivory, timber and gold; Meluhhans
lived in Mesopotamia (a "Meluhhan village" at Guabba). Lothal in Gujarat has a brick basin read as a dock **[contested]**;
Shortugai on the Amu Darya was a Harappan outpost near lapis and tin; Dilmun used Indus-standard weights.

**What is different.** No palaces, royal tombs or images of rulers have been found, and little sign of large-scale war:
**standardisation, planning and long-distance trade without an obvious redistributive king** **[observation firm; meaning
contested]**. Decline after ~1900 BC is linked to shifting monsoons and rivers and the fading Gulf trade **[contested]**.

### 5.4 How their supply chains worked

| Chain | Mesopotamia | Egypt | Indus | Aegean palaces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **grain** | irrigated barley; household granaries; rations; risk from salt and floods | basin-irrigated emmer and barley; royal granaries; the flood measured and taxed | two seasons; granaries **[use contested]** | wheat, barley, olives, figs; rations |
| **fibre and cloth** | **wool, the export**; thousands of dependent weavers | **linen**; temple workshops | **cotton** | **wool**: tens of thousands of sheep tracked |
| **metal** | all imported (Magan, Anatolia, the east) | Sinai copper by expedition; Nubian gold | Rajasthan and Oman copper | imported copper and tin, allocated to smiths |
| **timber** | imported | from Byblos | local | local |
| **money and measure** | silver by weight (shekel ~8.3 g) and barley | copper deben, grain sacks | chert weights | accounts in kind |
| **who carries** | temple and palace agents, later private merchants (Old Assyrian) | royal expeditions; village barter | merchants (inferred) | palace agents; Cypriot and Canaanite ships |

Different settings selected different institutions: a predictable flood and a river road gave Egypt one state and royal
expeditions; an unpredictable flood with salt gave Mesopotamia city-states, canal wars and records of debt; a vast plain with two
seasons gave the Indus standards without visible kings; hill country dependent on sea imports gave the Aegean selective palaces.

## 7. How supply chains formed and joined

1. **Chains began shallow and local.** An Oldowan flake is one person and one step.
2. **They lengthened by assembly.** The hafted spear needed stone, wood, glue and cord; the bow five chains. The joints (cord,
   glue, fire) became hubs.
3. **Fire was the first general-purpose hub.** Cooking, heat treatment, glue, smoking, plaster, pottery, salt and metal all pass
   through a hearth or kiln.
4. **Storage turned pulses into time capital.** Once a glut could be kept, people stayed put, owned stores and ranked themselves.
   Farming came after storage.
5. **Fields and herds made land and beasts into capital,** with herds as joint-product machines and the plough and cart as
   increasing-returns thresholds that rewarded those with land and teams.
6. **Chokepoints made exchange necessary, and exchange came before markets:** gift partnerships and down-the-line passing first,
   then directional trade by specialists.
7. **Metal made chokepoints the structure of whole societies.** Two rare ores from different places meant long routes, pack
   animals, ships, credit and treaties; control of routes became power, and their breaking ended a civilisation.
8. **Cities were redistributive machines with an export industry:** grain and wool turned into rations and cloth by dependent
   labour, cloth traded for what the plain lacked, and writing, weights and units of account as the information goods holding it
   together.

## 8. What a simulator could take from this

Possibilities to think with, not a build list.

1. **A return-rate ladder from the data.** Wild resources ranked over two orders of magnitude (big game ~15,000 to 30,000
   kcal/hour, small game 5,000 to 15,000, fish and fruit 2,000 to 5,000, nuts ~1,000 to 1,500, seeds 300 to 1,300, roots 100 to
   300), with search time rising as top ranks are depleted. Diet breadth should then widen under pressure: the broad spectrum
   revolution, and the road to farming.
2. **Processing as the real cost of low-ranked food.** Seeds and acorns are cheap to gather and dear to make edible. Tools that
   cut processing (grinding stones, pots, querns, then the mill) are what lift a food up the ranking, so economy_web.md §2.1's
   switch has earlier rungs in the quern and the pot.
3. **Storage before farming, and storage as the source of property.** A storable pulse (a fish run, a nut fall, wild grain) lets
   agents find sedentism and ownership before farming. The outcome to watch is Woodburn's shift from immediate-return sharing to
   delayed-return property.
4. **Sharing as insurance with rules.** Big kills pooled, plant food kept; demand sharing and tolerated theft as behaviour, not
   engine rules; partnerships with households elsewhere as the safety net in a bad year. Watch whether sharing fades once food is
   storable and predictable.
5. **Farming that pays less per hour but more per hectare.** Bowles's 63% is a calibration: early cultivation should not beat good
   foraging per hour. It should win on density, storage and defensibility, cost health and raise births. If farming pays better per
   hour from the first day, the transition is too easy to be interesting.
6. **Herds as joint products over a lifetime,** milked, shorn, worked and manured alive, eaten once dead: a time-capital choice.
   Cloth, at hundreds of spinning hours a garment, is the natural store of value and export.
7. **The pyrotechnology ladder as one chain.** Hearth, heat treatment, lime, kiln, smelting furnace, each needing more fuel, heat
   and draught. Metal could be reachable only by people who already run kilns and burn charcoal, so it grows out of the fuel and
   clay chains.
8. **Two-ore chokepoints.** Bronze needs copper and tin from different places, tin the rarer. One ore a day away and the other a
   week makes the economy depend on a route, carriers, credit and safe passage: Kanesh's donkeys, the naruqqum and the treaties
   are what agents would have to invent.
9. **Exchange without markets, read from the ground.** Down-the-line passing gives an exponential fall-off with distance;
   directional trade gives peaks far from source; prestige chains give hoards. Renfrew's method could be applied to a run's goods.
10. **Mines and drives as the first threshold works.** A crew fed for weeks before any return: the first case where a group must
    pool surplus and accept direction, a smaller, earlier mill or weir.
11. **Rations as the first wage, records as the first information good.** A great household that holds the granary, pays in
    standard measures and keeps records of quotas and deficits is redistribution at scale (02zzi §3.3). A late ration
    (Deir el-Medina's strike) tests whether labour organises.
12. **Settings that select institutions.** Predictable flood and a river road; unpredictable flood and salt; two seasons and wide
    trade; sea imports to hill country. Varying land and water between worlds, not rules, is the experiment history ran.
13. **Brittle interlocking chokepoints.** Shocks to several routes at once (tin, copper, grain) test how deep an economy can grow
    and how fast it falls back.
14. **Units of account before coin.** The deben of copper and the shekel of silver priced goods paid in other goods: prices can be
    compared without money changing hands, one of several routes to what the engine's coin does.
