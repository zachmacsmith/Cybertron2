## Summary

For most of history most work was not sold. Grinding, cooking, fetching water and fuel, spinning, brewing, washing, bearing and
nursing children, and caring for the sick and the old took more hours than any field task, and they were done mostly by women and
children inside the household. Where sources give time budgets the scale is plain: Mesoamerican women spent three to five hours a
day grinding maize for a family; a pre-industrial shirt took hundreds of hours of spinning; an American household in 1900 spent
about 58 hours a week on meals, laundry and cleaning; and today unpaid care still comes to about 16.4 billion hours a day, three
quarters of it by women, worth about 9% of world GDP at a minimum wage. Who did which task was set less by strength than by what
could be combined with minding small children, and by the tools: where the plough came, women left the fields and, by the strong
evidence of Alesina, Giuliano and Nunn, their descendants still work less outside the home. Trades that began as women's household
work (brewing, dairying, spinning, midwifery) passed to men when they needed capital and scale. The household gave work up to the
market in a long sequence, first the processing of food and fibre (the mill, the oven, the alehouse, the putting-out merchant, the
factory), then laundry and clothing, then care itself (schools, hospitals, nurseries, pensions, care homes), and in the twentieth
century piped water, electricity and appliances cut what was left by about two thirds, while women moved into paid work along
Goldin's U.

Kinship was the institution that held the rest together where no state did. Marriage moved labour, land and goods between
households: bride-price in two thirds of recorded societies (mostly hoe farming, where a woman's work was the value moved), dowry in
under 4% of them but in the plough-and-land societies of Europe and Asia where most people lived, and dower and the morning gift to
secure a widow. Inheritance rules decided whether land stayed whole or split, and so who stayed, who left for service, towns and
the colonies, and where rural industry took root. Lineages and clans pooled risk, lent, schooled and fought; Europe's weak kin
groups, late marriage and service in other households (Hajnal) pushed people into guilds, communes, parishes and companies instead.
Children were labour, insurance and a transfer: put out to service, apprenticeship, fosterage and wet-nurses, sometimes abandoned
to foundling hospitals. Firms grew out of the household (the Italian *compagnia*, the Fugger, the Mitsui house, the Chinese lineage
trust, the chaebol), and family control is still the commonest form of large-firm ownership outside the Anglo-Saxon world.

## 1. The work inside the house

### 1.1 What it is and how it came to be counted

- **The household's own work** is everything a household does for its own members that it could in principle buy: processing food,
  fetching water and fuel, making and mending clothes, cleaning and washing, bearing, feeding and teaching children, nursing the
  sick, keeping the old. Margaret Reid (*Economics of Household Production*, 1934) gave the test still used: an activity is
  production if someone else could be paid to do it (the **third-party criterion**) (*unchecked*).
- **National accounts leave it out.** The UN System of National Accounts counts goods a household makes for itself (grain, cloth)
  but not services it does for itself (cooking, cleaning, care) (*unchecked*). So the long shift of these services into the market
  shows as growth even when nothing more is done: the laundry's bill is GDP, the wash at home is not.
- **Time-use surveys** are how it is measured now: diaries of the day, in many countries since the 1960s.
- **The size today:** the ILO's *Care Work and Care Jobs* (2018), from time-use data in 64 countries covering two thirds of the
  world's working-age people, puts unpaid care work at **16.4 billion hours a day**, the same as 2 billion people working eight
  hours unpaid; **women do 76.2%** of it, more than three times as much as men; valued at an hourly minimum wage it equals about
  **9% of world GDP, US$11 trillion** (2011 PPP) (*checked*). In 2018, **606 million** women of working age said unpaid care kept
  them out of the labour force, against 41 million men (*checked*).

### 1.2 Food processing: grinding, cooking, brewing, dairy

Food reached the mouth only after more work than went into growing it, and most of that work was women's.

- **Grinding.** Grain must be ground before it can be baked or boiled as porridge. With a saddle quern (a rubbing stone on a slab)
  this is the longest daily task in a grain-eating household; the rotary quern was about 4.3 times as efficient and ground a
  household's daily flour in an hour or two (part 02 §6; part 10 on grinding stones). The best time budgets come from maize: in
  Mexico and Central America grinding nixtamal on the metate for tortillas took **three to five hours a day** for a family, and
  Rachel Laudan estimates about five hours a day for a family of five, close to the most one person can grind, so one adult in
  five was in effect a full-time grinder; Arnold Bauer put women's tortilla work at **35 to 40 hours a week** (*checked*, as
  estimates; ethnographic, not time-diary data). The switch from metate to village maize mill in twentieth-century Mexico freed
  those hours, as the lord's mill had in medieval Europe (with the difference that the lord's mill was a monopoly that tenants
  paid for and evaded: part 05 §2.4).
- **Cooking** needed fuel, water and watching. With an open hearth cooking was slow and smoky and a fire was kept in all day;
  stews and pottage could be left, bread could not. The village or the lord's oven (the *four banal*) took baking out of many
  houses in France and England (part 02); in rice and maize societies the daily processing stayed at home.
- **Brewing.** Ale was everyday drink and food in northern Europe. Unhopped ale soured within days (*unchecked*), so every
  household brewed often or bought from a neighbour who had just brewed. Judith Bennett's *Ale, Beer and Brewsters* (1996): before
  the Black Death, brewing for sale was a women's by-trade; in the manor court of Brigstock (Northamptonshire) the brewers fined
  under the assize were almost all women, with an elite of thirty-eight **alewives** who brewed often for the market and faced
  almost no male competition (*checked*, from a summary). After the plague, demand for ale rose, brewing grew in scale, hopped beer
  (which kept and travelled) came in from the Low Countries, and the trade needed capital, licences and guild membership, which
  men got more easily; by 1600 commercial brewing in towns was mostly men's (*checked*, qualitative; the pace varied, and village
  alewives lasted into the seventeenth century). The existing web node `brewsters_alewives` (part 02) is this trade.
- **Dairying** was women's in most of north-west Europe: milking, butter and cheese, and the dairy's profits were often the wife's
  to manage. Voigtländer and Voth (part 05 §6.6) tie the European marriage pattern to the post-plague shift to pasture, where women
  had paid work. Dairying passed to men with scale: in the United States cheese moved from farm women to factories from the 1850s
  (*unchecked*), and in England large commercial dairies of the late eighteenth century were run by men (Deborah Valenze's
  argument, *memory*).
- **Preserving** (salting, smoking, drying, pickling, making preserves) turned the harvest pulse into a year's food and fell mostly
  to women (part 02 §6 on preservation).

### 1.3 Water and fuel

- **Water had to be carried** from a well, spring or river until pipes reached the house (in English towns piped supply to houses
  spread from the mid-nineteenth century; in villages often after 1945: *rough*). A family's daily water for drinking, cooking and
  washing is tens of litres, so many trips a day with pails or a yoke.
- **Today's measure:** UNICEF (2016) estimated that women and girls spend about **200 million hours a day** collecting water; in
  sub-Saharan Africa one round trip averaged 33 minutes in rural areas and 25 in towns; in Malawi women who fetched water spent on
  average 54 minutes a day doing it, men who did spent 6 (*checked*). Where water is not piped, the work falls on women and
  children, especially girls.
- **Fuel:** a pre-industrial person burned one to two tonnes of wood a year (part 01, README finding 1). Gathering firewood (dead
  wood, furze, dung cakes, turves under common rights) was women's and children's work in much of the world, and common rights to
  fuel (estovers, turbary) were among those whose loss in enclosure fell hardest on cottagers' wives (Humphries 1990, cited in 05).
  Where wood grew scarce near towns, fuel was bought (London's sea coal; part 01), an early step of household provision into the
  market.

### 1.4 Cloth: spinning, weaving, sewing

- **Spinning was the largest single household task in fibre-using societies.** Every thread of every cloth was spun by hand with a
  spindle until the spinning wheel (in Europe from about 1280: part 03) and then the jenny, water frame and mule (1760s to 1780s:
  part 07). Estimates of spinning time are poor: reconstructions put a coarse linen shirt at about **500 hours** of spindle
  spinning, and Norfolk worsted at over 20 hours of spinning a metre (*rough*: experimental and hobbyist estimates differing by a
  factor of several). The folk rule of several spinners (often "seven") to keep one weaver at work is repeated but not measured
  (*rough*).
- **It filled the gaps:** spinning could be picked up and put down, done while minding children, walking, watching beasts or
  talking. It is why "spinster" came to mean an unmarried woman, and why the distaff was the symbol of women's work in medieval art
  (*unchecked*). Part 05 §1.3 counts it among the 300 or more housework days a year in the peasant household.
- **Weaving** was more often men's in Europe (the broadloom), women's in much of Asia and the Andes; **sewing and mending** were
  women's nearly everywhere until ready-made clothing (from the mid-nineteenth century with the sewing machine, *unchecked*).
- **Putting-out** (part 05 §1.9) sold the spinning hours: the merchant brought wool or flax to the cottage and paid by the piece.
  It was the first large market for women's and children's household time, and when the jenny and the mill took spinning out of
  the house (1770s to 1820s) cottage incomes fell sharply where nothing replaced it (*rough*).

### 1.5 Laundry and cleaning

- **Washing day** was the heaviest regular task: water carried and heated over a fire, clothes soaked in lye made from wood ash or
  in soap, pounded, scrubbed, boiled, rinsed, wrung, mangled, dried and ironed with irons heated at the fire. In nineteenth-century
  England and America it took most of Monday and the ironing much of Tuesday (*unchecked*).
- **It was among the first household tasks sold:** the washerwoman and laundress were among the commonest women's trades in towns
  from the middle ages; commercial steam laundries grew in the later nineteenth century, and in the American West Chinese immigrants,
  barred from much other work, ran many hand laundries from the 1850s (*unchecked*).
- **Cleaning** grew with the house: glass windows, floors, more goods, more linen. Ruth Schwartz Cowan (*More Work for Mother*,
  1983) argued that rising standards absorbed much of what appliances saved (§2.8).

### 1.6 Bearing and rearing children

- **Breastfeeding** was the largest single care task and the main contraceptive. Foragers nursed for three to four years and spaced
  births accordingly (part 05 §6.1); farmers weaned earlier on porridge and animal milk and had more children.
- **Childcare** in pre-industrial households was shared: older siblings, grandmothers and servants minded the small ones while the
  mother worked. Kristen Hawkes's "grandmother hypothesis" argues that post-menopausal women's provisioning of grandchildren was
  part of how humans came to raise children faster than apes (*contested*).
- **Teaching** (crafts, the household's ways, prayers, sometimes letters) happened in the house and, from about 7 or so, in work
  beside parents (§3).

### 1.7 Nursing the sick, the dying and the old

- **The sick were nursed at home** until the twentieth century for nearly everyone. Hospitals were for the poor without kin, the
  traveller and the soldier: monastic infirmaries and hospices, then the voluntary hospitals of the eighteenth century and the
  workhouse infirmaries of the nineteenth (§7).
- **The old were kept by their children or by contract.** In medieval England a retirement contract on the manor court roll gave
  the holding to an heir or a stranger in return for food, a room and clothing for life (part 05 §6.5; Richard Smith); in
  north-western Europe the old more often lived apart from their married children than in the east and south (Laslett).
- **Women laid out the dead,** and midwives and "wise women" were the first health workers of most villages (§2.4).

## 2. Who did what: the division of labour by sex and age

### 2.1 The cross-cultural pattern

- **The common pattern** from George Murdock and Caterina Provost's coding of 185 societies (1973): tasks nearly always men's were
  hunting large game, metalworking, smelting, lumbering, boat-building and mining; tasks nearly always women's were cooking,
  gathering wild plants, fetching water and fuel, preparing vegetable food, laundry and spinning; many tasks (planting, harvest,
  dairying, weaving, pottery) swung between the sexes from society to society (*memory*).
- **Why:** Judith Brown (1970) proposed that women's tasks were those **compatible with minding small children**: interruptible,
  near home, not dangerous, not needing long trips. Strength explains some (felling, ploughing), but not why grinding (very heavy)
  was women's. Specialisation inside the couple then followed from different skills learnt young (Gary Becker's *Treatise on the
  Family*, 1981, argued it from comparative advantage; *unchecked*).
- **The pattern moved with technology.** Each new tool or scale could move a task across: the plough moved field work to men
  (§2.3); the mill moved grinding from wife to miller; the brewhouse moved brewing from alewife to brewer; the factory moved
  spinning from women at home into mills, where the throstle and water frame were women's and children's work but mule spinning
  became men's, with children as piecers (part 07).

### 2.2 Boserup: female and male farming systems

Ester Boserup (*Woman's Role in Economic Development*, 1970) set the frame most of this section still uses.
- **Female farming systems:** shifting hoe cultivation, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South-east Asia, where women did
  most of the field work and men cleared, herded and fought. Land was plentiful, labour scarce, and a wife's labour was wealth: so
  **polygyny** paid (more wives, more fields worked), and so did **bride-price** (the groom's family pays the bride's for the loss
  of her work and children).
- **Male farming systems:** plough cultivation on permanent fields, mostly in Eurasia, where men ploughed and women worked more in
  the house, the yard and the dairy. Land was the scarce wealth; daughters took a share of it to marriage as **dowry**, and
  marriage was more often monogamous and between equals in property (Jack Goody's "diverging devolution", §4.3).
- **Boserup's warning:** colonial and development policy, assuming men were the farmers, gave land titles, credit and training to
  men in female farming systems and lowered women's status and output (*unchecked*, qualitative).

### 2.3 The plough hypothesis

- **Alesina, Giuliano and Nunn** ("On the origins of gender roles: women and the plough", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 128,
  2013) tested Boserup's idea with the ethnographic record. Descendants of societies that traditionally used the plough have today
  **less equal attitudes to women's roles, lower female labour force participation, fewer women owning firms and fewer women in
  politics**. They instrument historical plough use with the land's suitability for plough-positive crops (wheat, barley, rye
  against sorghum, millet) and find the same; second-generation immigrants in the United States carry the pattern (*checked*).
  Female participation in 2000 ranged from 16.1% in Pakistan to 90.5% in Burundi (*checked*).
- **The mechanism:** the plough needs upper-body strength and is hard to combine with child care, so men took the fields, women
  the house, and a norm that a woman's place is at home outlasted the plough.
- **Critiques:** the instrument may pick up other effects of cereal agriculture (state formation, inheritance of land); some
  regions (the Andes, parts of South-east Asia) had ploughs or intensive farming with high women's field work (*contested*).

### 2.5 Age: what children and the old did

- **Children** started with tasks that were light, near home and compatible with being small: fetching, bird-scaring, gleaning,
  minding geese and younger children, herding small stock, carding wool, winding thread. From about 12 to 14 they did adult tasks
  at lower output and left for service (part 05 §1.6). §3 takes this up.
- **The old** moved to lighter and supervisory work: minding, spinning, mending, teaching, managing a holding while a son did the
  ploughing; then into retirement contracts. In the engine an elder has 5 units a day (spec/agents/work.md).

### 2.6 The industrious revolution as a reallocation of the household's time

Part 05 §1.9 sets out de Vries's industrious revolution and its critics. Seen from inside the house, the argument is about
**women's and children's time**: households moved hours from making things for themselves (bread, ale, candles, cloth, clothes)
to making things for sale (spinning and weaving on putting-out, lace, straw-plaiting, nail-making), and bought the goods they had
stopped making along with new ones (sugar, tea, printed cotton, pottery). De Vries's later stage, the **breadwinner-homemaker
household** of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, reversed it: married women left market work and put their hours into
the home again, into cooking, cleaning, health and the schooling of fewer children; and a third stage, from the mid-twentieth
century, sent them back to the market (*contested*, de Vries 2008).

### 2.7 The male breadwinner household

- **The family wage.** Unions and reformers from the mid-nineteenth century argued for a man's wage sufficient for a family, so
  that wives and children need not work; factory acts limited women's and children's hours (part 05 §1.10); the respectable working
  household kept its wife at home if it could.
- **Goldin's U.** Claudia Goldin (1990, 1995) found that married women's labour force participation **falls and then rises** with
  development: high on farms and in family workshops, low when work moves to factories and offices away from home and carries a
  stigma for wives, then rising with schooling and white-collar work. In the United States the participation of married white
  women in 1890 was about **2.5%** (census counts that miss much farm and home work), white women's overall about **16.3%**; the
  bottom of the U came between 1890 and 1940, and overall female participation was as high in 1940 as in 1890 and rose after
  (*checked*, Olivetti's summary of Goldin).
- **Marriage bars** forbade employing married women in many teaching, clerical and civil-service posts in Britain and the United
  States until the 1940s and 1950s (the British civil service bar ended in 1946: *unchecked*).

### 2.8 The appliance revolution

- **The sequence** (part 08 on electricity): piped water and drains (from the 1850s to the 1950s depending on place), gas cooking,
  electric light (1880s on), then **appliances** (irons, washing machines, vacuum cleaners, refrigerators, from the 1910s and
  spreading widely in the United States in the 1920s to 1950s and in western Europe in the 1950s and 1960s, *rough*), which needed
  wired houses and consumer credit (part 08 §1). Bought food (canned, then frozen and ready-made) and ready-made clothes did as much.
- **Engines of liberation.** Greenwood, Seshadri and Yorukoglu (*Review of Economic Studies*, 2005) cite Stanley Lebergott's
  estimate that housework (meals, laundry, cleaning) took about **58 hours a week in 1900 and 18 in 1975**, and model the fall in
  appliance prices as driving married women into paid work: in their model, progress in household technology alone accounts for
  about **28 points of a 51-point rise** in married women's participation (*checked*, from the working draft).
- **The counter-evidence.** Joann Vanek (1974), from US time diaries, found full-time housewives spent roughly as much time on
  housework in the 1960s as in the 1920s, around 50 to 55 hours a week (*contested*: the selection of who was a full-time
  housewife changed, and the figure itself is disputed). Ruth Schwartz Cowan's *More Work for Mother* (1983) argued appliances
  removed men's and servants' tasks (hauling fuel and water, beating carpets) and raised standards of cleanliness and cooking, so
  that the housewife's hours fell little; Joel Mokyr called this the "Cowan paradox". Valerie Ramey's series finds home production
  fell about 6 hours a week from 1900 to 1965 and about 12 more from 1965 to 2005 (*checked*, from a summary). Cavalcanti and
  Tavares (2008) find appliance diffusion did raise women's participation across countries (*unchecked*).
- **Other causes of women's entry to paid work:** the rise of clerical and service jobs (typists from the 1880s, *unchecked*);
  schooling; falling fertility; the contraceptive pill, which Goldin and Katz (2002) argue let women invest in careers (*unchecked*);
  divorce law; and war work in both world wars. Today women are about 39% of the world's employed (*memory*, ILO), with the gap
  widest in South Asia, the Middle East and North Africa, the plough-and-seclusion belt of §2.3.

### 2.9 The division of labour: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

## 3. Children: their labour, their value, and their transfer between households

### 3.1 What a child cost and returned

- **Net consumers for a long time.** Among foragers and horticulturalists children produce less than they eat until their mid to
  late teens; Hillard Kaplan's figures for several foraging peoples, and Karen Kramer's for Maya subsistence farmers (*Maya
  Children*, 2005), show that children work a lot but that families with many young children depend on parents and older siblings
  to feed them (*memory*). The household's consumer-to-worker ratio, Chayanov's driver (part 05 §1.2), is a statement about
  children.
- **Wealth flows.** John Caldwell (1976) argued that in pre-transition societies wealth flows from children to parents over a life
  (labour while young, support in old age), so high fertility pays; when schooling, child labour laws and pensions reverse the flow,
  fertility falls (*unchecked*; *contested* as the main cause of the demographic transition: part 05 §6.7).
- **Old-age insurance.** Where there were no pensions, sons (or in matrilineal societies, daughters' children) were the pension;
  studies of state pensions find they lower fertility (*unchecked*).
- **Quality against quantity.** Gary Becker's model, and Galor's unified growth theory (part 05 §6.2), turn on parents choosing
  fewer children with more schooling when the return to education rises.

### 3.2 Children's work in the household

- **From about 5 to 7:** fetching water and fuel, bird-scaring, stone-picking, minding geese and infants, gleaning after harvest
  (often the right of the poor, the young and the old: part 05 §2.3).
- **From about 7 to 14:** herding, weeding, carding and spinning, helping at harvest, carrying meals to the field; boys led the
  plough team, girls worked in the dairy and kitchen.
- **Its value** was real but small per child; it was large in sum because children were many. In English rural households of the
  late eighteenth century, the earnings of children (and wives) from spinning, lace and straw-plait could be a fifth or more of
  family income where by-trades were available (*rough*; Horrell and Humphries 1995, *memory*).

### 3.3 Child labour in early industry, and its end

- **Humphries's autobiographies.** Jane Humphries (*Childhood and Child Labour in the British Industrial Revolution*, 2010), from
  more than 600 working men's autobiographies, finds that child labour **rose** between about 1790 and 1850 with mechanisation
  and the division of labour; children of factory workers and outworkers started work well below age 10; children born 1851 to
  1871 started later and stayed longer in school (*checked*, qualitative, from summaries; the sample is boys).
- **Why mills wanted children:** small hands for piecing broken threads and scavenging under machines; low wages; docility. Parish
  apprentices (pauper children bound to mills by poor-law overseers) were the early water mills' labour force where no town was
  near (*unchecked*).
- **Its end** came with factory acts (1833 on, part 05 §1.10), compulsory schooling (Prussia from 1763 in law, England and Wales
  1880, *unchecked*), rising adult wages and machines that no longer needed children. Today the ILO counts about 160 million
  children in child labour, mostly in family farming (*memory*: the 2020 estimate).

### 3.4 Children passed between households: service, apprenticeship, fosterage, oblation

A household with too many children for its land, or too few for its work, moved children. Part 05 covers **servants in husbandry**
(about 60% of English 15 to 24 year olds in service: Kussmaul) and **apprenticeship**; the wider pattern is the circulation of
children as labour.

| Form | Where | What moved | Typical terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| **service** | north-west Europe, from the middle ages to the nineteenth century | youths from about 12 to 14 until marriage | board, lodging, a small yearly wage; a year at a time (05 §1.6) |
| **apprenticeship** | European crafts and trades | a boy (sometimes a girl) of about 14 | training and keep for labour, often with a premium (05 §1.7) |
| **fosterage** | early medieval Ireland (regulated in the Brehon laws), Iceland, Wales, Scandinavia | children of the elite to another family, for alliance; of the poor for keep | foster-fees paid; lasting ties between fosterer and foster-child (*memory*) |
| **child fostering** | West Africa today | a child sent to kin or a patron to work, learn a trade or go to school | in some countries a fifth or more of children live away from their mothers (*rough*, *memory*, Isiugo-Abanihe 1985) |
| **oblation** | the early medieval church | a child given to a monastery for life, often with land | abolished as binding in the twelfth century (*unchecked*) |
| **pauper apprenticeship** | England, 1601 to the 1840s | parish children bound to masters, later to mills | to 21 or 24; the parish rid of the cost (*unchecked*) |

### 3.5 Wet-nursing

- **What it was:** a woman paid to breastfeed another's child, at her own home or the employer's. It was a market in milk and care
  from Mesopotamia (Hammurabi's code regulates wet-nurses' contracts, *memory*) to the twentieth century.
- **Paris in 1780:** of about **21,000** babies born in the city, only about **1,000** were nursed by their own mothers (*checked*,
  from a secondary summary of Lenoir's report); the rest went to wet-nurses, mostly in the countryside, organised from 1769 by a
  municipal **Bureau des nourrices** that registered nurses and collected fees (George Sussman, *Selling Mothers' Milk*, 1982:
  *unchecked*). Artisan and shopkeeping wives sent babies out so they could work in the shop; rich families hired nurses into the
  house.
- **The cost was in deaths:** infants sent out died at rates far above those nursed by their mothers (in some series half or more
  in the first year, *rough*), and the nurse's own child was often weaned early or itself put out.
- **Its end** came with sterilised bottles, safe cows' milk and formula from the late nineteenth century (*unchecked*).

### 3.6 Foundlings and adoption

- **Foundling hospitals** took abandoned infants: Florence's Ospedale degli Innocenti (opened 1445), the Paris Enfants-Trouvés,
  London's Foundling Hospital (1739) (*unchecked*). Mortality in them was very high (often well over half within the first year,
  *rough*), and they were also a route by which poor parents placed children temporarily.
- **Adoption** as a way to get an heir: Roman adult adoption (Augustus was adopted by Caesar's will); Japan's **mukoyōshi**, the
  adopted son-in-law who takes the family name and runs the house. Mehrotra, Morck, Shim and Wiwattanakantang ("Adoptive
  expectations", *Journal of Financial Economics*, 2013) find that about **10%** of successions in post-war listed Japanese family
  firms went to a son-in-law, adopted son or adopted son-in-law, and that firms run by adopted heirs **outperformed those run by
  blood heirs** and nearly matched founder-run firms (*checked*). The house, not the bloodline, was the unit that persisted. The
  medieval church discouraged adoption in Europe, which Goody counts among the reasons more property reached the church (§4.5).

## 4. Marriage as an economic institution

### 4.1 Marriage systems

- **Number of spouses:** most societies in Murdock's *Ethnographic Atlas* allowed **polygyny** (*memory*: roughly 85%), though in
  most of them most men had one wife; general polygyny went with female farming systems (§2.2) and with wealth in herds.
  Monogamy was the rule in Greece and Rome and became Christian Europe's law. Polyandry was rare (Tibet, parts of the Himalaya).
- **Whom one may marry:** exogamy (outside the clan or village), endogamy (inside caste or lineage), cousin marriage (preferred in
  much of the Middle East and South Asia, which keeps property inside the lineage; banned to wide degrees by the medieval western
  church).
- **Where the couple lives:** patrilocal (with the husband's kin: most of Asia, much of Africa), matrilocal, or **neolocal** (a new
  household: north-west Europe, the core of Hajnal's pattern: part 05 §6.6).
- **Who arranges:** in most societies the families; consent of the couple was the church's rule in Europe from the twelfth century.
  The engine already has this split: an arranged marriage is an agreement between heads, and the two still choose to partner
  (spec/life/birth_and_death.md, [[D2-140 Partnering is both people's choice|D2-140]]).

### 4.2 The transfers

| Transfer | From whom to whom | What it is for | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **bride-price** (bridewealth) | groom and his kin to the bride's kin | compensation for her labour and children, which pass to his lineage; it circulates (cattle received for a daughter pay for a son's wife) | two thirds of the 1,167 societies in Murdock's atlas (*checked*, Anderson 2007); most of sub-Saharan Africa; parts of Asia and Oceania |
| **bride service** | the groom works for the bride's family for a time | the same, paid in labour | foragers and hoe farmers; Jacob's fourteen years for Rachel |
| **dowry** | the bride's family to the bride or to the couple (or to the groom's family) | her share of the family's property, often in place of land; a fund for the new household | under 4% of the atlas's societies, but Europe and Asia, where most people live (*checked*, Anderson 2007) |
| **dower** | the husband to the wife, to keep if widowed | the widow's living | English common law: a life interest in a third of the husband's lands (*unchecked*); the Islamic **mahr**, owed to the wife, part paid at marriage and part deferred to divorce or death |
| **morning gift** (*Morgengabe*) | the husband to the wife the morning after the wedding | hers to keep; early Germanic and Anglo-Saxon law (the laws of Æthelberht mention it, *memory*) | early medieval Europe; it merged into dower |
| **trousseau** | the bride's family | linen, clothing, household goods: the house's working stock | wherever dowry was given |
| **direct exchange** | sister for sister between two lineages | no payment needed | parts of New Guinea, Australia |

Hammurabi's laws (about 1750 BC) already regulate both: a bride gift from the groom (*terhatum*) and a dowry from the father
(*šeriktum*) which returned to her family if she died childless (*memory*). Rome had the **dos**, the dowry the husband managed
but had to return on divorce (*unchecked*).

### 4.3 Why bride-price in some places and dowry in others

- **Goody's diverging devolution.** Jack Goody (*Production and Reproduction*, 1976) argued that where land is scarce and
  farming intensive (plough Eurasia), parents pass property to **daughters as well as sons** (diverging devolution): dowry is the
  daughter's inheritance paid early. Families then care whom she marries, so marriage is controlled, often monogamous, and between
  equals in wealth. Where land is plentiful and hoe farming uses women's labour (Africa), property goes down the male line, women
  are wealth as workers and mothers, and bridewealth with polygyny follows (*contested*, but widely used).
- **Siwan Anderson's economics** (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2007): bride-price is a price that clears a market where
  women's labour is valued; dowry can be a **pre-mortem inheritance** to daughters or a **groom-price** where grooms of a higher
  status are scarce. Where marriage is hypergamous (women marry up, as in caste society), dowries rise as wealth inequality among
  grooms rises with modernisation; in Europe they fell as wealth came from wages rather than land (Anderson 2003, *unchecked*).
- **India:** in a survey of about 40,000 rural marriages between 1960 and 2008, dowry was paid in about **95%** of marriages,
  though illegal since 1961, and average real net dowry was remarkably stable over time (*checked*, World Bank summary of Anukriti,
  Kwon and Prakash); in 2007 the average net dowry was about 14% of a year's household income, by press reports of the same study
  (*unchecked*).
- **Renaissance Florence:** dowries rose so high that in 1425 the commune founded the **Monte delle doti**, a dowry fund in which
  a father deposited a sum at a girl's birth that matured, with interest, at marriage after seven and a half or fifteen years; if
  she died first the deposit stayed with the commune. Records cover about 32,000 girls from 1425 to 1545 (*checked*). It doubled
  as public borrowing, it is argued to have fed dowry inflation, and families unable to dower all daughters placed some in convents
  (*contested*, Kirshner and Molho 1978; Molho 1994).
- **Bride-price and girls' schooling.** Ashraf, Bau, Nunn and Voena (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2020): when Indonesia (1970s)
  and Zambia (late 1990s) built schools, girls' schooling rose among ethnic groups that pay bride-price and not among others,
  because an educated daughter commands a higher bride-price (about **58% higher** for one who finished primary school in Indonesia)
  (*checked*). The transfer shapes investment in children.

### 4.4 What marriage moved: labour, land, alliance and insurance

- **Labour:** under patrilocality the bride's labour moved to the groom's household; under service and neolocality both spouses'
  labour formed a new one. Bride-price paid for the first; savings from service paid for the second (part 05 §6.6).
- **Land and goods:** dowries in land or cash; dower securing a widow; marriage as the main way land changed hands between
  families outside sale (the peasant land market in court rolls shows land moving at marriages as well as at deaths: part 05 §2.2).
- **Alliance and insurance:** Rosenzweig and Stark ("Consumption smoothing, migration and marriage", *Journal of Political
  Economy*, 1989), from south Indian village data, found that households facing more variable farm profits married daughters to
  more distant villages, and that marriage-migration reduced the variability of household food consumption: kin in a village with
  a different harvest were insurance (*checked*). Fulford (2013) disputes it: he finds transfers between a married daughter's natal
  and marital families almost absent (*contested*).
- **Status and politics:** dynastic marriage moved kingdoms (the Habsburg motto "let others make war; you, happy Austria, marry");
  merchant houses married into each other to bind partners (§8).

### 4.5 The church, kin and marriage in Europe

- **Goody's second argument** (*The Development of the Family and Marriage in Europe*, 1983): from the fourth to the twelfth
  century the western church banned marriage to cousins (to the seventh degree at its height, cut to the fourth in 1215), to
  in-laws and godkin, discouraged adoption, concubinage and remarriage, and insisted on the couple's consent. Each rule made it
  harder for a lineage to keep its property in the family and easier for land to reach the church by gift (*contested*).
- **The psychological extension.** Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp and Henrich (*Science*, 2019) and Henrich (*The WEIRD People*,
  2020) argue that exposure to the western church's marriage programme dissolved intensive kin groups and produced more
  individualist, impersonally trusting people, which favoured guilds, towns, universities and markets (*contested*; criticised on
  measurement and causation).
- **Hajnal's pattern** (part 05 §6.6) sits on top: late marriage, many never marrying, neolocal households and service before
  marriage. Its economic effects are argued: de Moor and van Zanden's "girl power" (women's bargaining and market work) and the
  link to savings and lower fertility, against Dennison and Ogilvie's (2014) cross-European test which finds the most extreme
  versions of the pattern (in central Europe) in the slowest-growing economies (*contested*).

## 5. Inheritance: who gets the land, and what follows

### 5.1 The rules

| Rule | What it does | Where (typified) |
|---|---|---|
| **partible** | divides among sons (sometimes daughters) more or less equally | Kent's gavelkind; much of France, western Germany, Italy; China among sons; Islamic law with fixed shares (a daughter takes half a son's share, *unchecked*); France for everyone from the Civil Code of 1804 (*unchecked*) |
| **primogeniture** | the eldest son takes the land; others get portions, training or nothing | English and Norman military tenures from the twelfth century (05 §2.2); aristocracies across Europe |
| **ultimogeniture** | the youngest takes the home and land (the one still there when the parents age) | "Borough English" in some English manors (*unchecked*) |
| **the stem family** | one heir (any child, sometimes chosen) takes all and lives with the parents; the others leave or stay unmarried | Japan's *ie*, Catalonia's *hereu*, the Pyrenees, the Austrian Alps (Le Play's *famille souche*) |
| **entail, strict settlement, fideicommissum, mayorazgo** | ties an estate to a line of heirs so no holder can sell it | English landed estates from the 1650s to 1925 (*unchecked*); Spain, Italy, Germany |
| **lineage land** | the land belongs to the descent group; members have use rights | much of Africa, Melanesia; Chinese lineage trust land in part |

The rule often differed by social level in one place: English peasants on some manors divided while the lord's estate passed whole;
villein holdings were often impartible because the lord wanted one tenant for the dues (*rough*).

### 5.2 Effects on land

- **Partible inheritance fragments holdings** over generations unless the land market, marriage or emigration puts them back
  together; scattered tiny plots in the Rhineland, southern Italy and post-1804 France are its marks (*rough*). It also kept more
  people with a claim on land, and so on the land.
- **Impartible inheritance keeps the farm viable** and makes a class of non-inheriting siblings: younger sons and daughters who
  must marry an heir or heiress, go into service, a craft, the church, the army or a town, or stay as unmarried kin on the farm.
  It lowered marriage rates and fertility in stem-family regions (*rough*).
- **Retirement and transfer within life:** in medieval England many holdings passed before death by surrender in the manor court
  with maintenance for the parents (part 05 §6.5); the timing of inheritance set the age at which heirs could marry (part 05 §6.6).

### 5.3 Effects on migration and industry

- **Habakkuk** ("Family structure and economic change in nineteenth-century Europe", *Journal of Economic History*, 1955) asked
  what inheritance customs did to development: impartible rules pushed younger sons off the land into towns, industry and overseas,
  and gave the heir a farm big enough to accumulate; partible rules kept people in the countryside on small holdings, supplementing
  them with rural industry (*checked*, the question; the argument *unchecked* in detail).
- **Rural industry where land was divided.** Joan Thirsk (1961) noticed that English rural industries (knitting, nail-making,
  weaving) clustered where partible inheritance and weak manorial control left many smallholders with time to spare; that is the
  labour putting-out used (part 05 §1.9) (*unchecked*; *contested*).
- **Emigration:** studies of nineteenth-century Hesse-Cassel and elsewhere compare emigration under partible and impartible rules
  (Wegge 1999); the sign is argued (*contested*).
- **Capital and credit:** strict settlement kept English estates whole but let them carry mortgages and portions for younger
  children, making landed estates a base for borrowing (Habakkuk 1994, *unchecked*). Islamic fixed-share inheritance, Timur Kuran
  argues, split merchant fortunes at each death and kept partnerships small, and the **waqf** (an existing web node) was one way to
  hold property whole outside inheritance (*contested*).

### 5.4 Women and property

- **Coverture:** in English common law a married woman's movable property passed to her husband and she could not contract in her
  own name; the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 ended it (*unchecked*). Equity, trusts and marriage settlements let
  richer families protect a daughter's property.
- **Widows** were the women with most economic independence: holding land in dower or free bench, running a dead husband's shop,
  brewhouse or craft (guilds often let a widow continue the workshop), lending money. Many manors' tenant lists show widows as a
  steady share of holders (*rough*).
- **Daughters' shares** under dowry systems were generally smaller than sons' and paid in goods rather than land, so land stayed in
  the male line.

### 5.5 Inheritance: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Rule | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| partible | heritable land; equal claims | many smallholders; people kept on the land | fragmentation; a land market to reassemble; rural by-industry |
| primogeniture and stem family | a lord's or state's interest in one tenant; heritable land | viable farms; estates as political and credit bases | younger children into service, crafts, towns, armies, colonies |
| entail and settlement | courts and trusts | dynasties of estates | land off the market; borrowing on settled land |
| lineage land | descent groups | land without a land market | rights by membership; hard to sell, hard to mortgage |
| dowry and dower | movable wealth; families that care whom daughters marry | marriages between equals; widows' livings | savings motive; dowry funds; dowry inflation |

## 6. Kin groups as insurance, credit and government

### 6.1 Lineages and clans

- **Descent groups** (a lineage: people who trace descent from a known ancestor; a clan: a larger group claiming a common ancestor)
  held land, paid and received bridewealth and blood money, settled disputes, fought feuds and kept ancestor rites. In segmentary
  societies (the Nuer, the Tiv: Evans-Pritchard) the lineage was the polity (*unchecked*).
- **Feud and compensation** (`feud_wergild` in the web, part 05 §4.1): a kin group was liable for its members' wrongs and owed
  blood money; collective liability is credit and insurance under another name.
- **Chinese lineages** after the Song had ancestral halls, written genealogies, **trust land** whose rent paid for rites, schools,
  relief and loans, and lineage elders who arbitrated. Fan Zhongyan's charitable estate (about 1050) is the classic founding case
  (*memory*). In south China lineages owned a large share of the land by the nineteenth century (*rough*, Freedman).
- **Greif and Tabellini** ("The clan and the corporation", *Journal of Comparative Economics*, 2017; *Two Paths to Prosperity*,
  2025) contrast the clan in China, where cooperation ran through kin obligation and personal ties, with the corporation in Europe
  (guilds, communes, universities, self-governing towns), where weak kin groups made people cooperate with non-kin under impersonal
  rules; each path fed back on itself through morality and institutions (*checked*, the argument; *contested*).

### 6.2 Kin as insurance and credit

- **Demand sharing among foragers** (part 05 §2.2) is kin insurance at its widest.
- **Village risk-sharing:** Robert Townsend (1994) found households in Indian villages insured much of their idiosyncratic risk
  through gifts and loans among kin and neighbours, though not fully (*unchecked*). Marriage-migration added insurance across
  places (§4.4).
- **Caste and kin networks** in India: Munshi and Rosenzweig argue caste-based insurance networks are strong enough that men who
  migrate lose access, which keeps rural Indian migration low (*unchecked*; *contested*).
- **The kin tax:** where kin can demand a share, saving and investment suffer. In an experiment in Kenya, Jakiela and Ozier
  (2016) found people gave up money to hide windfalls from relatives (*memory*). Kin insurance and kin taxation are the same
  institution seen from two sides.
- **Credit inside kin:** loans between kin are the commonest credit in poor economies; rotating savings and credit associations
  (ROSCAs) are often built on kin or neighbourhood (*rough*). Part 04 treats village credit.
- **Remittances** are kin insurance at world scale: migrants' transfers home exceed foreign aid to low and middle-income countries
  (*rough*, World Bank).

### 6.3 Weak kin and its substitutes

Where kin groups were weak, as in north-west Europe after the high middle ages, other bodies did their work:

| Kin's job | Substitute where kin was weak | Part |
|---|---|---|
| insurance in a bad year | the parish poor law; gilds and friendly societies | 05 §6.5 |
| enforcing promises | manor court, merchant law, royal courts | 05 §4 |
| training the young | service and apprenticeship in other households | 05 §1.6, §1.7 |
| old-age support | retirement contracts, almshouses, later pensions | 05 §6.5 |
| pooling capital | partnerships, companies with transferable shares | 04, 05 §2.6 |
| collective liability | frankpledge (the tithing, not the kin, answered for a man) | 05 §4.2 |

Solar's argument that the English poor law freed labourers from dependence on land (part 05 §6.5) is the same argument from the
other end: the parish did what a lineage did elsewhere, but for strangers and by tax.

## 7. Care moves into the market and the state

### 7.1 The sequence

The household's work left the house in a rough order: first the tasks that gain most from scale and capital (processing food and
fibre), then those that gain from a network (water, fuel, light), then tasks of skill and standard (washing, clothing, baking),
and last the care of people, which gains least from machines.

| Task | First moved out | To what | Paradigm (web) |
|---|---|---|---|
| grinding | eleventh to thirteenth century in Europe | the lord's and then commercial mills | feudal_manorial |
| baking | middle ages (towns, banal ovens) | bakers; the village oven | feudal_manorial |
| brewing | 1350 to 1600 in England | alehouses and breweries | commercial |
| spinning and weaving | 1500 to 1800 putting-out; 1770 to 1830 factories | merchants; mills | early_modern, industrial |
| candles, soap | seventeenth to nineteenth century | chandlers; soap works | industrial |
| water and fuel | 1850 to 1950 | piped water, gas, electricity | industrial, later |
| laundry | towns from the middle ages; steam laundries late nineteenth century | laundresses, laundries | industrial |
| clothing | mid-nineteenth century | ready-made clothing, sewing machines | industrial |
| schooling | Prussia 1763, England 1870 to 1880 (*unchecked*) | parish, church and state schools | industrial |
| nursing the sick | voluntary hospitals (eighteenth century); trained nurses (Nightingale school 1860, *unchecked*) | hospitals | industrial |
| childbirth | twentieth century | maternity hospitals | modern |
| infant care | crèches (France, 1844, *unchecked*); Owen's infant school at New Lanark (1816); Fröbel's kindergarten (1837) | nurseries, kindergartens | industrial, modern |
| care of the old | almshouses; workhouses; pensions (Germany 1889, Britain 1908, *unchecked*); care homes | the state, insurance, private homes | modern |
| prepared food | late nineteenth century canning; twentieth-century frozen and ready meals; restaurants and delivery | food industry | modern |

### 7.2 Domestic service: the bridge

- **Servants did much of the better-off household's work** before appliances. In England and Wales domestic service was the
  largest single occupation for women through the nineteenth century, with over a million female domestic servants by 1881
  (*unchecked*). A middle-class household with one maid-of-all-work was the norm the Victorian census describes.
- **It shrank** in the twentieth century as factory, shop and office work offered better terms and appliances replaced it; in rich
  countries it came back in another form with migrant domestic workers and cleaners.
- **Today** the ILO counts about 75 million domestic workers worldwide, most of them women and most informal (*memory*, the 2021
  estimate).

### 7.3 Care work today

- **Paid care:** the ILO (2018) puts the world's care workforce (education, health and social work, and domestic workers) at
  **381 million**, about **11.5%** of all employment, of whom about **249 million are women**; care is about 19% of women's
  employment worldwide (*checked*, as cited from the report).
- **Unpaid care** remains larger: the 16.4 billion hours a day of §1.1.
- **Global care chains:** Arlie Hochschild (2000) named the chain in which a woman from a poorer country cares for a richer
  family's children or parents while her own children are cared for by kin at home (*unchecked*). Remittances (§6.2) are its
  return flow.
- **The cost problem:** care is a service whose productivity barely rises (a nurse can turn a patient only so fast), so its price
  rises with wages elsewhere (Baumol's cost disease; part 12 on services), and states that take it on (the welfare state, `welfare_state`
  in the web; part 05 §5.6) face a rising share of spending, more so as populations age (part 05 §6.7).
- **Valuing it:** satellite household accounts value unpaid household work at between about a fifth and two fifths of GDP depending
  on the country and method (*rough*).

## 8. Kin structure and the firm

### 8.1 The household as the first firm

Every early firm was a household: the merchant, his sons, slaves or servants and kin abroad. What changed over time was how far
the firm could reach beyond kin.

- **Old Assyrian merchants** at Kanesh (about 1950 to 1850 BC) ran family firms with sons and brothers as agents in Anatolia and
  wives in Assur who produced and shipped textiles and managed accounts; the naruqqum fund pooled investors beyond the family
  (part 11; `kanesh_caravan_firms`). The wives' letters show women as partners in the firm (*unchecked*).
- **Roman** business ran through the *familia*: slaves and freedmen as agents with a *peculium* (part 11, `peculium_slave_agents`).
- **The Maghribi traders** (part 05 §4.4) used kin and coalition ties across the Mediterranean.

### 8.2 Medieval and early modern European merchant houses

- **The compagnia** (from *cum panis*, those who share bread, *unchecked*) of Tuscan cities was a family partnership with
  unlimited joint liability: the Bardi, Peruzzi and Acciaiuoli of fourteenth-century Florence took outside partners and deposits
  but were named and run by a family, and fell together in the 1340s (*unchecked*).
- **The Medici bank** (1397 to 1494) was a holding partnership of the family with separate partnerships for each branch, each with a
  manager who held a share: a way to limit the family's liability to one branch's failure and bind non-kin managers (Raymond de
  Roover, *unchecked*). Datini's network of a dozen branches (part 04) was similar.
- **The Fugger of Augsburg** (Jakob Fugger, 1459 to 1525) were a family firm that became Europe's largest, in mining, cloth and
  lending to the Habsburgs; their partnership contracts kept control in the male line and excluded daughters' husbands
  (*unchecked*).
- **Diaspora houses:** Armenian merchants of New Julfa, Sephardic and Ashkenazi Jewish houses, Hanseatic families and Huguenot
  bankers spread kin across ports and used marriage to bind partners (*unchecked*; Aslanian on Julfa).

### 8.3 Asian houses

- **Japan's ie** made the house the firm: the **Mitsui** house (from 1673 in Edo) and **Sumitomo** wrote house codes, held property
  in common, and passed headship to the most able, by adoption if needed (*unchecked*). They grew into the **zaibatsu**: holding
  companies controlled by a family over banks and industries, dissolved under the occupation in 1945 to 1947 and reborn in looser
  form as keiretsu (`keiretsu_supplier_network` in the web).
- **Chinese lineage trusts as firms:** Madeleine Zelin's study of the Zigong salt merchants of Sichuan (*The Merchants of Zigong*,
  2005) shows lineage trusts used as shareholding vehicles that owned wells, furnaces and pipelines, before company law (*unchecked*).
- **Korea's chaebol** (Samsung 1938, Hyundai 1947) are family-controlled groups of affiliated firms built with state credit
  (*unchecked*).
- **India's business communities** (Marwaris, Parsis, Chettiars, Gujarati banias) financed and traded through caste and kin
  networks; the **Hindu Undivided Family** is still a legal and tax entity (*unchecked*). The Tata group, founded 1868, is the
  best-known Parsi family house (*unchecked*).

### 8.4 Family firms today

- **Ownership:** La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes and Shleifer ("Corporate ownership around the world", 1999) found that outside the
  United States and Britain most large listed firms have a controlling owner, usually a family, often through pyramids
  (*unchecked*). Most small and medium firms everywhere are family firms (*rough*).
- **Succession is the weak point.** Bennedsen and others (2007), from Danish data, find that handing the firm to a family CEO lowers
  performance compared with an outside CEO (*memory*); the Japanese adoption result (§3.6) is the counter-case where the family
  widens the pool of heirs. "Shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations" is the proverb.
- **What limited liability changed:** the corporation with transferable shares and a separate legal person (part 05 §2.6,
  `limited_liability`, `joint_stock_company`) let a firm outlive its family and raise capital from strangers; families kept control
  through block holdings and dual-class shares.

### 8.5 Why kin firms

| Reason | How kin helps | How it hurts |
|---|---|---|
| trust where courts are weak | kin can be punished by kin; shared reputation | cannot hire the best stranger |
| capital | the family's savings and dowries pooled | limited to the family's wealth |
| information | letters between brothers at distant ports | narrow |
| succession | a ready heir, trained from childhood | the heir may be poor; inheritance splits the firm |

## 9. How these interact with production and exchange

- **The household's time is the first scarce input.** Every hour at the quern or the well is an hour not spinning for sale or
  weeding; each machine or network that frees it (the mill, the wheel, pipes, appliances) shows up as more market work.
- **Who may sell what** follows the division of labour: women's surplus ale, butter, eggs and yarn were the first cash of many
  households, and the trades moved to men with capital.
- **Marriage and inheritance set the land market** and the supply of landless labour: impartible rules make servants, apprentices
  and emigrants; partible rules make smallholders who need by-employment.
- **Kin is the first insurance and credit,** and its strength decides how much else (parish, guild, court, company) is needed.
- **Children are labour, pension and heir;** their circulation between households (service, fosterage, apprenticeship) was the
  pre-industrial labour market for the young.
- **Care leaves the house last** and grows as a share of paid work as everything else is mechanised.

### Inside the medieval village and market town

- **Household work as time.** The map's main lesson for the village is that processing, carrying and care take most hours. The
  engine counts care units and grinding units already; cooking, fetching water, brewing, spinning and washing are not tasks there.
  The border would take them only where they make dependence or a chokepoint (02zzk §1): grinding at the quern against the mill is
  the strongest (part 02, part 05 §2.4); malt and ale is already a "next" row in 02zzk (part 02), and this part adds that it was a
  women's by-trade sold in turns under the assize; spinning waits on clothing, which 02zzk puts at the border. `scenario_design.md`'s
  limit (food at most 30% of actions) bounds how much processing time a world can afford.
- **The division of labour as something to read, not to write.** The engine records sex and makes pregnancy and nursing cost a
  mother food and units; nothing assigns tasks by sex. Whether households divide work (who grinds, who minds, who travels) is a
  measurement a run can make, and Brown's compatibility-with-care explanation is testable in it: the carried infant (weight 2) and
  the care units are the engine's version of the constraint.
- **Marriage transfers as agreements.** Since an arranged marriage is already an agreement between heads, a dowry or a bride-price
  is a leg in it (goods, a land leg in plots, or work: bride service is a work leg); a dower or retirement contract is an `every`
  leg of food for life. These are expressible in the agreement grammar now as legs (part 09 §2 "Credit"); what they lack is what
  every agreement lacks (below).
- **Inheritance rules and their effects.** The three rules exist and are household choices; their historical effects (partible
  fragments plots and keeps people; eldest makes younger children leave, take service or found elsewhere) are things the descendants
  benchmark (OPEN [[L-104 MAJOR the descendants benchmark|L-104]]) could read in the runs, with the generator's kin between villages ([[Q-45 Generation redesign|Q-45]]) as the base. Rosenzweig and
  Stark's marriage across villages with different harvests is the historical form of the dependence between villages that
  generation_redesign.md wants.
- **Service, fosterage and wet-nursing.** A youth can already join another household and work `for` it (hiring.md); service is that
  arrangement for a year with board as pay. Care can be given by anyone at the child's place, so a non-mother nursing or minding
  another household's infant for food is already possible in the physics; what is missing is only the standing relation.
- **Care's effects** beyond survival (OPEN [[L-26 Care's effects beyond survival|L-26]]) and **beliefs passed in childhood** ([[L-25 Beliefs, values and customs|L-25]]) are where the map's material on
  childhood (teaching in the household, wealth flows) bears.

### Behind the social primitives (OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]])

- **Transfers held to their terms:** a dowry returned if the wife dies childless (Hammurabi, Rome, Florence's fund), a dower a
  widow can claim against the heir, a retirement contract the heir must keep. These need agreements paid by deliberate acts and
  seen by witnesses, notice and remedies (part 09 §9 additions 1 and 3).
- **Kin obligation and liability:** blood money and collective liability; kin insurance as a standing relation; lineages as
  parties beyond the household with land held in trust (part 09 P1, P2).
- **Service as a standing relation** with a hiring day (Martinmas, Michaelmas: part 05 §1.6) and apprenticeship with a premium
  (P2).

### At the border: the market town

- The women's trades sold in town (the alewife and the alehouse, the baker, the laundress, the midwife, the dairy's butter and
  cheese at market) and their passing to guilds and capital; clothing with spinning and weaving (02zzk); apprenticeship and guilds;
  widows continuing a craft; a town's almshouse or hospital.

### Past the border: map only

- Putting-out at scale and the industrious revolution's reallocation of women's and children's time; child labour in mills and its
  end in schooling; the breadwinner household and Goldin's U; piped water, appliances and the fall of housework hours; domestic
  service as a mass occupation; schools, hospitals, nurseries, pensions and the welfare state; the care economy and global care
  chains; dowry funds and public finance; lineage trusts, merchant houses, zaibatsu, chaebol and family control of listed firms.
  These need people who are not agents, populations by place, institutions beyond the household and eras a run never reaches (part
  09 additions 2, 6 and 7).
