they take to the present: **woods and forestry; stone, copper and bronze; iron and steel; building; textiles; leather and animal

## Summary

The pre-industrial crafts were not separate industries but one web hung on three shared inputs: **wood** (fuel, building, bark,
charcoal), **the carcass** (hide, fat, horn, bone, sinew, wool) and **water** (power, washing, retting, tanning, fish). Almost every
deep chain was an **assembly** that drew on two or three of them at once, and almost every big change was a **switch of fuel or
power** that let one chain escape a shared bottleneck. The bloomery needed 10 to 18 kg of charcoal for every kilogram of iron, and a
charcoal blast furnace about 8 tonnes of wood for each tonne of pig iron, so iron was bound to the coppice and to a radius of about
three miles of woodland round each furnace; coke broke that bond after 1709 and moved iron to the coalfields. Tanning was bound to
oak bark and to a year or more in the pits; chrome salts after 1858 cut that to days and moved leather from the woods to the chemical
works. Cloth, the largest medieval industry, was bound to the hours of spinning (a spinner made perhaps 40 to 100 metres of yarn an
hour, so a few metres of cloth took days); the spinning wheel, the water-powered fulling mill and the putting-out system each
reorganised who did that work and who owned it, long before the mule. Fishing was bound to the pulse of the run and to salt; the
buss that gutted and salted herring at sea, and later the steam trawler and the freezer, each cut the tie to the shore.

Two lessons run through all six strands. First, **each paradigm switch changed the social form more than the product**: the fulling
mill made a lord's monopoly and moved cloth to the uplands; the blast furnace made iron a capitalist's works with a hired crew and a
woodland estate; the deer park and the warren turned a commons into private property guarded by law; the weir and later the trawler
set one community's take against another's. Second, **chains merged where they shared a bottleneck**: iron and tanning competed for
oak; fulling, bellows, hammers and mills competed for the same stream; rendering gathered the whole carcass's leftovers into one
industry that now feeds soap, feed, fuel and fertiliser. For a simulator the strongest material is in the woods (coppice as time
capital and commons), the carcass (joint products feeding leather, light and glue), the river (one site, many claims) and metal
(an assembly with a chokepoint at the ore); cloth and building matter most as sinks for labour and as the first places where capital
and wage work appear.

## 1. Woods and forestry

### 1.1 The chain

| Step | What | Shape | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| wildwood and wood pasture | timber, browse, mast, game | commons | grazing beasts and deer under scattered trees; pannage for pigs in autumn |
| **coppice** | underwood cut to the stool on a rotation; shoots regrow | time capital, joint products | hazel on 7 to 10 years; most species 10 to 30 years |
| **standards** | oaks left among the coppice to grow to timber | time capital (generations) | the "coppice with standards" of the lowland medieval wood |
| pollards | trees cut above browsing height | time capital | the wood pasture's way of having both grazing and wood |
| conversion | rods, poles, stakes, faggots, bark, charcoal, timber | joint products | one cut gives several goods; the by-products decide its value |
| charcoal burning | wood stacked, covered, burned slow without air | long skill | the collier; a clamp watched day and night |
| bark peeling | oak bark stripped in spring when the sap runs | pulse | for tanning; peeled oak then sold as poles or charcoal wood |
| carrying | wood is bulky; charcoal is light | threshold | charcoal travels where firewood cannot |

**Yields.** Modern British guidance, for well-managed coppice, puts dry wood growth for broadleaves (ash, oak, lime, sweet chestnut)
at about **2.5 tonnes per hectare per year** over a rotation, measured to 5 cm. A well-stocked hazel coppice of 1,500 to 2,000 stools
a hectare cut at ten years gives about **25 tonnes a hectare**, or **25,000 to 30,000 rods** of 3 to 4.5 m from a hectare of eight- or
nine-year hazel. These are figures for good, worked coppice; a medieval wood, browsed at the edges and cut irregularly, would give
less **(rough)**. One acre of hazel at eight years is therefore about 10,000 rods: enough for a great many hurdles, wattle panels and
fish traps, cut once in eight years.

**What a wood is for.** The woods' goods are joint products with very different horizons:

| Good | Horizon | Users |
|---|---|---|
| rods and withies (hazel, willow) | 1 to 10 years | wattle walls, hurdles, fences, baskets, fish traps, the weir |
| poles and stakes | 10 to 20 years | charcoal, rafters, tool handles, hop poles, fences |
| bark (oak) | the coppice cycle, peeled in spring | tanning |
| faggots and brushwood | every cut | ovens, kilns, hearths |
| timber (standards) | 50 to 150 years | frames, ships, mills, bridges |
| mast (acorns, beech nuts) | each autumn | pigs (pannage) |
| browse and grazing | every year, but it kills young regrowth | herds and deer |

### 1.2 Charcoal and the collier

Charcoal is wood with the water and volatiles driven off, so it burns hotter, cleaner and is light to carry. A traditional earth
clamp gives about **25% charcoal by weight** (50 to 60% by volume); skilled 17th-century colliers reached up to 90% by volume, and a
survey of reported yields runs from **10 to 36%**, with earth mounds and pits at the low end **(rough; it depends on whether the
wood was green or dry)**. Archaeometallurgists use about **8 kg of wood per kg of charcoal**. The engine's 5 timber to 1 charcoal sits
inside that range.

The collier was a long-skill trade on the margin of the village: he lived in the wood for the burning season, built the stack, and
watched it for days and nights to keep the burn slow; a lapse turned the wood to ash **(m: burns of several days to over a week)**.
Charcoal was the woods' cash crop wherever there was metal to smelt, and the smelter's demand set the coppice's rotation over whole
districts.

### 1.3 Timber for building and ships

A standard was capital across generations: the oak felled for a hall had been left by the grandfathers of those who used it. Oliver
Rackham's study of Grundle House, a 15th-century timber-framed house at Stanton in Suffolk, worked out its timber from the frame
itself and found it built mostly of many small, young oaks rather than a few great ones **(m: his figure is about 330 trees, most
under 18 inches across; to verify against Rackham 1972)**. That is the important point for a simulator: medieval building used a
wood's ordinary standards, not ancient giants, so building and the coppice-with-standards fit each other. Great timbers (for
cathedrals, mill shafts and ship keels) were genuinely scarce and were moved long distances or bought abroad.

Ships were the largest single sink for great timber in the early modern period, and naval demand turned oak into a strategic good
regulated by the state **(m)**. That is a chokepoint created by a later chain (navies) on a resource that grows on the scale of a
century.

### 1.4 Woods as contested commons

The wood had more claimants than any other land: the lord's timber and game, the commoners' firewood, browse and pannage, the
collier's and the tanner's needs, and the deer. Two of these destroy each other directly: browsing kills the coppice's regrowth, so
a coppice had to be fenced (by bank, ditch and hedge) for its first years after each cut, while wood pasture gave up underwood for
grazing. Medieval woods were therefore divided in time (fenced panels by year of cut) and in right (who may take what, when). This
is economy_web.md §2.6's commons in its real form: **restraint enforced by a fence and a calendar, with fines for breaking it.**

### 1.5 How the chain changed later

| Switch | When | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| coal for heat | 16th to 18th c. in Britain | household fuel left the woods; the coppice kept going only for crafts and charcoal |
| coke for iron | 1709 on (adopted widely from the 1750s) | the woods' biggest buyer left; coppice declined over the 19th c. |
| bark replaced by chrome and extracts | late 19th c. | the second buyer left |
| plantation forestry | 19th to 20th c. | conifers in even-aged blocks for pit props, pulp, boards; the wood became a crop |
| pulp and paper, engineered wood | 20th c. | wood as fibre and as panels (plywood, particleboard, glulam), rejoining building |
| wood as biomass fuel | 21st c. | the oldest use returns at industrial scale (pellets) |

**The social effect:** the coppice was the wood worked by a crowd of small trades on a calendar; plantation forestry is a crop
managed by an owner with machines. The commons fell with the demand that made it worth defending.

**Links:** iron and copper (charcoal), building (timber, wattle, laths, shingles), leather (bark), fishing (weirs, traps, boats),
farming (hurdles, fences, tool handles, ploughs), herds (pannage, browse, hay of leaves), hunting (the deer and the park).

## 2. Stone, copper and bronze

### 2.1 Flint and stone tools

Stone tools are the shallowest chain with the longest skill: anyone can strike a flake, but a good knapper chooses the stone, reads
it and wastes little. The first true extractive industry was the flint mine. At **Grime's Graves** in Norfolk at least **433 shafts**
were dug into the chalk over about 37 hectares, the largest more than 14 m deep, mostly about 2600 to 2300 BC; the site may have
yielded **16,000 to 18,000 tonnes** of flint **(rough, an extrapolation)**. One estimate is that a large shaft meant removing more
than 2,000 tonnes of chalk, perhaps twenty people for about five months, before reaching the floorstone **(rough)**. So even the
Neolithic had crew work for a place-bound good of quality, and the good was traded far from the mine. Polished stone axes from a few
quarries travelled across Britain in the same way **(m)**.

### 2.2 Copper

Copper was the first metal because native copper and the bright oxide and carbonate ores sit near the surface and smelt at
relatively low temperature. The early chain **(m)**:

1. find and mine the ore (fire-setting: heat the rock, quench it, break it with stone mauls and antler picks);
2. crush and pick the ore by hand;
3. smelt in a small furnace or crucible with charcoal, a blowpipe or bellows;
4. cast into ingots or objects; work by hammering and annealing.

**Shape:** an assembly (ore, charcoal, bellows, a furnace) with a chokepoint at the ore and a long skill at the smelt. The first
copper was often arsenical (from mixed ores), which made it harder **(m)**.

### 2.3 Bronze and the tin trade

Bronze (copper with roughly a tenth tin **(m)**) is harder and casts better than copper, but tin is rare and lies far from most
copper. Bronze therefore made the first **long-distance chokepoint**: the Uluburun wreck off southern Turkey, about 1320 BC, carried
**348 ingots, about 10 tonnes of copper**, mostly from Cyprus, and **about 1 tonne of tin**, the right ratio for about 11 tonnes of
bronze; isotope work traced a third of its tin to the Mušiston mine in Uzbekistan. One ship carried enough metal for thousands of
weapons. Cornwall and Devon tin later supplied much of Europe, under the stannary system of chartered rights and royal dues **(m)**.

**Social effects:** bronze needs two ores from two regions, so it needs traders, ships and the safety of routes, and the elites who
controlled the flow; when those routes broke (the late Bronze Age collapse **(contested)**) the metal failed with them. The shape is
**assembly plus double chokepoint**, the strongest form of dependence between places.

**Links:** woods (charcoal), leather (bellows), stone (moulds, mauls), travel and ships (the tin route), coin and status.

## 3. Iron and steel

### 3.1 Why iron changed everything

Iron ore is common almost everywhere (bog ore, ironstone), so iron ended bronze's double chokepoint. It replaced a scarce ore with a
scarce **fuel and skill**: the bloomery smelts below iron's melting point, producing a spongy bloom of iron and slag that must be
hammered hot to drive the slag out. The chokepoint moved from the mine to the woods and the smith.

### 3.2 The bloomery chain

| Step | Labour, capital, energy | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| dig and roast ore | labour | bog ore or ironstone; roasting drives off water |
| make charcoal | the collier; the coppice | about 8 kg of wood a kg of charcoal |
| smelt in a bloomery | a clay shaft furnace, bellows (leather), a crew for a day | 10 to 18 kg of charcoal per kg of iron (experimental) |
| consolidate the bloom | hammering at the hearth | much of the bloom's weight lost as slag |
| smith | the forge, anvil, hammers, bellows | the long skill; edges, nails, tools |

Experimental smelts give **10 to 18 kg of charcoal per kg of iron**, so with 8 kg of wood per kg of charcoal, roughly **60 to 150 kg of
wood for each kilogram of iron**. Field smelts in Ontario got from **8% to 27%** of the ore's weight as bloom, depending on the ore
and the operators. A village-sized bloomery was therefore not limited by ore but by charcoal and by the people who could run the burn
and the smelt.

**Against the engine:** smelting copper in the engine takes 3 ore and 1 charcoal (or 4 timber) at the anvil, and the charcoal takes
5 timber, so about 5 timber per copper. Historically the wood to metal ratio is one to two orders of magnitude larger. The engine's
charcoal is light; if metal is to bind to the woods as it did, the charcoal input is the lever.

### 3.3 From bloomery to blast furnace and finery

| Paradigm | When **(m)** | Output | What it needed | Social form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| bloomery, foot or hand bellows | Iron Age to medieval | a bloom of a few kg a smelt | a crew for a day, charcoal | a household craft or a village smith |
| water-powered bloomery and hammer | 13th c. on | larger blooms | a stream, a wheel, a dam | the mill site: the lord's or a monastery's |
| **blast furnace** with water bellows | 15th c. (Weald from 1490s) | liquid **pig iron**, run continuously for months | a wheel, a stone stack, a woodland estate, a crew in shifts | capital; a master; wage labour |
| **finery forge** | with the blast furnace | pig iron refined to bar (wrought) iron under a water hammer | a second site and more charcoal | a two-site enterprise |

The blast furnace burns hotter, melts the iron and takes up carbon, so it gives cast iron (good for guns and pots, brittle) which the
finery then decarburises into wrought iron. A charcoal blast furnace took about **8 tonnes of wood to make 2 tonnes of charcoal, which
smelted 1 tonne of pig iron**. That is several times better than the bloomery per tonne, which is the increasing return, but the
furnace ran for months, so it needed a huge steady supply: sustaining a post-medieval furnace took the wood of about a
**three-mile radius**. The Weald peaked at **over 9,000 tons a year in the 1590s**; by 1720 about **15 furnaces made about 2,000 tons,
about 133 tons each**; charcoal furnaces elsewhere could reach **500 tons a year**.

**What this did socially:** the ironmaster held leases on woods for miles round and bought coppice on long rotations; colliers,
woodcutters, carters, fillers and founders became a hired workforce; the woods were now managed for one buyer. Charcoal iron is the
first place in this web where **a chain's fuel demand reorganised land use over a district**, and where the commons of the wood
gave way to a contract.

### 3.4 Coke, puddling, steel

| Switch | When | What it broke |
|---|---|---|
| **coke smelting** (Abraham Darby, Coalbrookdale) | furnace in blast January 1709; coke pig iron used in forges from the mid-1750s, when it became cheaper than charcoal pig | the tie of iron to the woods |
| puddling (Cort) **(m)** | 1784 | refining with coal; the puddler as a heavy long-skill job |
| crucible steel (Huntsman) **(m)** | 1740s | small quantities of uniform steel for tools and springs |
| **Bessemer converter** | 1856 | cheap bulk steel: US rails fell from **$174 a ton in 1868 to $17 in 1898**; in Britain from about **$80 in 1873 to under $20 in 1886** |
| open hearth (Siemens-Martin) **(m)** | 1860s | steel from pig iron and scrap; control of quality |
| basic process (Gilchrist Thomas) **(m)** | 1878 | phosphoric ores made usable: Lorraine, Sweden |
| basic oxygen furnace **(m)** | 1950s | fast converters; the main route today |
| electric arc furnace **(m)** | 20th c., growing | steel from scrap with electricity; small mills |

Today the world makes about **1,883 Mt of crude steel a year (2024)**: about **70% by blast furnace and basic oxygen**, **29% by
electric arc**, much of it from scrap. The chain has closed into a loop: the scrap yard is now an ore deposit.

**How iron merged with others:** with coal (coke; the coalfield towns), with railways (rails were the first mass market for steel,
and railways carried the coal and ore), with building (cast iron columns, then the steel frame), with ships (iron hulls, steel
plate), with machine tools (every other industry's machines), and with chemistry (fluxes, the basic process's slag sold as
fertiliser).

### 3.5 The smith and the trades of metal

The smith was the deepest village trade: anvil, hearth, bellows, hammers, tongs, a stock of iron and charcoal, and years of skill.
Medieval towns divided the trade by product (blacksmiths, locksmiths, bladesmiths, nailers, farriers, cutlers, armourers, pewterers,
braziers), and the nailers and cutlers were among the first metal trades organised on putting-out lines **(m)**. Metal changed labour
by tiers, which the engine already models: an iron edge on a spade, axe, sickle or ploughshare multiplied the labour of every other
chain, and the smith's mending kept those edges working. The village depended on the smith as on the miller.

### 3.6 Lead, silver and coin

Silver came chiefly from lead ores, separated by **cupellation**: the lead is oxidised away in a hearth of bone ash, leaving silver
**(m)**. So silver mining was a lead industry with a precious by-product, and the great silver strikes (the Rammelsberg, Kutná Hora,
the Saxon and Bohemian mines, then the Americas) **(m)** made mining towns with their own law. **Potosí** in the Andes shows the
chain at its most extreme: after **1572** its ores were refined by the patio (mercury amalgamation) process, with mercury from
**Huancavelica**, and its labour came from the **mita**, a draft that took about **one in seven** able-bodied men of the subject
districts each year. Its share of world silver is **contested**: 60 to 80% in the late 16th century by some estimates, about a fifth
or over a third across 1545 to 1810 by others. Huancavelica and Potosí together put out more than a quarter of the mercury vapour
emitted in Latin America from 1500 to 1800.

**Shape:** chokepoint (the lode), assembly (ore, mercury, salt, fuel, labour), and **coerced labour** where the capital needed more
hands than wages could draw. Coin made from it fed every other chain's exchange.

### 3.7 Mining rights and capital

Mining was the first chain where a **right to a resource** was separated from ownership of the land: the stannaries, the free miners
of the Forest of Dean and the German *Bergregal* (the ruler's right to minerals) let miners stake claims under a special law, paying
dues to the crown **(m)**. As mines went deeper they needed drainage (adits, then pumps, then steam), and so they needed shares and
investors: mining was an early home of joint ownership in parts (the German *Kuxe*) **(m)**.

## 4. Building

### 4.1 The materials and their chains

| Walls | Chain | Labour against capital | Life and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **wattle and daub** | coppice rods woven on stakes, daubed with clay, dung, straw | cheap; household labour; days | wears fast; burns; needs constant mending |
| **cob** | earth with straw, built up in lifts, each left to dry | almost no materials; much labour; weeks of waiting | lasts if kept dry ("good boots and a hat") |
| **turf, unfired brick** | local earth | labour | regional |
| **timber frame** | standards felled, hewn and sawn, jointed by the carpenter, raised by a crew; infill of wattle and daub | a specialist and a crew for the raising; the frame is prefabricated | lasts centuries; burns |
| **stone** | quarrying, carting, dressing by the mason, lime mortar | dear: quarry, cart, mason, lime kiln | lasts; fire-resistant; warm |
| **brick** | clay dug, weathered, moulded, dried, fired in a clamp or kiln | fuel and a kiln; increasing returns | fire-resistant; regular; used where stone is short |

**Roofs** have their own chain:

| Roof | Material chain | Life **(m)** | Fire |
|---|---|---|---|
| thatch of straw | a by-product of grain | about 15 to 30 years by type | the worst risk |
| thatch of water reed | reedbeds, cut in winter | longer, up to about 50 years | the worst risk |
| shingles | cleft oak | decades | burns |
| clay tiles | clay, a kiln, fuel | long | resistant |
| slate | quarry, splitting, carriage | very long | resistant |
| lead | mine, smelt, cast sheet | very long, but stolen | resistant |

### 4.2 Lime and the kilns

Lime mortar ties stone building to a fuel chain: limestone or chalk burned in a kiln to quicklime (losing its carbon dioxide),
slaked with water, mixed with sand. Lime also went on the fields and into the tanner's pits, so the lime kiln is a **hub**: rock,
fuel, building, farming and leather in one fitting. Its fuel demand was large (often coal where it could be had, wood elsewhere)
**(m: a tonne of lime took a substantial fraction of its weight in coal; to verify)**. Brick and tile kilns are the same shape:
**fuel-bound, increasing returns** (a large firing costs less fuel per brick), and a **pulse** (a clamp fired once a season).

### 4.3 The crafts

- **Carpenter:** the frame; the long skill of jointing and of laying out a frame on the framing ground before raising. Timber framing
  is a prefabrication system: frames were cut at a yard and carried to the site.
- **Mason:** the rough mason (walls) and the freemason (dressed stone); itinerant crews moving between large works under a master;
  lodges, wages by the day.
- **Thatcher, tiler, slater, plumber (lead), plasterer, glazier, sawyer** (the pair at a saw pit).
- **Labourers and carters:** much of any building's cost was carriage. Stone was moved by water wherever possible.

**Labour and material per building.** No figure for "a medieval house" was found in this session. The engine's current ratios are a
reasonable shape: a small wattle room is 8 timber and 6 units (about a day); a stone hall is 150 stone, 20 timber and 250 units
(weeks of a crew). Historically the order of magnitude for a peasant house was days to weeks of household labour plus a carpenter;
for a timber-framed hall house, a carpenter's crew for a season and the timber of many trees; for a stone church or castle, years and
hundreds of workers **(rough)**.

### 4.4 Fire

Fire is the reason building changed in towns. After London's great fire of **1212** the City's regulations under Henry fitz Ailwin
forbade new roofs of reed, rush, straw or stubble and required tile, shingle or lead; the rules were widely flouted. After **1666**,
when about **13,200 houses** burned, the rebuilding acts required brick and stone **(m for the act)**. Fire risk is the hinge between
the cheap chain (wattle, thatch) and the dear one (stone, tile, brick), and it is a **commons**: one house's thatch threatens the
street, so the rule has to be made collectively.

### 4.5 From wattle to concrete and the steel frame

| Switch | When **(m)** | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| the "great rebuilding" in brick and stone | 16th to 17th c. | chimneys, glazing, upper floors; brick as a mass good |
| sawmills, machine-made nails | 18th to 19th c. | the balloon frame: light timber nailed, no joinery; housing as a mass product |
| Portland cement | patented 1824 | an artificial hydraulic cement; concrete at scale |
| cast and wrought iron, then steel frames | 19th c.; the skyscraper from the 1880s | the wall no longer carries the building |
| reinforced concrete | from the 1860s to 1890s | steel and cement fused into one material |
| prefabrication, cranes, curtain walls | 20th c. | the site as an assembly line |

Building is now the largest sink for two other strands, steel and cement: cement alone emitted about **1.56 Gt of CO2 in 2023**,
roughly **5 to 8%** of the world's emissions, about 60% of it from the calcination itself rather than fuel. That is the lime kiln of
§4.2 at planetary scale. The shape of building did not change: **substitutes with trade-offs** (cost against permanence and fire),
**capital sunk in place**, crews and specialists; what changed is that its materials became products of the heaviest industries.

**Links:** woods (timber, wattle, laths, shingles, fuel for kilns), rock (stone, lime, slate), clay (brick, tile), metal (nails,
lead, ironwork, later the frame), straw (thatch, daub), herds (dung and hair in daub and plaster), transport (carriage was a large
share of any cost).

## 5. Textiles

### 5.1 Why cloth was the largest medieval industry

Everyone wore it, it wore out, and it took an enormous number of hours, most of them in spinning. Wool and cloth were also
England's main exports: raw wool exports were about **25,000 sacks in 1280**, peaked at about **40,000 to 45,000** sacks a year, were
**33,000** in 1355, and had fallen to about **9,700** by 1476 as cloth took their place. Cloth exports rose from about **10,000** cloths a
year in 1349-50 to **60,000** in 1446-47 and about **140,000** in 1539-40 **(secondary summary of Carus-Wilson and Coleman)**. One sack of
wool made about **4.3 broadcloths**. Cloth was also where the first wage workforces, the first putting-out capitalists and the first
industrial towns appeared (Flanders, Florence, then the English cloth districts).

### 5.2 The chain from fleece to cloth

| Step | Who | Shape | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| shear, sort, wash | the household, the woolman | joint product (wool from the herd) | fleece weights were small **(m: about a kilo a sheep)** |
| card or comb | women and children at home | labour | carding for woollens, combing for worsteds |
| **spin** | women at home: distaff and spindle, then the wheel | **the bottleneck** | 40 to 100 m of yarn an hour by hand spindle |
| warp and weave | the weaver, a man at a loom in a town or cottage | long skill; a loom is capital | efficient weaving used two looms and two weavers |
| **full** | by feet in a trough, then the fulling mill | paradigm switch to water power | felting and thickening with water and fuller's earth or urine |
| tenter, raise, shear | the shearman | long skill | the finish set the price |
| dye | the dyer | assembly with a chokepoint | woad, madder, weld; alum as mordant, a traded chokepoint |
| sell | the draper, the merchant | exchange | the chain's profit gathered here |

**Labour.** Spinning dominated. Katrin Kania estimates **40 to 100 metres of yarn an hour** for a hand spinner; even at 200 metres an
hour, the yarn for about **three metres of cloth took about 45 hours** of spinning. Another estimate is **over 20 hours of spinning for
each metre** of fabric **(rough)**. A household's clothing therefore cost hundreds of hours a year, which is why spinning was the
constant work of women and children in every household and the most important paid female occupation in pre-industrial England.

### 5.3 Flax and linen

Flax is a field crop, so linen draws land from grain: sown, pulled by hand, **retted** in water for days to rot the stems (fouling
the water, killing fish downstream), dried, broken, scutched and heckled to free the fibre, then spun and woven, and bleached in the
sun for weeks **(m)**. Hemp follows the same chain and made rope, sailcloth and sacking. **Shape:** a long chain with a water
conflict at its retting step (economy_web.md §2.9 already names this) and a seasonal pulse at bleaching.

### 5.4 The paradigm switches before the factory

| Switch | When | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| **horizontal treadle loom** | high middle ages **(m)** | weaving leaves the household for men in workshops and towns |
| **spinning wheel** | 13th c. in Europe **(m)** | faster spinning; guilds sometimes banned its yarn for warps at first **(m)** |
| **fulling mill** | from the 12th to 13th c. in England | fulling by water hammers; the industry moves to fast streams in the north and west, away from the chartered towns; Carus-Wilson's "industrial revolution of the thirteenth century" (her case is now modified) |
| **putting-out** | late medieval on | the clothier owns the wool and the cloth at each stage; spinners and weavers work at home by the piece; the rural cloth districts |

**The fulling mill** is the clearest pre-industrial case of a site-bound paradigm switch in cloth: it needed a stream, a wheel and a
building, it was often the lord's (with suit of mill, as for the corn mill), and it pulled weaving after it into upland valleys. The
first English examples appear in the Bishop of Winchester's pipe rolls. **Putting-out** is the first separation of capital from
labour in this web without any machine: the clothier's capital is the material in process and the market; the labourer owns a wheel
or a loom and works at home. It gave households cash work through the winter and made them dependent on a merchant's orders.

### 5.5 Mechanisation, cotton and synthetics

The mechanisation of spinning and weaving is covered in the industrial revolution part. In supply-chain terms **(m for dates)**: the
flying shuttle (1733), the spinning jenny (about 1764), the water frame (1769) and the mule (1779) attacked the spinning bottleneck;
Crompton's first mule had **48 spindles** and spun about **a pound of fine 60s yarn a day**; Roberts's self-acting mule (patents 1825 and
1830) spun about **900 hanks a machine a day against about 25 for a jenny and 8 for a hand wheel**. Labour hours to spin 100 lb of cotton
fell, by the classic estimate, from about 50,000 by Indian hand spinners to about 135 on the self-acting mule **(m, contested; the
original is Catling's estimate, often quoted)**.

The chain then merged with three others:
- **slavery and plantation agriculture** (American cotton) and the **Indian** handloom economy it displaced;
- **chemistry**: bleaching (chlorine), synthetic dyes from coal tar (from the 1850s), then **regenerated cellulose** (rayon) and
  **synthetic fibres** from oil (nylon in the 1930s, polyester in the 1940s) **(m)**;
- **global logistics**: the garment chain now runs from oil or cotton fields to fibre plants, spinning and weaving mills, dye houses
  and cut-and-sew factories in low-wage countries, to brands and retailers.

World fibre production was about **132 Mt in 2024**: **polyester about 59%** (about 78 Mt, 88% of it fossil-based, about 12% recycled),
**cotton 19%**, cellulosics 6%. Wool, the medieval staple, is now about one percent **(m)**. The garment chain is the putting-out
system made global: the brand owns the design and the market, contracts the sewing by the piece, and the risk falls on the workshop
(the Rana Plaza collapse of 2013, which killed over 1,100 garment workers, is the emblem **(m)**).

**Links:** herds (wool, sheep as the largest medieval land use change), fields (flax, hemp, later cotton), water (fulling, retting,
dyeing), rock (fuller's earth, alum), plants (dyes), later oil and chemistry, and trade (cloth as money's partner in long-distance
exchange).

## 6. Leather and animal by-products

### 6.1 The carcass as joint products

| Part | Becomes | Trade |
|---|---|---|
| meat, offal, blood | food (fresh, salted, smoked); black pudding; dog food, bait | butcher |
| **hide** (cattle) and **skins** (sheep, goats, deer, calves) | leather, parchment, vellum | tanner, tawer, currier, parchmenter |
| **fat** (suet, tallow) | candles, soap, grease, dubbin | chandler, soap-boiler |
| **horn** | combs, lantern panes, cups, spoons, handles | horner |
| **bone** | handles, combs, dice, needles, glue, ash for cupellation, later fertiliser | bone-worker |
| **hooves, sinew, scraps** | glue, size, neatsfoot oil, bowstrings | glue-boiler |
| **hair, wool** | plaster and daub binder, felt, stuffing, brushes | |
| **guts** | sausage casings, strings | |
| **dung** | fields, daub, the tanner's bate | |

This is the clearest case of **by-product capture** in the web: the butcher's leftovers fed half a dozen trades, each in its own
street, and the medieval town was zoned by smell (tanners and glue-boilers downstream and downwind). The engine already gives hide,
tallow and offal at slaughter (E) and has horn and antler; economy_web.md §2.4 proposes butchering with skill.

### 6.2 Tanning

**Vegetable (bark) tanning** is the long chain:

1. soak and wash the hide; flesh it;
2. **lime** it in pits (weeks; up to about three months, with the dung or beer bates after) to loosen the hair; scrape the hair;
3. **bate** it (dung of dogs or birds) to soften it;
4. **tan** it: weeks in weak ooze, then layered with ground oak bark in pits, the liquor strengthened in stages; one 17th-century stage
   alone kept hides covered with a foot of tanbark for **three months**;
5. dry, then **curry**: shave, oil (tallow, fish oil), work and finish.

The whole process for heavy hides took **11 to 18 months, sometimes two years**, by an 1823 account; by the 1860s oak tanning took
**6 to 8 months**, sole leather two or three more. **Shape:** **time capital and assembly** (hide, bark, lime, water, pits), with the
capital locked in the pits for a year: the tanner needed credit or a merchant's backing, which is why the tanner was often among the
richer townsmen **(m)**. Bark tied the tanner to the oak coppice (peeled in spring), so tanning and iron competed for the same wood,
and the water tie made the tan yard a downstream nuisance.

**Tawing** (alum and salt, for white leather, quick) and **oil tanning** (chamois, with fish oil) were the faster, lighter
substitutes for gloves and fine goods **(m)**. Currying and cordwaining (shoemaking) were separate guilds from tanning in medieval
towns, and the guilds fought over who could do which step **(m)**: a strong example of how **trades formed round the steps of one
chain**, each with a monopoly on its step.

**Yields.** No clean medieval yield (weight of leather per hide, bark per hide) was found in this session. Treat a hide as one hide's
leather, with a waste in trimmings that went to glue **(rough)**.

### 6.3 Tallow, candles and soap

Tallow (rendered beef or mutton fat) made **dip candles** and **rushlights** for the household and the chandler, and, boiled with lye
from wood ash, **soft soap** **(m)**. Wax candles, from bees, were for the church and the rich. The engine has tallow lights (E).
Later chains: soap from vegetable oils and soda (from the Leblanc process, then Solvay), stearin candles, kerosene and gas light
replaced tallow in the 19th century **(m)**. **Shape:** by-product with **substitutes** (rushlight, tallow, wax, oil lamp) differing by
price and smoke.

### 6.4 From tanning pits to chrome and rendering

| Switch | When | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| tannin extracts (quebracho, chestnut) **(m)** | 19th c. | tanning freed from local bark; imported extracts |
| **chrome tanning** | described by Knapp in 1858; commercial from about 1878; patented in the US 1884 | tanning in days instead of months; leather moves to chemical works; about **80%** of leather is now chrome-tanned (15 to 18% vegetable) |
| machine shoes and belts **(m)** | 19th c. | leather as an industrial input (machine belts, harness, boots for armies) |
| plastics and synthetics **(m)** | 20th c. | leather's uses shrink to shoes, upholstery, luxury goods |
| **rendering** | 19th to 21st c. | everything not eaten gathered into one industry |

About **half of a modern meat animal is not eaten** (about 49% of a steer's live weight, 44% of a hog's). In North America rendering
takes about **27.5 Mt of raw material a year** (other estimates run higher) and turns it into fats, proteins and meals for feed, pet
food, soap, oleochemicals, biodiesel and fertiliser. Rendering is the medieval chandler, glue-boiler, soap-boiler, horner and
bone-grinder fused into one continuous plant beside the slaughterhouse: **by-product capture completed**. Its chain merged with
energy (biofuels), chemistry (oleochemicals), animal feed (and, through feed, with the BSE crisis **(m)**) and waste management.

**Links:** herds and hunting (hides, fat), woods (bark), rock (lime), water (pits, washing), farming (dung, harness), travel (packs,
saddles, harness), metal (the bellows), light (tallow), building (hair and glue in plaster), writing (parchment), and the military
(boots, belts, armour).

## 7. Fishing and hunting as economies

### 7.1 Fishing before the industrial fleets

| Way | Capital | Shape | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| by hand, spear, line | almost none | shallow | everyone's fallback |
| **wicker traps, eel baskets** | withies, a few days' making | accretive | the coppice into the water |
| **nets** | cord (flax, hemp), many hours of netting | long skill | seines, drift nets |
| **the fish weir** | stakes, wattle, stones, a crew; maintenance | paradigm switch, site-bound, commons | takes a share of every run; blocks boats and the fish of those upstream; Magna Carta ordered weirs removed from the Thames and Medway **(m)** |
| **fishponds and stews** | digging, dams, sluices | keeping instead of catching | high-status: manors, castles, monasteries; "living larders"; carp later **(m)** |
| boats and sea fishing | a boat, a crew, nets | capital | coastal villages; shares in the catch |
| **salt fish** | salt (a chokepoint), barrels | assembly | herring and cod as the staple of fast days |

**Herring** shows the switch from the shore to the sea. The medieval Baltic herring fair at Scania and the Hanseatic trade in salt
herring **(m)** gave way to the **Dutch buss**, a decked vessel of about **15 crew and a 50 ft keel** that gutted (gibbed) and salted
the catch at sea in barrels, so it could stay out for weeks. Dutch fleets went to the Dogger Bank and Shetland in hundreds (**400 to
500** ships in one account; a 1614 English pamphlet claimed a thousand, likely inflated). The herring was a regulated national
industry with standards of gutting and packing, and its **assembly** (salt from the Biscay coast, barrels of staves, nets of hemp,
ships of Baltic timber) tied the Dutch economy to half of Europe. A last of herring was about a tonne.

Cod dried (stockfish) or salted from Norway, Iceland and later Newfoundland ran a parallel chain **(m)**. Both made fish a long-
distance commodity carried with salt, wood and grain.

### 7.2 Industrial fisheries and aquaculture

| Switch | When **(m)** | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| steam trawlers, the otter trawl | 1880s to 1890s | the seabed swept; fishing becomes a capital fleet with wage crews |
| ice and railways | 19th c. | fresh fish to inland cities; the fish train |
| diesel, sonar, synthetic nets, freezer and factory trawlers | 20th c. | distant-water fleets; catches far beyond the fish's recovery |
| exclusive economic zones | 1970s to 1980s | the sea enclosed by states |
| quotas and tradable shares | late 20th c. | the commons privatised as rights to a share of the catch |
| **aquaculture** | late 20th c. on | keeping instead of catching, at scale |

The **northern cod** off Newfoundland is the cautionary chain: harvestable biomass fell **82% from 1962 to 1977**, the spawning stock
was about **1% of its estimated peak** by 1992, and the **moratorium of 2 July 1992** put some **30,000 to 40,000** people out of work in
hundreds of communities. It is the weir and the commons at the scale of an ocean.

In **2022** aquaculture overtook capture fisheries for aquatic animals for the first time: **94.4 Mt (51%)** farmed against **92.3 Mt**
caught (81 Mt marine, 11.3 Mt inland); total output with algae was 223.2 Mt. Capture has been roughly flat for thirty years while
aquaculture has grown about **5% a year** since 2000. The medieval fishpond became the dominant form, and it merged with the feed
industry (fish meal, soy), with rendering (feed fats), and with coastal land use (mangroves, salmon pens).

### 7.3 Hunting: rights, parks and warrens

Medieval hunting was less a source of food than of **status and right**. The chain shows the commons becoming property:

| Form | Who | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| hunting the wild | everyone in prehistory; later only where rights allowed | commons, long skill |
| the **forest** (in law) | the crown: forest law over whole districts, protecting the deer and their cover, with its own courts **(m)** | chokepoint by law |
| **chases** and **free warren** | lords granted rights over game **(m)** | privileged right |
| **deer parks** | lords: about **3,200 parks** in England by 1300, about **2% of the land**, usually over 100 acres, enclosed by a **pale** of fence, bank and hedge, or wall | paradigm switch to keeping; capital in the pale |
| **rabbit warrens** | introduced by the Normans by about 1100; manors and monasteries; leased warrens in the Breckland | keeping instead of catching; meat and fur for sale; a luxury, later a bulk good **(m)** |
| **falconry** | the aristocracy | long skill; status good; birds traded at high prices **(m)** |
| **poaching** | everyone else | the contest over the commons that law took away |

**Who hunted and for whom:** the lord hunted for display, sport and the table; his foresters, parkers, warreners and huntsmen were a
staff of specialists; the peasant snared and poached for food and was punished for it. The park was a **paradigm switch to keeping**
(the deer fed, guarded and culled rather than chased) built on **capital sunk in the pale** and enforced by **law and keepers**. It is
the same switch as fishing's from the run to the pond, and both moved meat from a commons to a lord's store. The rabbit is the most
complete example: a luxury kept in a guarded warren in the 13th century, a common pest of the countryside by the 19th **(m)**.

**Later arc:** game management and the shooting estate (the 19th-century grouse moor and pheasant cover, with gamekeepers and the game
laws **(m)**), the decline of subsistence hunting in industrial countries, commercial fur trapping as a global chain (the North
American fur trade **(m)**), and today licensed hunting as recreation and wildlife management.

## 8. How the chains merged

| Shared bottleneck | Chains that met on it | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **oak** (bark, charcoal, timber) | tanning, iron, building, ships | competition for the wood; long leases; navies regulating oak; coke and chrome each released one claimant |
| **the stream** (a fall of water) | corn mill, fulling mill, bellows and hammers, tanning, retting, fish weirs | one site, many claims; the lord's mill rights; disputes over water held and fouled |
| **the kiln** (heat at scale) | lime, brick, tile, pottery, malting, salt boiling | fuel-bound, increasing returns; then coal; now cement as a top industry |
| **the carcass** | food, leather, light, glue, horn, bone, felt, fertiliser | many trades round one butcher; then rendering absorbed them |
| **salt** | fish, meat, cheese, hides (curing before tanning) | a regional chokepoint carried far; the herring and cod chains ran on it |
| **cord** (flax, hemp) | nets, rope, sails, sacks, linen | flax and hemp as cash crops tied to fisheries and navies |
| **the iron edge** | every tool chain | the smith as dependency; later the machine-tool industry |
| **coal** | iron, lime, brick, salt, glass, home heat | the woods' claims moved underground, to the coalfields; the regional economy reshaped round them |
| **oil** (from the 20th c.) | fibres, dyes, plastics replacing leather, fishing fleets, fertiliser | the modern equivalent of the oak: one source feeding many chains |

**The pattern:** a shared bottleneck makes chains compete, then a substitute for the bottleneck (coal for wood, chrome for bark, oil
for tallow and wool) releases one chain, which grows explosively and relocates, while the old shared source loses its buyers and its
commons decays. Merger is the reverse: when several chains' waste or inputs are of one kind, an industry forms to gather them
(rendering, scrap, chemicals from coal tar).

## 9. What a simulator could take from this

**Chains that matter most** (ranked by how much social structure they generate per good added):

1. **The woods as time capital and commons (coppice with standards).** One parcel state with a rotation gives withies, poles, bark and
   charcoal wood as joint products, with timber from standards over a longer horizon; browsing herds damage the regrowth, so fencing
   and a calendar are needed. This is economy_web.md §2.6 and should stay the first priority: it feeds building, fishing, leather and
   metal at once.
2. **Metal as an assembly bound to fuel.** Keep ore as a chokepoint, but make **charcoal the real constraint**: historically 60 to 150
   wood per metal by weight for a bloomery, against about 5 timber per copper in the engine. A heavier charcoal input (say 3 to 5
   charcoal per copper, calibrate) would make smelters need a wood, a collier and a rotation, and set the woods' commons against the
   anvil, as they were.
3. **The carcass as joint products feeding leather and light.** Hide into leather (time capital: weeks to months in tan pits, bark and
   lime), tallow into light, bone and horn into small tools, offal into bait. The tan pit is a fitting that locks capital up over
   nights, which the engine's batch-over-nights mechanism can already express.
4. **The river as one site with many claims.** The mill, a fulling or hammer wheel, the tan yard, retting and the weir all want the same
   stretch, and each harms the others or those below. Brief 5 has the weir; the rest can be claims on a water parcel with a downstream
   effect.
5. **Building as the sink for surplus, with fire as a commons.** The engine has the substitutes; add the roof as a choice (thatch:
   cheap, burns; tile or slate: needs a kiln or a quarry) and a fire spread that makes one household's thatch the street's risk.

**Goods to add (or keep proposed):** withies, poles and bark (from the coppice); leather; cord (bast or nettle, then flax); bone (with
horn and antler as one good); lime (one good for mortar, fields and tanning); later wool and cloth, iron. Keep each one only if it
passes 02zzh §6's test (changes a decision, ties a chain to a place, skill or capital).

**Goods and steps to aggregate:**
- all the fine leathers (tawed, oil-tanned, curried) into one **leather**; currying folded into tanning's skill;
- all roofing into a property of the building (thatch, shingle, tile) rather than goods;
- carding, combing, spinning, weaving, fulling, dyeing into **two** steps if cloth is added: **yarn** (household labour, the bottleneck)
  and **cloth** (a loom, a specialist; fulling as an optional mill that raises quality); dyes and alum left out;
- tin, lead, silver and bronze left out unless a world needs coin from a different metal; **iron** could replace copper as the common
  metal (ore common, charcoal binding) with copper kept as the scarcer metal of coin and ornament;
- herring, cod and eel as **fish**, with salt fish as the kept form;
- deer parks and warrens as one **keeping** paradigm (a pale round a wood or a warren on a heath) when kept beasts arrive.

**Paradigm switches worth modelling as institutions rather than better tools:** the fulling mill and the hammer mill (as the corn mill:
a lord's site and a rent); the blast furnace (a works needing a wood lease and a hired crew: the first enterprise larger than a
household); putting-out (a merchant owning material in process, paying households by the piece: needs only agreements and stores,
which the engine has); the deer park (a commons enclosed and guarded, with poaching as its contest); the weir and later the quota (a
share of a run as a right).

**What to leave out (or keep far later):**
- the industrial switches (coke, Bessemer, chrome, synthetic fibres, steam trawling, rendering, Portland cement) as built mechanics:
  they belong to scenarios of the later parts of this folder. Their **shape** is what to keep in mind: a substitute for a shared
  bottleneck releases one chain, moves it to a new resource and lets the old commons decay;
- the many specialised guilds of one chain (tanner, currier, cordwainer; mason and freemason); one trade per chain is enough, with
  skill doing the rest;
- dye plants, mordants and the finishing trades of cloth;
- falconry, forest law's courts, and the detailed game laws, until a world has lords to hold them;
- exact historical ratios: they are a guide to which inputs bind (charcoal for metal, hours for cloth, time for leather, the fall
  of water for power), not values to copy.

**Two metrics to read against the null arm:** whether the woods are cut on a rotation or stripped (a coppice kept or lost), and
whether the deep steps (tanning, smelting, a mill) become one household's specialism serving others for a rent or a price.
