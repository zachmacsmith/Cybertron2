## Summary

The pre-industrial world held several economies as large and as deep as Europe's, and for long stretches larger. Imperial Rome
fed a city of perhaps a million on wheat shipped from Egypt and Africa, ate Spanish olive oil from tens of millions of amphorae,
and smelted so much lead and silver that its output can be read in Greenland ice. Song China (960 to 1279) made iron in tens of
thousands of tonnes a year with coal and blast furnaces, moved rice north on a canal more than 1,700 km long, and was the first
state to issue paper money. India clothed much of the Old World in cotton with fast colours no one else could match; the Islamic
world ran transferable credit across three continents and carried paper, sugar and irrigation west; the Inca fed an empire of
millions with no markets and no money, from storehouses on mountainsides; and West African gold supplied the Mediterranean's coin
for centuries. The same few problems (feeding a capital, moving bulk, paying an army, trusting a stranger far away, holding a
chokepoint) were solved in very different ways, and each way made a different society.

These systems were joined long before 1500 by relay trade along the Silk Roads, across the Sahara and around the Indian Ocean,
each segment run by its own merchants, institutions and seasons. After 1500 American silver, mined by Andean forced labour with
mercury from Spain and Peru, flowed both ways round the world and ended largely in China, which had made silver its tax money;
American crops remade the food base of Europe, Africa and China; and sugar, moving west from India through the Islamic world to
the Atlantic, became the plantation and slave system. The comparison with Europe is not one of backwardness: China and India
reached high output, fine specialisation and wide markets on an organic energy base. What none did (and Europe did only late,
helped by coal and colonies) was switch to mineral energy. For a simulator it offers shapes and institutions the medieval
village frame lacks: state procurement and monopoly, redistribution without markets, water in time shares, transferable credit,
relay trade, monsoon pulses, and output read from what a society leaves behind.

## 1. Classical antiquity

### 1.1 A precedent: merchants on credit at Kanesh

Long before coins, Old Assyrian merchants of Assur (about 1950 to 1750 BC) sent tin and woollen textiles by donkey caravans of
tens of animals some 1,000 km to Kanesh in Anatolia, sold them for silver and gold, and sent the metal home to buy more. Over
22,000 of their letters and contracts survive: family firms with branch offices, pooled-capital partnerships, interest-bearing
loans, tolls paid to the city-states en route [14]. Merchant credit, partnership and agency are Bronze Age institutions, not
medieval Italian inventions.

### 1.2 Grain to Rome: the annona

Rome under Augustus had perhaps 800,000 to a million people *(rough)*, far more than its hinterland could feed.

| Item | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Rome's yearly grain need | about 150,000 t (Erdkamp, a minimum), 200,000 t (Rickman), 400,000 t (Casson) *(contested)* | [1] |
| Egypt's contribution | 20 million modii (about 135,000 t) a year, said of Augustus's time | Epitome de Caesaribus 1.6 [1] |
| Africa against Egypt | Africa fed Rome "eight months of the year", Egypt four | Josephus, Jewish War 2.383–386 [2] |
| Free grain | 5 modii a month to about 200,000 citizens under Augustus (about 80,000 t a year) | Res Gestae 15 [1] |
| Ships | 340 t common; the Isis in Lucian's Navigium perhaps 1,000 t or more *(rough)* | Casson [1] |

The state did not own the fleet. The **cura annonae** under a prefect levied or bought grain (much of Egypt's was tax in kind),
contracted private shipowners (**navicularii**), and stored grain in warehouses (horrea) at Ostia and Portus. Emperors bought ships
in with privileges: Claudius insured shippers against storm losses (Suetonius, Claudius 18–19), and a Junian Latin who built a
ship of 10,000 modii and carried grain to Rome for six years earned citizenship (Gaius, Institutes 1.32c). Claudius and Trajan
built the harbour at Portus for the big ships. The free grain was under half of what the city ate; the rest was sold. So the
annona was a **hybrid**: state demand and guarantees calling a private shipping industry into being, which also carried private
cargo.

```
Bread in Rome  (assembly; pulse; chokepoint at the harbour)
├── wheat: Egyptian Nile farms (one flood crop; tax in kind) + African estates (rent, tax) + Sicily, Sardinia
├── shipping (navicularii, private) ← timber, pitch, sailcloth, crews; state privileges and insurance
│   └── sailing season about April to October → a year's supply in six months: a pulse
├── harbours: Puteoli, then Ostia and Portus ← state works, dredging, lighthouses
├── Tiber barges (towed by men and oxen) → horrea, state and private
├── milling (donkey or slave mills) and baking (the bakers' guild, later state-regulated)
└── distribution: the frumentatio lists (citizens with tickets) + the market
```

Whoever held Egypt held Rome's bread, which is why Augustus ruled Egypt through his own prefect and barred senators from it.

### 1.3 Oil and wine in amphorae

Amphorae survive and can be traced to their kilns, which makes them the best evidence of ancient bulk trade. **Monte Testaccio**
in Rome is a hill about 35 m high made of broken oil jars, mostly the globular **Dressel 20** from Baetica (the Guadalquivir
valley), dumped from about AD 140 to 260 because oil-soaked sherds could not be reused. Estimates run from 20 to 25 million
amphorae (a gravimetric study) to 53 million (the older, often repeated figure) *(contested)*; at about 70 litres each, 1.4 to 6
billion litres of oil [3]. It holds only the state-handled share of one route. Painted labels name the estate, shipper and the
officials who checked the weight: the hill is in effect a customs archive.

```
Olive oil to Rome and the army (time capital; assembly)
├── groves (15 to 20 years to full bearing) ← land, tenants and slaves, peace
├── lever and screw presses ← stone, timber
├── amphora kilns on the river (estate stamps) ← clay, wood fuel, potters
├── barges down the Guadalquivir → Hispalis (Seville) → sea shipping
├── state purchase for the annona and the frontier armies (Rhine, Britain)
└── Monte Testaccio: the dump for jars that cannot be cleaned
```

Shipwrecks give a second index. A. J. Parker's 1992 catalogue of 1,189 Mediterranean wrecks peaks in the 1st century BC; Andrew
Wilson's probability-weighted reworking moves the peak to the 1st century AD, with a sharp fall after [9]. Most historians read
the curve as a rough index of seaborne trade, with caveats (organic cargoes vanish; sailing habits changed).

### 1.4 Mining, metals and the record in the ice

Rome's money rested on Iberian mines: silver and lead at Carthago Nova and in the Sierra Morena, copper and silver at **Rio
Tinto**, gold in the north-west (Las Médulas, where aqueducts brought water to wash hillsides away: Pliny, Natural History
33.70–78).

- Polybius, quoted by Strabo (3.2.10), says the mines near Carthago Nova employed **40,000 workers** and yielded the Roman people
  **25,000 drachmae a day** [7].
- At Rio Tinto a 1924 company survey estimated about 15 million tonnes of ancient lead-silver slag and 1 million of copper slag;
  other estimates range from 6 to 16 million tonnes depending on what is counted *(contested)* [6].
- **Lead in Greenland ice.** Smelting lead and silver puts lead into the air, and some reaches the Arctic. Hong and colleagues
  (1994) first found Roman-era lead in Greenland ice about four times the natural level [5]. McConnell and colleagues (2018)
  measured the NGRIP core year by year from 1100 BC to AD 800: emissions rose with Carthaginian and Roman mining in Iberia,
  peaked under the early Empire, fell with the late Republic's civil wars and with the Antonine plague (from AD 165), and moved
  with the denarius's silver content. They read it as evidence of **sustained growth in the first two centuries of the Empire**
  [4].

```
Silver coin (chokepoint; assembly; joint products)
├── ore: lead-silver galena; jarosite at Rio Tinto ← a seam (place-bound)
├── labour: slaves and convicts underground; contractors on leased shafts (the Vipasca tablets set the lease rules)
├── drainage: water-lifting wheels in series, worked by men ← timber, bronze, carpentry
├── fuel: charcoal in huge volume ← the woods around the mines
├── smelting → lead bullion → cupellation → silver, plus lead (pipes, sheeting) as a by-product
├── state ownership, leased to contractors (publicani, then imperial procurators)
└── mints → denarii → army pay → taxes → back to the state
```

### 1.5 Coin, tax and the cost of land transport

The denarius (about 3.9 g, nearly pure, under Augustus) carried a monetary economy that reached peasants in many provinces. Nero
cut its weight and fineness (AD 64); by the 260s its successor held a few per cent silver *(rough)*, and prices rose steeply.
Keith Hopkins's **taxes and trade** model [10] is the most useful mechanism for a designer: the state taxed the rich inner
provinces in money and spent it in Rome and on the frontier armies; to get coin for next year's tax, the provinces had to sell
goods where the coin was spent; so **a money tax forced trade** in a circuit. The same mechanism recurs in Mughal India (§3.4)
and Ming China (§6.3).

Duncan-Jones's reading of Diocletian's Price Edict (301) puts carriage by sea, river and road at roughly **1 : 5 : 30 to 60**
*(rough)* [10]. Wheat carted 300 Roman miles roughly doubled in price; shipped from Alexandria to Rome it rose by a fraction. The
empire was a sea economy; inland bulk moved only on rivers.

### 1.6 Slavery's role

Walter Scheidel puts the slave population of Italy at its height at about **1 to 1.5 million** in perhaps 6 to 7.5 million
people (older figures said 2 to 3 million), and about **10 per cent** of the empire as a lower bound [8] *(contested)*. Slaves
worked the mines (the worst lot), the big estates of Italy and Sicily, households, and skilled trades and business, where they
often ran shops, ships and money for their owners as agents (with a peculium, a fund they managed and could buy freedom with).
Freedmen were a large part of the urban commercial class. After the conquests, supply fell back on breeding and frontier
capture, and from the 2nd to 4th centuries the slave estate gave way to tenants (coloni) bound to the land.

### 1.7 Building: Roman concrete

**Opus caementicium** mixed lime with volcanic ash (pozzolana, from Puteoli) and rubble. The ash reacts with lime to set hard
even under water, so harbour moles could be cast in the sea. It lasts because minerals keep growing in it (aluminous tobermorite
and phillipsite in marine concrete: Jackson and others 2017) and because lime clasts from hot mixing heal cracks (Seymour and
others 2023) [11]. It let semi-skilled gangs build vaults and domes cheaply inside brick facing: the Pantheon's unreinforced dome
(about AD 126) is 43.3 m across, the world's largest for over a thousand years.

```
Concrete vaults (assembly; long skill at the top)
├── lime ← limestone + kilns + wood fuel
├── pozzolana ← ash pits near Naples (a regional chokepoint; crushed brick as a weaker substitute elsewhere)
├── rubble aggregate; light pumice for the upper courses of a dome
├── brick facing ← brickyards, many owned by senators and the emperor (stamped)
└── timber formwork ← carpenters; gangs of free workers and slaves under contractors (redemptores)
```

### 1.8 Greece: coin, silver and grain

- **Coin** was first struck in Lydia and Ionia in the late 7th century BC in electrum, then in gold and silver under Croesus;
  by the 5th century the Athenian silver tetradrachm ("owl") was the Aegean's common money.
- **Laurion.** A rich silver strike in Attica in 483/2 BC was spent, at Themistocles's urging, on a fleet of about 200 triremes
  that won at Salamis (Herodotus 7.144) [12]. The state leased the mines to contractors who worked them with slaves; Thucydides
  (7.27) says over 20,000 slaves, mostly craftsmen, fled Athens when Sparta fortified Decelea in 413. Rowers paid in coin from a
  mine dug by slaves: one loop of silver, labour and sea power.
- **Grain.** Athens imported grain from the Black Sea, Egypt and Sicily; Demosthenes (20.31–32) says about 400,000 medimnoi
  (roughly 12,000 to 16,000 t) a year came from the Bosporan kingdom [13]. Athens policed the Hellespont route, forbade its
  citizens to ship grain elsewhere, and set grain wardens over the market. How dependent it was is debated (Garnsey: less than
  once thought).

### 1.9 What kind of economy?

A century of argument between "primitivists" (Moses Finley: status, not markets; cities consumed and did not produce) and
"modernists" has settled in between: real, integrated markets for grain, oil, wine, labour and credit (Peter Temin), some growth
per head in the late Republic and early Empire, but on an organic base with no sustained rise in productivity [10]. The evidence
is itself a lesson: Rome's output is measured from what it threw away and what drifted into the air (ice-core lead, wrecks,
jars, coin hoards), each a partial index of one activity.


## 2. China

### 2.1 The Han salt and iron monopolies

China smelted **cast iron** in blast furnaces from about the 5th century BC, over 1,500 years before Europe did at scale, and in
the Han made steel by decarburising cast iron (chaogang, a puddling process). In 117 BC Emperor Wu, needing money for wars with
the Xiongnu, approved a **state monopoly of salt and iron** run by Sang Hongyang, a merchant's son turned minister [15]. The Han
shu lists some four dozen iron offices *(rough)*; archaeology finds big state ironworks just outside Han city walls, worked by
conscripts and convicts [15]. In 81 BC a court debate, written up as the **Discourses on Salt and Iron**, set Confucian scholars
(the monopolies compete with the people for profit, force poor, brittle tools on peasants at fixed prices, corrupt officials)
against the ministers (they pay for defence and stop great merchants dominating) [15]. A wine monopoly was dropped; salt and iron
stayed, and the salt monopoly remained a fiscal pillar of Chinese states for two thousand years: **a state taking a chokepoint as
revenue**, and the argument that follows.

```
Han iron tools (assembly; increasing returns; a chokepoint made by law)
├── ore + charcoal (coal later, in the north) ← mines and woods near the works
├── blast furnaces; bellows by men and horses, then water (Du Shi's water-powered bellows, AD 31)
├── clay and iron moulds for mass-cast ploughshares and hoes; annealing; chaogang steel ← iron masters
├── labour: conscripts, convicts, hired men
└── sale at fixed prices through state offices → peasants' tools; the army's weapons
```

### 2.2 The Song "economic revolution"

From about 900 to 1200 China's economy changed so much that historians (Shiba Yoshinobu, Mark Elvin, Robert Hartwell) call it a
revolution. Its parts reinforced each other:

| Part | What changed | Figures |
|---|---|---|
| **Rice** | The south overtook the north: wet rice with transplanting, irrigation by treadle pumps, night soil and canal mud as manure. | population passed about 100 million around 1100 *(rough)* |
| **Champa rice** | Early-ripening, drought-tolerant rice from Champa (central Vietnam) via Fujian; after the 1012 drought in the lower Yangzi and Huai, Emperor Zhenzong had 30,000 bushels of seed sent out [20]. It allowed a second crop, or a crop on higher land. | its origin is debated in genomic studies [20] |
| **Canals** | The Grand Canal, begun by the Sui (from 605), joined the Yangzi to the north; the Yuan extended it to Beijing. | about 1,776 km; 3 to 4 million shi of tax grain a year in Ming and Qing [19] *(rough)* |
| **Coal and iron** | Coal replaced charcoal in northern ironworks and heated the capital, Kaifeng. | 1078 output 75,000 t (Hartwell 1962) to 125,000 t (his later figure); "no reliable calculation is possible" (Wagner) [16][17] *(contested)* |
| **Money** | Copper cash in billions; iron cash in Sichuan; then paper. | minting peaked at roughly 5 to 6 billion coins a year around 1080 *(rough)* [18] |
| **Commerce** | The walled ward-and-market city gave way to streets of shops, night markets and guilds; ships to Southeast Asia and India from Quanzhou and Guangzhou. | Kaifeng, then Hangzhou, near a million people *(rough)* |

**Iron (Hartwell's estimates).** Hartwell took the iron delivered to the state in tax records, assumed it was a 10 per cent tax on
part of the industry, and multiplied: 75,000 tons in 1962, about 125,000 in later work, a sixfold rise since 800, comparable to
or above Britain's 1788 output of about 76,000 tons. Donald Wagner accepts the picture (a large, partly private, coal-using iron
industry in Hebei and Henan, two of whose prefectures supplied over 75 per cent of the state's iron deliveries in 1078) but
rejects the multiplier [17]; the 125,000 t figure is about 1.2 kg a head. Iron went into arms for armies of a million, farm
tools, salt pans, Sichuan's coins and building. Output fell after the Jurchen conquest of the
north (1126), the Mongol wars and the Yellow River's change of course (1194).

```
Northern Song iron (assembly; paradigm switch from charcoal to coal; increasing returns)
├── ore ← Cizhou and Xingzhou (Hebei) and other prefectures
├── coal from shafts, replacing charcoal as the northern woods ran out ← miners; carts and canals
├── blast furnaces; water-driven bellows (described by Wang Zhen, 1313)
├── refining to wrought iron and steel ← masters; private works under state licence and quota
└── buyers: the state's armies, Kaifeng's builders, farmers; carried by the Bian canal
```

**Paper money.** Sichuan's coins were iron and too heavy to carry for trade, so merchant houses there began issuing deposit
receipts, **jiaozi**, around 1000; the state suppressed them and took over issue in 1023 to 1024 with an official office at
Chengdu [18]. Notes had a term, a fixed issue and a cash reserve. Over-issue for war depreciated them; the Southern Song's
**huizi** (from the 1160s) depreciated badly in the wars with the Jin and Mongols; the Yuan made paper the main money and
over-issued it too. The Ming returned in the 15th century to copper and, above all, **uncoined silver by weight**, which set up
the silver demand of §6.3. The arc is complete: heavy money, private receipts, a state monopoly of issue, fiscal over-issue, lost
trust, a return to metal.

### 2.3 Porcelain: Jingdezhen

Jingdezhen in Jiangxi was the world's porcelain city from the Song on, with the imperial kiln (from 1369 or 1402) among hundreds
of private ones. Its two raw materials lay nearby: **kaolin** (named after Gaoling hill) and **petuntse** (china stone),
crushed by water-powered trip hammers on the streams and shipped to town as white bricks. Blue-and-white ware, begun under the
Yuan, used **cobalt** imported from Persia (Qamsar, near Kashan); when the supply failed, potters used duller local cobalt [21].
Song Yingxing's Tiangong kaiwu (1637) says a cup passes through seventy-two hands; the Jesuit d'Entrecolles, writing from the
town in 1712, reported some 3,000 kilns and nearly a million people *(reported, probably exaggerated)*.

```
Jingdezhen blue-and-white (assembly; long skill split across many trades; one imported chokepoint)
├── body: kaolin + china stone ← hill quarries; water-powered trip hammers; settling tanks; bricks
├── glaze: china stone + lime-and-fern ash ← lime kilns
├── cobalt: Persian (via Mongol and sea trade) or local (dull): substitutes with a trade-off
├── throwing, moulding, trimming, painting, glazing: separate workshops; saggar-making a trade of its own
├── firing: egg-shaped or dragon kilns ← pine wood in vast amounts, fetched from ever further away
├── transport: Chang river → Poyang lake → the Yangzi, or overland to Guangzhou and Quanzhou
└── buyers: the court, the empire, Japan, Southeast Asia, the Islamic world (to Persian taste), Europe after 1600
```

The imperial kiln shows **the state as a quality-setting customer** inside a private industry: it commissioned designs, drew the
best workers and passed its rejects to the market.

### 2.4 Silk

```
Silk cloth (time capital; long skill; joint products)
├── mulberry trees (years to grow; leaves picked several times a season) ← dikes and field edges
├── silkworms: egg cards over winter; a month's rearing in a warm room by women; leaves fed hourly at the end (a labour pulse)
├── cocoons → boiling, reeling the filament (skilled hours at a reel) → raw silk → twisting, dyeing
├── weaving: plain cloth in peasant homes; damask and brocade on drawlooms in Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing
│   └── imperial weaving bureaus in those cities under the Ming and Qing
└── sale: tax silk (households paid tax in cloth for centuries); markets; overland and sea export; after 1570 for silver
```

In the Pearl River delta and round Lake Tai the **mulberry-dike fish pond** closed a loop: ponds dug for fish, the spoil heaped
into dikes planted with mulberry, leaves fed to silkworms, their droppings fed to the fish, and the pond mud dredged back onto
the dikes. It is **joint products** at landscape scale.

### 2.5 Why no industrial revolution followed

Song China had coal-fired iron, water-powered machines (Wang Zhen describes a water-driven hemp-spinning frame in 1313), paper
money, print and a national market. Why it did not go on to sustained, machine-driven growth (Needham's question) has many
answers [22]:

| Explanation | Who | The argument | Weak point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shocks** | Hartwell | conquest, floods and plague broke the northern coal and iron region; the economy moved south, far from coal | explains Song decline, not Ming and Qing |
| **High-level equilibrium trap** | Elvin (1973) | cheap labour and refined traditional skills meant labour-saving machines never paid | Ming–Qing wages and invention are disputed |
| **Coal and colonies** | Pomeranz (2000) | Jiangnan around 1750 matched England; England diverged because its coal lay near industry and colonies supplied land-hungry goods | Jiangnan's output per worker was lower (Broadberry, Guan and Li) |
| **Political economy** | Rosenthal and Wong (2011) | a big, peaceful, low-tax state favoured cheap rural industry; Europe's wars pushed industry into walled cities with dear labour and cheap capital | hard to test |
| **Relative prices** | Allen | machines pay where labour is dear and fuel cheap; in China the reverse | |
| **Knowledge** | Lin (1995); Mokyr | examinations drew talent to classics; no open, cumulative culture of experiment | China had a large technical literature |
| **Early divergence** | Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) | output per head peaked in the Northern Song; behind Italy by about 1300, Britain by 1400 | thin data *(contested)* |

The common ground: high output and wide markets on an **organic** base (02zzi §1) are not enough. The switch needs cheap,
concentrated energy where the industry is and a reason (dear labour) to replace people with machines.

## 3. India

### 3.1 Cotton textiles and the Indian Ocean

India was the world's great cloth maker from antiquity to about 1800: Gujarat (coarse and printed cottons for the Red Sea, East
Africa and Southeast Asia), the Coromandel coast (painted chintz), Bengal (the fine muslins of Dhaka) and the Deccan. Pliny
already complained that India, China and Arabia took 100 million sesterces a year from the empire (Natural History 12.84) [42].
In the 17th and 18th centuries textiles were about two-thirds or more of the value of the English East India Company's Asian
imports [23]; the calico craze in England led wool and silk interests to win the **Calico Acts** (1700, 1721), banning printed
calico imports and then most cotton sales [23]. Indian cloth was also a main currency of the Atlantic slave trade, exchanged for
captives in West Africa (Riello [23]). Maddison gives India about a quarter of world output in 1700 *(contested; a total output
share)* [24]; Broadberry and Gupta show Indian output per head falling behind Britain's from 1600 [23].

```
Indian painted and printed cotton (assembly; long skill; dispersed households under merchant advances)
├── raw cotton (many local kinds) ← black-soil fields
├── churka roller gin → bowing → spinning on the charkha (women at home); yarn fineness set by the spinner's skill
├── weaving: caste weavers on pit looms in villages and towns
├── bleaching by washermen in river water with lye and sun
├── dyeing and printing: mordants (alum, iron) fix colours; madder and chay root (red), indigo (blue); resist and hand painting
├── finance: merchants advance cash to weavers before the season (dadni) ← bankers (shroffs) and bills (hundi)
├── ports: Surat, Masulipatnam, Hughli, Calicut; ships sail on the monsoon, so cloth must be ready by a date (a pulse)
└── buyers: Southeast Asia (for spices), East Africa (gold, ivory), the Gulf and Red Sea, Europe, West Africa (captives)
```

The **dadni** system is proto-industry (02zzi §3.6) centuries before Europe's: merchants advanced capital to dispersed household
producers and collected the cloth, all carried on a credit network of shroffs and hundis.

### 3.2 Indigo and dyes

Indigo leaves were fermented in one vat, the liquor beaten in a second to oxidise it, and the blue sediment pressed into cakes:
light and valuable, ideal for long trade. The best came from Bayana near Agra and Sarkhej near Ahmedabad. In Europe woad interests
won bans and duties on indigo in the 16th and 17th centuries, which the stronger dye eventually broke: substitutes decided as much
by politics as by chemistry. Under the British, indigo moved to Bengal plantations on forced contracts (the Indigo Revolt, 1859 to
1860); synthetic indigo (BASF, 1897) ended the trade.

### 3.3 Wootz steel

South India and Sri Lanka made **crucible steel**: iron and a carbon source (wood, leaves) sealed in small clay crucibles, held
at heat for hours and cooled slowly, giving a cake of high-carbon steel (about 1 to 2 per cent carbon) with a banded structure
[26]. Crucibles at Kodumanal in Tamil Nadu date to about the 3rd century BC to early centuries AD; in Sri Lanka, Gillian Juleff
found furnaces on hill ridges blown by the monsoon wind. The cakes went across the Arabian Sea to Persia and Syria to be forged at
low heat into the patterned blades Europeans called "Damascus steel". The secret lay in the whole process: ores with traces of
vanadium and other elements, the crucible recipe, slow cooling, low-heat forging (Verhoeven and Pendray). When those ores and
that knowledge broke in the 18th and 19th centuries, the blades could no longer be made [26]: **long skill** with a **chokepoint**
in an ore.

### 3.4 The Mughal economy

The Mughal empire at its height (late 16th to early 18th century) ruled perhaps 110 to 150 million people *(contested)* [25].

- **Revenue in cash.** Under Akbar land was measured and assessed by crop and average prices (zabt), at about a third to a half
  of the produce, collected mostly **in money** (Irfan Habib [25]). Peasants had to sell grain to pay, which pulled the
  countryside into markets and supported dense market towns. Moosvi's reconstruction puts the assessed revenue around 1595 at
  roughly 90 to 100 million rupees *(rough)* [25].
- **Assignments.** Revenue was assigned to officers (mansabdars) as jagirs, rotated every few years so no one rooted himself; they
  kept cavalry and spent in the cities, on luxuries from imperial workshops (karkhanas) and city artisans.
- **Carrying.** Grain moved by the **banjaras**, caste caravanners with pack bullocks in herds of thousands (Peter Mundy reported
  about 14,000 laden oxen in one train in 1632 *(reported)*); rivers carried bulk.
- **Money and bankers.** The silver rupee (Sher Shah, 1540s, about 11 g). India sold cloth and took in silver and gold, a "sink"
  for the world's precious metals; Mughal mint output swelled with American silver. Shroffs tested coin, remitted money by
  **hundi** (bills drawn on correspondents in other cities), and lent to officers against revenue assignments.

It is Hopkins's mechanism again (a money tax forces sales and draws in silver), and **redistribution through a court** (02zzi
§3.3) at the scale of a subcontinent, with markets everywhere beneath.

## 4. The Islamic world

### 4.1 Networks, camels and the funduq

From the 8th century one cultural and legal zone ran from al-Andalus to Central Asia and the Indian Ocean coasts, with Arabic as
the language of trade, shared contract law, gold dinars and silver dirhams, and the yearly pilgrimage moving people and goods.
After the 10th century Cairo, the Red Sea and Aden took the India trade from Baghdad and the Gulf.

- **The camel and the wheel.** Richard Bulliet argued that from about the 3rd to the 6th century camels with a better saddle
  displaced carts across the Middle East and North Africa, being cheaper per load and needing no roads [31]. Land transport became
  caravans of hundreds or thousands of camels moving between watered stops.
- **The caravanserai, khan and funduq**: walled inns on the routes and in cities, with a courtyard for beasts and goods, rooms
  above, one guarded gate. In ports the funduq was also warehouse, customs point and residence for foreign merchants of one
  nation (Venice had one at Alexandria, and copied the form at home in the Fondaco dei Tedeschi). Many were built and kept by
  **waqf**, a perpetual endowment, so a road's inns and markets were paid for by rents on endowed property.
- **The muhtasib**, a market inspector, enforced weights, measures, the prices of necessities and honest dealing (hisba): the
  market's rules, visible and enforced on the spot.

### 4.2 Paper

Paper, Chinese in origin (Cai Lun's improvement, AD 105), reached Samarkand by the 8th century (the story that prisoners from the
battle of Talas in 751 brought it is a legend). A paper mill worked in Baghdad by about 794 to 795; Damascus, Cairo and Fez
followed; al-Idrisi in the 12th century praised the paper of Xàtiva in al-Andalus; Christian Europe's first major centre was
Fabriano by the 1270s [27]. Jonathan Bloom argues that paper transformed the Islamic world before print: cheap writing made
bureaucracy, accounts, commercial letters and books in quantity practical [27].

```
Paper, Islamic method (assembly; increasing returns with water power; a substitute for papyrus and parchment)
├── fibre: linen and hemp rags (China used mulberry bark, bamboo, hemp) ← a used-cloth trade
├── retting rags in water → beating to pulp with trip hammers driven by water or animals ← a river
├── forming sheets with a mould dipped in the vat; couching, pressing, drying ← skilled vatmen
├── sizing with starch (Islamic) or gelatine (Fabriano); burnishing with a stone
└── users: chanceries, merchants (letters, accounts, bills), scholars and copyists
```

### 4.3 Sugar

Cane sugar was first crystallised in India (Sanskrit khanda, the root of "candy"), then in Sasanian Persia. Under Islamic rule cane
spread to Iraq, the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, Cyprus, Sicily and al-Andalus. Andrew Watson's **"Arab agricultural
revolution"** (1974) credits Muslim rule with spreading a package of crops (sugar, rice, cotton, citrus, sorghum) and summer
irrigation; Michael Decker (2009) and others argue many were already in the Roman and Sasanian world [29] *(contested)*. Mamluk
Egypt refined sugar at scale and exported it to Europe. Sugar is the chain that passed most directly from the Islamic world to the
colonial one: Crusader states and Venetian Cyprus, then Madeira (1450s), São Tomé, Brazil (the leading producer from the later
16th century) and the Caribbean (from the 1640s), where it became the plantation system run on enslaved African labour.

```
Sugar (assembly; a harvest pulse that cannot wait; increasing returns at the mill; fuel-hungry)
├── cane (12 to 18 months to mature) ← irrigation (Egypt, Iraq) or rain (Brazil, Caribbean)
├── cut cane must be crushed within a day or two or the juice spoils → field and mill one unit
├── crushing: edge-runner stones or roller mills, by animals or water ← timber, stone, iron, millwrights
├── boiling in a train of copper kettles ← fuel in vast amounts (woods cleared; later cane waste)
├── purging in clay cone moulds → loaf sugar; molasses → rum (later)
├── labour: peasants and wage workers in Egypt; enslaved Africans in gangs in the Atlantic
└── buyers: Islamic courts and cities, then Europe, where sugar went from medicine to mass food
```

The cane's need for immediate crushing is why it could not be grown by dispersed peasants for distant mills: field, mill and
boiling house had to be one unit under one command. That technical fact made the plantation.

### 4.4 Qanats

A **qanat** is a gently sloping tunnel that taps groundwater under an alluvial fan and leads it out by gravity to fields
kilometres away, dug from a line of vertical shafts. It began in Iran in the early first millennium BC and spread to Oman (aflaj),
Central Asia (karez), North Africa (foggara) and Spain. The Qasabeh qanat at Gonabad, about 2,500 years old, has a mother well
some 300 m deep and 427 shafts over about 33 km; one count finds about 37,000 qanats still in use in Iran in the 2000s, of many
more built [28] *(counts vary widely)*.

- **Water in time shares.** A qanat's flow was owned in shares of time (so many hours in a cycle of days), measured with a water
  clock (a bowl with a hole sinking in a basin) and run by a water master. Shares were sold, inherited and pledged.
- **Specialists.** Hereditary diggers (muqannis), paid by the owners, dug and cleaned the tunnels.
- **Shape:** time capital (years to dig), increasing returns (one tunnel serves a village), a commons among the owners, long skill
  (surveying a gradient underground), and a chokepoint (the village dies if it collapses).

### 4.5 Credit and partnership

| Instrument | What it was | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| **suftaja** | money paid in one city, repaid in another by the issuer's correspondent | moved value without carrying coin on dangerous roads; jurists debated it because the lender gained safe carriage |
| **hawala** | legal transfer of a debt to another debtor | let debts circulate; the root of the modern informal transfer system of that name |
| **sakk** | a written order to pay (Europe's "cheque") | payment by paper among known bankers |
| **qirad / mudaraba** | one partner gives capital, the other travels and trades; profit shared | joined capital to skill without interest; very like the later Italian commenda (Udovitch [30]) |

**Who enforced them?** Avner Greif argued that the 11th-century **Maghribi traders** of the Cairo Geniza enforced agents' honesty
by collective reputation: cheat one and none would employ you [30]. Edwards and Ogilvie (2012) replied that no such coalition can
be shown and the traders used courts and dealt with outsiders; Greif partly conceded that courts were used; Jessica Goldberg
(2012) argues reputation worked through networks of reciprocal agency rather than a closed club [30]. It is exactly the question
of OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]] (who enforces a promise when the engine does not): reputation, courts and kin, in a mix that changed with distance.

## 5. The Americas and Africa

### 5.1 The Inca: an empire without markets or money

The Inca state (about 1438 to 1533) ruled perhaps 6 to 14 million people *(contested)* along the Andes, with no money, no
writing in the usual sense, no draft animal but the llama, and little market exchange at the imperial level [32].

- **Labour as tax (mit'a).** Households owed turns of labour, not goods: farming state and temple land, herding, building,
  soldiering, carrying. The state fed workers while they worked, with food and maize beer at feasts: hospitality as payment.
- **Cloth as wealth.** Fine cloth, woven by "chosen women" (aqllakuna) and specialist men, was the state's chief gift and store of
  value, given to soldiers, lords and allies.
- **Storage.** Rows of storehouses (**qollqa**) stood on cool, windy hillsides near state centres: Huánuco Pampa had 497, mostly of
  highland tubers (50 to 80 per cent of the space) [32]; Cotapachi in the Cochabamba valley about 2,400 *(reported)* (D'Altroy and
  Earle 1985). Food kept for years as **chuño** (potatoes freeze-dried by night frost and day sun) and **ch'arki** (dried meat).
- **Roads and records.** The Qhapaq Ñan ran some 30,000 to 40,000 km (estimates 23,000 to 60,000) [32], with way stations (tambos)
  a day apart, stocked from the qollqa, and relay runners (chasqui). Labour owed and goods stored were recorded on knotted cords
  (**khipu**).
- **Vertical archipelago.** John Murra showed that Andean communities held land at several altitudes (tubers high, maize in the
  valleys, coca and cotton lower) and moved goods between them within the kin group or the state instead of trading [32].

D'Altroy and Earle name the choice: **staple finance** (the state pays in food and cloth from its own stores) against **wealth
finance** (it pays in valuables or money that people trade for what they need). The Inca ran an empire on staple finance and
labour tax, with local barter beneath: the strongest case that a large society can coordinate without markets if it has
storage, roads, records and a labour obligation. The Spanish found the qollqa full.

```
Inca staple finance (redistribution; time capital in terraces and stores; pulse at harvest)
├── land: state, temple and community fields, all worked by the community in turn
├── labour: mit'a turns per household, recorded on khipu ← local lords (kurakas) organise
├── terraces and irrigation ← mit'a labour over generations
├── processing: chuño, ch'arki, maize for beer ← frost, sun, women's labour
├── storage: qollqa at every provincial centre
├── carrying: people and llama caravans (a llama carries about 25 to 30 kg) on the Qhapaq Ñan
└── outflow: feasts for workers, rations for soldiers and builders, famine relief, gifts of cloth
```

### 5.2 The Aztecs: tribute and markets

The Aztec Triple Alliance (about 1428 to 1521) combined **tribute** from conquered provinces with some of the largest **markets**
of their time.

- **Tribute.** The Codex Mendoza (about 1541) records the tribute of 38 or 39 provinces on fixed schedules: maize, beans,
  amaranth, cacao, cotton cloaks (by the tens of thousands *(rough)*), warriors' costumes, feathers, jade, gold, copper, paper,
  lime, timber [33]. Tribute fed palaces, warriors and temples, and much was redistributed or sold.
- **Tlatelolco.** Cortés (second letter, 1520) described the market of Tlatelolco, Tenochtitlan's twin city, with "more than sixty
  thousand" people buying and selling daily; other estimates are 20,000 to 25,000 a day, more on the five-day market day [33] *(the
  60,000 is a conquistador's estimate)*. Goods were sold by count and measure in streets by kind, under **judges sitting in the
  market** to settle disputes and punish fraud.
- **Money.** Cacao beans for small sums; cotton cloaks (**quachtli**) worth 65 to 300 beans for large ones [33]; copper axe blanks
  and quills of gold dust.
- **Merchants.** The **pochteca**, a hereditary guild with its own courts, traded far beyond the empire and spied for it.
- **Carriers.** With no draft animals or wheeled carts, land carrying was by porters bearing about 23 kg some 20 to 30 km a day
  (Hassig). Drennan (1984) showed this made staple maize uneconomic to move more than a few hundred kilometres by land [33]: bulk
  food came from nearby and by canoe across the lakes; luxuries came from far.
- **Chinampas.** In the shallow lakes of Xochimilco and Chalco, farmers built long raised beds of mud and vegetation between
  canals, anchored by willows; canal muck renewed fertility, and seedlings raised in nurseries gave several crops a year. At
  their height they covered perhaps 9,000 to 9,500 ha *(rough)*; Tenochtitlan's population is put anywhere from about 40,000 to
  over 200,000 *(contested)* [33].

Tribute, markets and merchants fed each other: tribute goods reached the market, market cloaks served as money, and the state kept
the market's peace.

### 5.3 Trans-Saharan gold and salt

West Africa held the Old World's richest gold fields: **Bambuk** (Senegal–Falémé), **Bure** (upper Niger) and, from the 14th to
15th century, the **Akan** forest. The Sahara held rock salt at **Taghaza** and later **Taoudenni**. The south had gold and no
salt; the north wanted gold. With the camel in wide use and Muslim merchants from the 8th century, the trade crossed the desert for
nearly a thousand years [34].

- **Scale.** At its height perhaps two-thirds of the gold in the medieval Mediterranean came from West Africa *(rough)*; output
  estimates run from about one to several tonnes a year *(contested)*. It was minted into dinars at Sijilmasa and elsewhere [34].
- **The chokepoint and its toll.** Al-Bakri (1068) says the king of Ghana took one dinar on each donkey-load of salt coming in and
  two on each going out, and kept all gold **nuggets**, leaving the dust to the people, so that gold would not become abundant and
  lose its value [34]: a state restricting supply to hold the price, described in the 11th century.
- **Salt.** Ibn Battuta reached Taghaza in 1352 and found houses and a mosque built of salt blocks roofed with camel skins, worked
  by slaves; a camel carried two great slabs [34], each up to about 90 kg *(rough)*. Its price multiplied many times on the way
  south.
- **Mansa Musa.** The Mali ruler's pilgrimage through Cairo in 1324 spent and gave away so much gold that al-Umari, about twelve
  years later, wrote that its price there had not recovered. Tonnages quoted (12 to 18 t) are modern guesses *(rough)*.
- **Other goods.** Southward: salt, copper, horses, cloth, beads, books, and **cowrie shells** from the Maldives by way of Cairo,
  which became West Africa's small change. Northward: gold, captives, ivory, hides; within the south, kola nuts from the forest.

```
Gold for the Mediterranean (chokepoints at both ends; seasonal)
├── gold: alluvial washing and shallow shafts in Bambuk, Bure, Akan, by farmers in the dry season ← local lords' rights
├── control: Ghana, then Mali, then Songhay, taxing at the desert's edge; Wangara (Mande) traders inland
├── salt: Taghaza / Taoudenni, worked by slaves ← all food and water brought in
├── camels, guides, water: Saharan nomads (Massufa, later Tuareg) as carriers and protectors, for a fee
├── desert ports (Walata, Timbuktu, Gao) ↔ northern termini (Sijilmasa, Tlemcen, Tripoli, Cairo)
└── mints: Almoravid and Fatimid dinars, later Genoese and Venetian gold coin
```

From 1482 the Portuguese at Elmina drew Akan gold south to the sea; from the mid-16th century American bullion far outstripped
West Africa's [34].

### 5.4 Great Zimbabwe and the Swahili coast

**Great Zimbabwe** (about 1100 to 1450), a capital of massive mortarless stone enclosures, drew its wealth from **cattle** herded
seasonally under the court and from control of gold won by many small miners on the plateau and carried to **Sofala** on the
coast, then north to **Kilwa** [35]. Population estimates have fallen from up to 18,000 to under 10,000 *(contested)*. Chinese
porcelain and Persian ceramics have been found there. Explanations for its abandonment about 1450 include
exhausted gold, trade routes moving north, overgrazing and drought [35].

The **Swahili coast** (Mogadishu to Sofala) was a string of coral-stone Muslim trading towns exporting gold, ivory, captives,
ambergris and **mangrove poles** (sent to treeless Arabia as roof beams), and importing Indian cloth, beads and porcelain. Kilwa
minted copper coins from about the 11th century. The monsoon allowed one round trip a year, so foreign merchants waited months as
**guests of local hosts** who stored and sold their goods: a hosting institution like the funduq, and why the coast's towns were
brokers rather than carriers.

### 5.5 West African iron

At Taruga (Nok culture, Nigeria) iron furnaces date to the mid-first millennium BC (dates from about 600 to 200 BC have been
reported) [36]. Whether African iron was invented independently or came from the north is unsettled; the unlike furnaces make a
route via Meroë unlikely for West Africa [36]. African smelters built many kinds of **bloomery**: bowl furnaces with bellows, and
tall natural-draft furnaces that drew air without bellows. Schmidt and Avery (1978) reported that Haya smelters in Tanzania
preheated their air blast; the temperatures claimed are disputed [36]. Smiths were often a distinct caste, respected and feared
(the Mande numu). Iron hoes and axes opened forest and savanna to farming; iron bars and hoes served as money and bridewealth.
Benin's later bronzes were cast from the brass bracelets (manillas) the Portuguese brought by the tens of thousands.

## 6. The connecting systems

### 6.1 The Silk Roads

The overland routes from China through Central Asia to Persia and the Mediterranean (the name is von Richthofen's, 1877) opened
as state business, with the Han envoy Zhang Qian's missions (from 138 BC) and the Han conquest of the Tarim basin. They carried
**relay trade**: few merchants went end to end; goods passed through oasis cities (Dunhuang, Turfan, Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv),
each segment run by its own people. The **Sogdians** of Samarkand were the great intermediaries from the 4th to the 8th century
[44]. Valerie Hansen argues most traffic was local and much of it state-driven: the Tang paid its Central Asian garrisons in silk,
which then entered trade [44]. Silk went west; horses, glass and silver east;
religions, crops and techniques both ways. Under the Mongols one power held the
whole road, with safe-conduct tablets (paiza) and a relay post (yam); travel peaked, and the plague of 1346 came the same way.

### 6.2 The Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean was the largest trade zone before 1500, run on the **monsoon**: south-west winds in summer and north-east in
winter made crossings predictable but seasonal [43]. K. N. Chaudhuri divides it into three segments (the Arabian Sea, the Bay of
Bengal, the South China Sea), each with its own season, joined at **emporia** (Aden, Hormuz, Cambay, Calicut, Melaka, Quanzhou)
where merchants waited for the wind and exchanged cargoes [43]. Roman Egypt traded directly with India from Red Sea ports; the
**Muziris papyrus** (2nd century AD) values one ship's cargo from Kerala, of Gangetic nard, ivory (about 7 million sesterces) and
more, near 9 million sesterces in all, of which the state took a quarter in customs [42]. Zheng He's Chinese fleets (1405 to 1433)
reached East Africa. Merchant communities (Gujaratis, Chettiars, Arabs, Persians, Jews, Armenians, Chinese) lived as resident
minorities in each port under their own headmen, a pattern the Portuguese broke after 1498 with their cartaz system (passes sold
at gunpoint: selling protection, not goods).

### 6.3 The Columbian exchange and the silver that went to China

**Silver.** Potosí (in modern Bolivia) was found in 1545, Zacatecas in Mexico in 1546. Amalgamation with mercury (the patio
process: Bartolomé de Medina, Pachuca, 1554; at Potosí from about 1571 to 1572) let lower-grade ore be worked, with mercury from
Huancavelica in Peru and Almadén in Spain [38]. Viceroy Toledo turned the Inca mit'a into the Potosí **mita**: about 13,000 to
14,000 men a year from seventeen provinces, a seventh of the eligible men, each working a year in the mines and mills [38]; its
effects on the supplying districts still show in household consumption today (Dell 2010 [38]). Registered Peruvian output rose
from 6.4 million pesos in 1570–74 to 47 million in the 1570s and 64 million in the 1580s *(rough)* [38]. Spanish America produced
about 150,000 tonnes of silver from 1500 to 1800, perhaps 80 per cent of world output [37].

```
Spanish American silver to China (two chokepoints; forced labour; increasing returns at the mills)
├── ore: Potosí's Cerro Rico; Zacatecas, Guanajuato
├── labour: the mita (forced) plus wage workers; in Mexico more free and enslaved labour
├── mercury: Huancavelica, Almadén ← a crown monopoly; workers poisoned
├── amalgamation in patios ← salt, copper sulphate, mules treading the mix; stamp mills on dammed lakes
├── food and fuel: Potosí (perhaps 100,000+ people at 4,000 m, rough) fed from valleys far below by llama trains
├── minting: pieces of eight (the Spanish dollar), the first global coin
├── west: Acapulco → Manila (galleons from 1571, about 2 million pesos, 50 t, a year) → Chinese junks [37]
├── east: Seville → Europe's wars and trade → the Baltic, the Levant, the Dutch and English companies → India and China
└── demand: Ming taxes in silver (single-whip reforms, 1570s–80s); gold:silver about 1:6 in China, 1:12 in Spain c. 1600
```

Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez date the birth of a global economy to **1571**, when Manila linked the Americas directly to
Asia: China drew silver from Japan and the Americas because its money and taxes ran on silver after paper failed, and the price
gap paid for the whole system until the ratios met [37]. How much of the world's silver ended in China is debated (a third to a
half is often cited *(contested)*). Silver bought silk, porcelain and tea; Europe could pay for Asian goods mainly because it had
American silver.

**Crops, animals and disease.**

| From the Americas | Effect | From the Old World | Effect |

| potato | about a quarter of Old World population and urban growth 1700–1900 (Nunn and Qian, conservative [39]); a sixth to a quarter in a critical review [39] | sugar cane | the Atlantic plantation complex |

The collapse of the American population was the precondition of the colonial economy that followed: land emptied of people,
filled with plantations, cattle and forced labour, and about **12.5 million** Africans embarked across the Atlantic from 1501 to
1866, of whom about 10.7 million survived the crossing [41].

### 6.4 How the regional chains merged

| Date | Join | What it merged |
|---|---|---|
| ~1900 BC | Assur to Kanesh | eastern tin, Babylonian textiles, Anatolian silver |
| 5th c BC | Athens and the Black Sea | Crimean and Egyptian grain with Athenian silver and the fleet |
| 1st c BC–2nd c AD | Rome's sea; the Red Sea to India | grain, oil, wine and metal across the Mediterranean; pepper and cloth for Roman silver |
| 2nd c BC on | the Silk Roads | Chinese silk with Central Asian horses; Buddhism east, paper west |
| 8th–10th c | the Abbasid world | Indian sugar, cotton and rice west; paper west; dinars and credit across the zone |
| 9th–16th c | across the Sahara | West African gold into Mediterranean coin; salt south; Maldive cowries to West Africa |
| 11th–15th c | the Indian Ocean circuits | Chinese porcelain, Indian cloth, Southeast Asian spices, African gold and ivory |
| 1498–1571 | Portuguese and Spanish sea routes | Europe direct to India and the Spice Islands; Manila joins the Americas to China |
| 16th–18th c | the Atlantic and the silver cycle | silver to China and India; Indian cloth to Africa for captives; captives to American sugar; sugar to Europe |

Trade joined regions first through goods of **high value per weight** (silk, spices, gold, silver, fine cloth, porcelain),
carried by relay through many hands and institutions, and only later and locally through bulk staples (grain to Rome and Athens,
rice up the Grand Canal), which needed water and usually a state. When bulk moved far, it was almost always on a state's demand.

### 7.2 Compared with the European path

| Feature | Medieval and early modern Europe | Elsewhere | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Energy** | wood, charcoal, water and wind mills, animals; English coal from the 16th century | the same organic base; Song coal in the north; human porters in Mexico | high output is possible on an organic base; the switch needs coal near industry |

| **Labour** | serfdom, then wages; putting-out | slavery (Rome, Athens), corvée and mit'a, caste specialists, forced mining, plantation slavery | the worst labour followed mines and sugar |

Europe's path was neither the normal one nor the best before 1750. It was one of several high-output organic economies,
distinctive mainly in its competing, warring states, its Atlantic access to American land, silver and crops, and English coal.

## 8. What a simulator could take from this

Not a build list: mechanisms and shapes the history offers that the medieval village frame does not.

1. **State demand calls an industry into being.** The annona owned no ships; it bought carriers in with privileges, insurance and
   citizenship. An institution posting a standing demand (grain delivered to a capital at a price, with privileges for carriers)
   could test whether a private carrying trade forms to meet it. Bulk moved far almost only on a state's demand.
2. **A money tax forces markets** (Hopkins; the Mughal zabt; the Ming single whip). A tax payable only in coin makes households
   sell, and a market forms where none was: a strong lever for 02zzi §7's "does a market emerge?". Its opposite is the Inca: a tax
   in labour, repaid in food and cloth, needs no market at all.
3. **Staple finance or wealth finance.** Store food and cloth and pay in kind (needing storage, roads, records), or pay in money
   and let people buy (needing coin and markets). A world could allow both and see which an agent-run state picks.
4. **Chokepoints invite monopoly and price control, not only tolls.** Ghana taxed salt in and out and kept the nuggets to hold
   the gold price; the Han made salt and iron monopolies; Potosí hung on two crown mercury mines. The 81 BC debate shows the
   arguments a society has about it.
5. **Water in time shares.** The qanat's flow owned in hours of a cycle, timed by a water clock, sold and inherited, kept by a
   specialist craft: a precise historical form for a commons that is also property.
6. **Joint-product loops.** The mulberry-dike fish pond is a closed loop, each output another's input: an economy_web.md web
   after centuries of tuning.
7. **Pulses that spoil force organisation.** Cane crushed within a day or two; Nile grain and the sailing season; cloth ready for
   the monsoon. A pulse that cannot wait binds field and mill under one command (the plantation) or makes merchants advance
   capital before the season (dadni).
8. **Heavy money breeds paper; over-issue kills it.** Sichuan's iron coins led to receipts, state notes, inflation and a return
   to silver. A world's money could follow the same arc if both the weight of coin and the trust in an issuer are part of its
   state.
9. **Credit that moves value without moving coin.** The suftaja and hundi let a merchant pay in one city and be paid in another,
   through correspondents who trust each other. Their enforcement mixed network reputation, kin and courts (the Maghribi debate,
   and OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]]).
10. **Relay trade and hosts.** Long routes ran in segments, each people (Sogdians, Saharan nomads, Swahili hosts) taking a margin,
    with foreign merchants lodged and policed in funduqs or by hosts. Long-distance trade could be a chain of local intermediaries,
    not one carrier end to end.
11. **Transport sets what goes far.** Camels replaced carts where roads were poor; porters limited Mexican staple trade to a few
    hundred kilometres; Rome's sea was thirty or more times cheaper than its roads. High value per weight goes far; bulk goes far
    only on water.
12. **One product, many social forms.** Iron by a state monopoly (Han), licensed private works (Song), a smith caste (Africa);
    cloth by households on advances (India) or imperial bureaus (China); silver by slaves (Laurion), leaseholders (Roman Spain) or
    forced rotation (Potosí). The technology does not fix the society: a world can hold the chain fixed and let the form vary.
13. **Proxies for output.** Rome is measured from lead in ice, wrecks and a hill of broken jars. A simulator could keep the same
    kinds of trace (slag, discarded containers, wrecks, fouled water) and test whether an economy can be read from them, then check
    the reading against the true state, which historians cannot do.
14. **Scale is not the switch.** Song China had coal-fired iron, water-driven machines, paper money and national markets, and did
    not industrialise. A world that adds output and depth still sits under the organic ceiling unless it adds cheap, concentrated
    energy near industry and a reason (dear labour) to use it: the edge noted in 02zzi §3.8.
15. **Collapse paths.** Great Zimbabwe when the gold or routes went; the Song north after invasion and flood; the Andes after the
    epidemics; Rome after the Antonine plague. A deep economy breaks at its chokepoints.
