# 06: types of society and the transitions between them, from foragers to the twenty-first-century city (15 Nov 2026)

Part of the 02zzj economy research folder. This file is the spine: it names the kinds of society, how each one is put together, how
societies move from one to another (and back), and what each needs before it can exist. It deepens the memory-based frame in
`02zzi_economies_scaling.md` §3–§5 and grounds it in the scholarship. Prehistory and the first economies, ancient and non-European
economies, cities as economies, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century industries and supply chains have their own files in this
folder; this one keeps to societies and transitions and touches industries only where they mark a transition.

## Summary

Every society solves the same few problems: how to get energy and materials from the land, how to move goods and information, how
to make promises hold between people who are not kin, and who takes the surplus. The standard types (band, tribe, chiefdom, early
state, agrarian empire, manor, city-state, territorial state, industrial capitalism, central planning, the service and information
economy) are recurring bundles of answers to those problems. They are not rungs on a ladder. Scholars from Elman Service and
Morton Fried to Allen Johnson and Timothy Earle sorted societies by scale and integration; David Graeber and David Wengrow have
recently attacked that sorting as too tidy, and their critics reply that variation within a stage does not abolish the stages. The
working position here is that the types are real attractors with real preconditions, that societies move between them in both
directions, and that several branches (pastoral nomads, irrigation societies, maritime networks, settler frontiers, plantation
slavery, gift-and-rank economies) are as stable as the main line.

The transitions have mechanisms, and most are well argued but contested: population pressure (Boserup) against the Malthusian
ceiling; the extent of the market (Smith); energy (Wrigley's organic and mineral economies); useful knowledge (Mokyr); institutions
that limit or open access (North, Wallis and Weingast) or extract or include (Acemoglu and Robinson). Collapses show the same
mechanisms run backwards: when a fiscal system, a trade route or a population falls below a threshold, the specialists that depended
on it vanish, and the depth of the economy is lost faster than it was built. For a simulator the lesson is that each type needs the
world to hold specific things (a storable staple, a chokepoint, a means of recording debts, a mineral fuel, a market reachable by
cheap transport), and that a type will not appear, or will not last, if any one of them is missing. The closing section lists those
requirements type by type.

## 1. How to read a typology

| Scheme | Categories | What it sorts by | Main critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Service (1962), Fried (1967) | band, tribe, chiefdom, state; egalitarian, ranked, stratified, state | political integration; access to status and resources | unilinear; built from ethnography of colonised peoples treated as living fossils |
| Johnson and Earle (1987, 2000) | family-level group, local group, regional polity (simple and complex chiefdom, archaic state, agrarian state, nation-state) | intensification of production, integration, stratification | still a single direction; less on reversals |
| Polanyi (1944, 1957) | reciprocity, redistribution, (market) exchange; householding | how goods move | the three coexist in most societies; markets older and wider than he allowed |
| Marx (1859 preface and after) | primitive communism, ancient (slave), Asiatic, feudal, capitalist, (socialist) modes of production | who owns the means of production and how surplus is taken | teleological; the "Asiatic" mode a European projection; used here only as a frame for surplus extraction |
| Earle and D'Altroy (1985) | staple finance against wealth finance | whether rulers are paid in bulk food or in portable valuables | a useful axis rather than a typology |
| Morris (2013, 2015) | foragers, farmers, fossil-fuel users | energy captured per person (from roughly 4,000–5,000 kcal a day for foragers up to hundreds of thousands in industrial societies, following Earl Cook) | energy is one index among several; values and politics follow it only loosely |
| Graeber and Wengrow (2021) | against stages: seasonal alternation, choice, "play farming" | the freedom to move, disobey and reorganise | Scheidel (2022) and others: flexibility within a stage does not refute the overall trend in scale and hierarchy |

The pattern the evidence supports is a strong correlation, not a law: larger, denser populations with storable surpluses tend toward
more levels of hierarchy, more specialisation and more extraction, and the reverse happens when population, surplus or connection
fall. Within that trend, societies at the same scale differ widely, and many once-common forms (complex foragers, steppe empires)
do not sit on the main line at all.

## 2. The types

### 2.1 Overview

### 2.2 Foraging bands
Foragers live in small mobile groups that fission and fuse through the year, linked by kin and marriage to a regional network of a
few hundred. Food is shared, often by rule, and status is levelled by ridicule and the threat of leaving (Richard Lee's !Kung work).
Marshall Sahlins called them "the original affluent society" because food getting took only part of the week; later work (Hawkes,
O'Connell) showed that processing, tool making and childcare raise the total sharply, and that the leisure was seasonal. Mobility
caps possessions and stored wealth. Not all foragers were egalitarian: where a rich, storable, defensible resource existed (salmon on
the Northwest Coast, acorns in California) foragers became sedentary, ranked and slave-holding (§3.6). The limit is the wild yield of
the land, usually well under one person per square kilometre.

### 2.3 Horticultural and tribal societies
Gardeners clear plots, crop them for a few years and let them return to forest or bush. Ester Boserup's fallow ladder (forest
fallow of 20–25 years, bush fallow, short fallow, annual cropping, multi-cropping) describes how this intensifies as people per acre
rise. Settlement is sedentary or semi-sedentary in villages of a few hundred; political life runs through lineages and councils.
In Melanesia the typical figure is the big man (Sahlins 1963), who builds a following through feasts and pig exchange and loses it
when he stops giving; his position does not pass to his son. Surplus becomes pigs, feasts, bride-wealth and alliances. Warfare is
frequent and often the main check on growth. The limit is that no one can store, measure or tax the surplus at scale.

### 2.4 Chiefdoms
A chiefdom has hereditary ranks and a central person or lineage above the village, with one level (simple) or two (complex) of
hierarchy. Chiefs collect part of the harvest and labour, fund feasts, craftsmen and warriors, and raise monuments; Polynesia,
Cahokia on the Mississippi (perhaps 10,000–20,000 at its peak c. 1050–1150 CE) and Bronze Age Wessex are standard cases. Patrick
Kirch argues that Hawaii's paramount chiefs had become archaic kings by the seventeenth century, with a population of several hundred
thousand, land divided into tax units and divine kingship. Earle stresses that chiefs need a control point: irrigation works,
fishponds, prestige goods from afar, or warriors. Chiefdoms cycle: they expand, fragment on succession and re-form, because the chief
cannot yet make his officials independent of their own kin.

### 2.5 Early states and temple economies
The first states (Mesopotamia from the late fourth millennium BCE, Egypt about 3100 BCE, the Indus, the Yellow River, later
Mesoamerica and the Andes) add a standing administration, taxes, a monopoly claim on force and, usually, writing. Southern
Mesopotamia shows the temple and palace households at the centre: they held land, herds and workshops, paid dependants in barley and
wool rations recorded on clay, and ran weaving shops of hundreds of women. Writing grew out of accounting. James Scott's *Against the
Grain* (2017) argues that early states were grain states because cereals ripen at once, can be seen, measured, stored and taxed, and
that many people fled them; Mayshar, Moav and Pascali (2022) find cross-cultural support that cereals' appropriability, rather than
land productivity alone, predicts states. Critics of Scott note the role of non-grain crops and that many people chose towns. The
limit is how far grain can be hauled and how many people a centre can feed and keep.

### 2.6 Agrarian empires
Rome, Han and Tang China, the Achaemenids, the Mauryas, the Abbasids, the Ottomans and the Mughals are empires of tens of millions on
a peasant base. Most people farm; perhaps 10–15 per cent live in towns; an elite of a few per cent takes the surplus through rent and
tax. Milanovic, Lindert and Williamson (2011) measured pre-industrial "extraction ratios": elites in many empires took close to the
maximum compatible with subsistence of the rest. Empires gave peace, roads, coin and law across large areas, and they can show real
per capita growth (Jack Goldstone calls these "efflorescences": Roman, Song, Dutch), but growth in an organic economy hits the land
ceiling and turns into population. Peter Turchin and Sergey Nefedov describe secular cycles of two to three centuries: growth,
crowding and elite overproduction, crisis, depopulation.

### 2.7 Feudal and manorial society
In the Latin West after the Carolingians, power fragmented to lords who held jurisdiction over dependent peasants; the manor joined a
village's holdings to the lord's demesne, worked by labour services, and to his mill, oven and court (the ban). Marc Bloch and Georges
Duby described the system; Evsey Domar (1970) argued that serfdom appears where land is plentiful and labour scarce and the political
authority sides with landlords, a hypothesis later critics found too loose across cases. Surplus went into the lord's household,
castles, churches and war. The famous "Brenner debate" (1976 onward) set Robert Brenner's class-relations account against the
neo-Malthusian one of Postan and Le Roy Ladurie: the same demographic shock (the Black Death) ended serfdom in the west and produced a
"second serfdom" east of the Elbe, because the balance of power between lords and peasants differed. The limits are land per peasant,
the cost of violence between lords, and peasant mobility.

### 2.8 City-states and commercial republics
Sumerian city-states, Phoenician ports, about a thousand Greek poleis (most with a few thousand people on territories under
200 km²), the Swahili coast, the Hanse, and above all Venice, Genoa and Florence are societies where trade and craft dominate and food
is bought or taken from outside. Medieval Italian cities invented or spread the commenda partnership, double-entry bookkeeping,
marine insurance, bills of exchange and public debt. The Dutch Republic (about 1.9 million in 1650, with over half of Holland's people
in towns) was the largest version: Jan de Vries and Ad van der Woude call it "the first modern economy", with specialised farming,
peat energy, wind-powered industry and the world's busiest shipping. Stephan Epstein argued that city-states also restricted trade to
favour their own citizens, and that larger territorial states eventually integrated markets better. The limits are food supply,
dependence on routes others can close, and larger neighbours.

### 2.9 Early modern territorial states
From about 1500 European states grew by war. Charles Tilly (1990) shows two paths: coercion-intensive states built bureaucracies to
squeeze land and peasants (Prussia, Russia); capital-intensive states bargained with merchants who could lend (Venice, the Dutch);
the most successful (England, France) combined them. John Brewer's "fiscal-military state" describes England after 1688: excise
officers, a funded national debt and the Bank of England (1694) raised revenue sharply without revolt. Chartered companies (the East
India Company 1600, the VOC 1602) carried state power overseas. Proto-industry spread through the countryside, and de Vries'
"industrious revolution" had households working more for the market to buy new goods (sugar, tea, cotton, pottery). Ming and Qing
China, Mughal India, Ottoman and Tokugawa states were comparable agrarian states with large commercial sectors. All were still
organic economies.

### 2.10 Industrial capitalist societies
Industrial society begins when mineral energy and machines make output per worker grow for generations. In Britain the share of
workers in agriculture fell from about a third in 1800 to about a fifth by 1851 and under a tenth by 1900. The first industrial
revolution (cotton spinning, iron, steam, canals and railways) was followed after about 1870 by a second (steel, chemicals,
electricity, the internal combustion engine), led by Germany and the United States, with the large managerial firm that Alfred
Chandler called "the visible hand", research laboratories, and from 1913 mass production on Ford's assembly line. Wage labour became
the normal relation; cities became majorities; states added schooling, factory law and social insurance. The limits are business
cycles and financial crises, conflict between capital and labour, and in the long run the environmental cost of fossil fuel.

### 2.11 Socialist planned economies
The Soviet Union from the first Five-Year Plan (1928) and its imitators replaced markets with administered output targets, prices and
allocation, collectivised agriculture and pushed savings into heavy industry and defence. Robert Allen's *Farm to Factory* argues the
growth to the 1960s was real and fast; the famine of 1932–33 and the camps were its cost. Friedrich Hayek (1945) argued that a planner
cannot gather the dispersed, local knowledge prices carry; János Kornai (*Economics of Shortage*, 1980) showed how soft budget
constraints made firms hoard inputs, ignore cost and quality, and produce chronic shortages. Growth slowed from the 1970s as extensive
growth (adding labour and capital) ran out. The Soviet economy collapsed after 1989–91 (§5.5); China's reform from 1978 kept the
party-state but let markets grow alongside the plan.

### 2.12 The service and information economy
Colin Clark (1940) and Jean Fourastié described the shift of employment from agriculture to industry to services as incomes rise.
Services now hold about three quarters of jobs in high-income countries. Daniel Bell's *Coming of Post-Industrial Society* (1973) put
theoretical knowledge at the centre; William Baumol explained why labour-intensive services (health, education, performance) grow
dearer relative to goods ("cost disease"). Since about 1990, Richard Baldwin argues, cheap communication allowed a "second unbundling":
factories split their stages across borders into global value chains, which now carry close to half of world trade (World Bank 2020),
and manufacturing moved toward China and other "industrialising" economies. Saskia Sassen's *Global City* (1991) showed that the
dispersal of production concentrated control in a few cities (New York, London, Tokyo) where finance, law, accounting and advertising
("producer services") cluster. The world became majority urban about 2007. Capital is increasingly intangible (software, data,
designs, brands; Haskel and Westlake 2017), which scales without limit and favours winner-take-most firms and cities. Fertility in
most rich countries is below replacement. The limits are slow productivity growth in services, ageing, inequality between and within
cities, housing, and the move off fossil fuel.

## 3. The branches

| Branch | What made it viable | What it depended on | Typical end |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pastoral nomadism | grassland unfit for crops; herds turn grass into food, wool, transport; mobility as defence | grain, metal and cloth from settled neighbours; the horse for steppe empires | absorbed, or conquers and settles |
| Irrigation ("hydraulic") society | river floods on fertile alluvium; very high yields per acre | canals that need upkeep and allocation of water | salinisation, silting, war over the head-works |
| Maritime trading network | cheap water transport; monsoon or coastal regularity; entrepôts at straits | open routes; commercial law and trust across ports | larger states take the chokepoints |
| Frontier and settler economy | land abundant, labour scarce; high wages and early marriage | access to markets for staples; force against the original inhabitants | boom, bust and export rescue (Belich); frontier closes |
| Plantation and slave economy | export crops with economies of gang labour (sugar, cotton, tobacco); land abundant | supply of captives; coercive state; distant consumers | abolition; persistent inequality |
| Gift and rank economy | a rich storable resource (salmon) or pig and sweet-potato surplus; competition for rank | alliances and marriage between groups; ceremony | colonial bans; incorporation in markets |

### 3.1 Pastoral nomadism
Anatoly Khazanov's central point is that nomads are never self-sufficient: they need grain, metal and cloth from farmers and get them
by trade, tribute or raid. Thomas Barfield showed that steppe empires (the Xiongnu, the Turks, the Uighurs) rose with Chinese dynasties
and lived on extracting tribute from them by an "outer frontier strategy" of alternating raids and peace; the Mongols were the
exception that conquered and ruled. Nomadic economies are viable where cropping fails, where animals store wealth on the hoof, and
where mobility makes them hard to tax.

### 3.2 Irrigation societies
Karl Wittfogel's *Oriental Despotism* (1957) argued that large irrigation works required, and produced, centralised despotic states.
Robert McC. Adams showed that in Mesopotamia the early canals were local and states came before large-scale works; Stephen Lansing's
work on the Balinese subak showed water managed by a nested system of farmer associations and water temples rather than a king, though
critics in *Human Ecology* (2012) dispute how old that system is. The fair reading: irrigation gives high and reliable yields and a
control point that can be captured, but it does not require despotism.

### 3.3 Maritime trading networks
The Indian Ocean (K. N. Chaudhuri; Janet Abu-Lughod's world system before 1250) ran on the monsoon's regular wind reversal: ports such
as Aden, Calicut, Malacca and Quanzhou exchanged cloth, spices, horses and porcelain through merchant diasporas with their own law.
Austronesian voyagers, the Phoenicians, the Hanse and Venice are other cases. Viability came from water transport many times cheaper
than land, from entrepôts at straits, and from trust institutions across ports. Avner Greif's account of Maghribi traders enforcing
contracts by collective reputation is the classic model; Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie argued from the same Geniza letters that
they also used courts and outsiders, and Greif disputed their reading.

### 3.4 Frontier and settler economies
Frederick Jackson Turner's frontier thesis (1893) credited free land with American democracy; historians since have stressed conquest
and federal support. Domar's logic applies: abundant land and scarce labour mean either high wages and family farms or coerced labour.
Stanley Engerman and Kenneth Sokoloff argued that crops decided which: wheat and mixed farming favoured broad landholding and
inclusive institutions; sugar and silver favoured large estates, slavery and lasting inequality. James Belich's *Replenishing the
Earth* describes Anglo settler booms in the nineteenth century as cycles of boom, bust and "export rescue" once railways and steamships
let the frontier ship staples to the metropolis.

### 3.5 Plantation and slave economies
About 12.5 million Africans were embarked in the transatlantic slave trade and about 10.7 million arrived (Slave Voyages database).
Sugar in Brazil and the Caribbean, then tobacco, rice and cotton in North America, ran on gang labour that free workers would not
accept at the wages owners would pay. Fogel and Engerman's *Time on the Cross* (1974) argued plantations were efficient and profitable;
critics (Gutman, David and Temin) attacked its evidence and its picture of slave life, while accepting profitability. Eric Williams
argued that slavery's profits financed British industry, a claim most economic historians now think too large in quantity but right
about the Atlantic system's role in cotton supply. Viable where land was abundant, the crop had economies of scale, a state backed
coercion, and a supply of captives continued.

### 3.6 Gift economies of the Pacific Northwest and Melanesia
Marcel Mauss's *The Gift* (1925) built on two cases. On the Northwest Coast, salmon runs dried and stored in quantity let foragers
live in large winter villages with hereditary ranks, slaves and the potlatch, in which chiefs gave away or destroyed goods to validate
names and rank. Archaeologists find that ranking followed the intensification of salmon storage. Wayne Suttles argued the potlatch
spread food across villages whose local resources fluctuated; Canada banned it from 1885 to 1951. In the Trobriand Islands, Bronisław
Malinowski described the kula, in which shell necklaces travel clockwise and armshells anticlockwise around an island ring, creating
lasting partnerships and, alongside, ordinary barter. In highland New Guinea, the moka (Andrew Strathern) is competitive exchange of
pigs between big men, made possible by sweet potato. What made these viable: a storable surplus without a state to tax it, and rank
won by giving.

## 4. Transitions and their mechanisms

### 4.1 The Neolithic transition
Farming began independently in at least seven to eleven centres: the Fertile Crescent (cultivation by about 11,500 years ago),
northern and southern China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, the Andes, eastern North America, the Sahel and perhaps others. Domestication
was protracted, over one to three millennia (Dorian Fuller). Explanations include climate (Richerson, Boyd and Bettinger: farming was
impossible in the unstable Pleistocene and close to inevitable in the stable Holocene), population pressure (Mark Cohen), a broad
spectrum revolution forced by scarcity (Kent Flannery), feasting and competition (Brian Hayden), and property rights (Bowles and Choi).
Its costs were real: skeletons show shorter stature, worse teeth and more infection (Cohen and Armelagos 1984). Its gain was numbers:
Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel's "Neolithic demographic transition" finds birth rates rising within a few centuries of farming, probably as
sedentism shortened birth spacing. Farming won by outbreeding foragers, not by making individuals better off.

### 4.2 The rise of the state
Integrative theories (Childe's urban revolution; Wright and Johnson's information processing; managerial irrigation) see the state as
a solution to coordination. Conflict theories see it as capture: Robert Carneiro (1970) argued that where farmland is bounded by desert,
sea or mountains (circumscription), defeated villages cannot flee and become tribute payers; critics say this needs a universal drive
to conquer and fails in cross-cultural tests, though it fits several valleys. Michael Mann's "caging" and Scott's grain state are
versions of the same idea: states arise where people and their harvest cannot easily leave. Most specialists now combine both: a
storable, appropriable surplus, a density at which face-to-face rule fails (Dunbar's figure of about 150 is the usual reference),
competition between polities, and a technology of records.

### 4.3 The commercial revolution of the high Middle Ages
Robert Lopez dated it 950–1350. Europe's population perhaps doubled or tripled between 1000 and 1300; towns were founded or chartered
in thousands; in England alone, kings granted well over a thousand market and fair charters between 1200 and 1349 (Richard Britnell).
Italian merchants developed partnerships, credit and bookkeeping; the Champagne fairs of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries joined
Italian and Flemish trade. The mechanisms were Smithian (wider markets, finer specialisation), demographic (more people, more
intensive farming) and institutional (peace among lords, town liberties, merchant law). It ended in the famines of 1315–17 and the
Black Death: growth had been largely extensive and ran into the land ceiling.

### 4.4 The agricultural revolution
In England, output per acre and per worker rose between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries through convertible husbandry, new
fodder crops (turnips, clover) and rotations that fixed nitrogen and fed more animals, giving more manure (Mark Overton). Robert Allen
argued in *Enclosure and the Yeoman* that much of the rise came from small farmers in the seventeenth century, not from later
enclosure, which mainly redistributed income to landlords. The key effect for transition is that fewer farmers could feed more
townspeople, releasing labour to industry.

### 4.5 The Great Divergence
Kenneth Pomeranz (2000) argued that the Yangzi delta and England were similar in living standards, markets and ecological strain into
the late eighteenth century, and that Britain diverged because of two accidents: coal near its industrial centres and the "ghost acres"
of the New World (land-intensive cotton, sugar, timber and food grown abroad). Critics answered with data. Stephen Broadberry and
Bishnupriya Gupta found northwest European silver wages reflected higher productivity; Allen and coauthors (2011) found Chinese real
wages close to southern Europe's but below England's and Holland's; Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) date the divergence of the Yangzi
delta to about 1700 and of China as a whole much earlier. Others point to an earlier "little divergence" within Europe (Amsterdam and
London pulling away after 1500), to political fragmentation and war (Rosenthal and Wong), and to a culture of useful knowledge (Mokyr).
The debate moved the question from "why Europe" to "why northwest Europe, why the eighteenth century, why coal".

### 4.6 The industrial revolution
Revised national accounts (Crafts and Harley) made British growth slower and longer than the old picture: under 2 per cent a year,
concentrated in a few sectors. Explanations: Allen's high wages and cheap coal made labour-saving, energy-using machines pay in Britain
first (critics Humphries and Schneider show hand spinners' wages were low, which Allen disputes); Mokyr's industrial enlightenment, an
open market in useful knowledge between scientists and craftsmen; Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda's skilled mechanics; Wrigley's energy
transition (by 1800 Britain's coal equalled the fuel from woodland covering about a third of its surface); and North and Weingast's
secure property after 1688. Most historians now treat these as complements.

### 4.7 The demographic transition
Warren Thompson (1929) and Frank Notestein (1945) described the sequence: high birth and death rates, then falling death rates
(population boom), then falling birth rates, then low and stable rates. Mortality fell first through better food (Thomas McKeown) or,
as Simon Szreter argued, through public health in cities. The Princeton European Fertility Project (Coale and Watkins) found fertility
fell almost together across regions of very different economies, and read this as cultural diffusion; Brown and Guinnane showed its
statistical methods were biased against finding economic effects. Oded Galor's unified growth theory links the transition to rising
returns to education: parents trade quantity of children for quality. The "second demographic transition" (Lesthaeghe and van de Kaa)
describes fertility below replacement as values shift toward individual choice. It matters for this spine because it breaks the
Malthusian link: income gains stop turning into more people.

### 4.8 Twentieth-century transitions
Three more transitions finish the line. Mass production and the consumer economy (1910s–1970s): standardised goods, high wages, mass
schooling, the welfare state. The East Asian developmental state (Chalmers Johnson on Japan's MITI, Alice Amsden on Korea, Robert
Wade on Taiwan): states directing credit to export industries, with land reform and education first. Deindustrialisation and the
global value chain (from about 1980–1990): manufacturing jobs fall in rich countries, rise in Asia, while control and services gather in
global cities. The post-1978 Chinese transition from plan to market, and the post-1991 East European one, run part of the sequence in a
generation.

## 5. Theories of how societies move

| Theory | Core claim | What it explains best | Main critiques |
|---|---|---|---|
| Malthus (1798) | population grows faster than food; checks (famine, disease, late marriage) hold income near subsistence | pre-industrial stagnation; secular cycles; post-plague wage booms | misses the escape after 1800; underrates technical change and preventive checks (Hajnal's European marriage pattern) |

(2004): human capital comes first and institutions follow; contested settler-mortality data (Albouy); growth under extractive regimes (China) |

Fair summary: Malthus and Boserup describe the pre-industrial dynamic and its two sides (pressure lowers living standards, pressure
pushes intensification). Smith explains the efflorescences. Wrigley and Mokyr explain why the industrial one did not stop. The
institutional theories explain which societies took part, and their critics show that knowledge and human capital move with
institutions rather than after them.

## 6. Collapse and reversal

### 6.1 The Late Bronze Age collapse (about 1200–1150 BCE)
The eastern Mediterranean of the thirteenth century BCE was a system of palace economies (Mycenaean Greece, Hittite Anatolia, Ugarit,
Cyprus, Egypt, Assyria, Babylon) joined by diplomacy and the trade of copper and tin for bronze. The Uluburun wreck (about 1300 BCE)
carried about ten tonnes of Cypriot copper and a tonne of tin with glass, ivory and resin. Mycenaean palaces recorded taxes, rations and
craft allocations on Linear B tablets. Within a few decades most palaces were destroyed, the Hittite state vanished and Ugarit burned.
Eric Cline's *1177 B.C.* treats it as a systems collapse (Colin Renfrew's term): drought (pollen and isotope evidence; Knapp and Manning
warn the climate data are thin), earthquakes, the "Sea Peoples" as symptom as much as cause, rebellion, and the failure of the trade
network on which bronze depended. **Depth lost:** in Greece the palaces, writing, fine craft and long-distance trade disappeared for
about four centuries; population fell; the alphabet returned only around 800 BCE. Iron, available locally, spread partly because the
tin trade had broken.

### 6.2 The end of the Western Roman economy (fifth to seventh centuries)
The "transformation" school (Peter Brown and successors) stresses continuity of culture and religion; Bryan Ward-Perkins (2005)
countered with material evidence of a sharp fall in living standards: fewer and coarser pots, smaller cattle, roofs of thatch instead
of tile, coins out of use. In Britain the fall was nearly total: within a generation of 410, coin use, wheel-made pottery and towns had
gone. Chris Wickham's explanation is fiscal: the Roman tax-and-army system had moved grain and goods across the Mediterranean and paid
the soldiers and officials whose demand sustained specialist producers; when the tax system broke into kingdoms that lived off land,
the aristocracy shrank, exchange simplified, and production for distant markets ended. Greenland ice cores (McConnell et al. 2018)
show lead pollution from silver mining peaking in the first and second centuries and falling with the Antonine plague (from 165) and
the third-century crisis, staying low for some five centuries: the decline began well before 476. Kyle Harper adds climate and plague;
Haldon and coauthors (2018) warn against reading environmental data as simple causes. **Depth lost:** mass production for distant
markets, money taxation, urban demand, literate administration in the west; recovery to Roman levels of output took centuries.

### 6.3 The Black Death (1347–1352)
Plague killed perhaps 30–50 per cent of Europe's people by older estimates, up to about 60 per cent in Ole Benedictow's reckoning, and
recurred for three centuries. It is a collapse that became a push forward. With land suddenly abundant and labour scarce, real wages in
England rose substantially over the following century despite the Ordinance and Statute of Labourers (1349, 1351) that tried to freeze
them; labour services and serfdom faded in the west; diets shifted toward meat and ale; land went to pasture. East of the Elbe, lords
used the same scarcity to tie peasants tighter (the Brenner debate, §2.7). Voigtländer and Voth (2013) argue that plague, war and
urban disease kept northwest European populations low enough for incomes to stay high, a precondition of the later little divergence.
Walter Scheidel counts it among the great levellers of inequality. **Depth lost and kept:** population and much marginal settlement
were lost, but the institutions, knowledge and trade routes survived, so per capita income rose.

### 6.4 Joseph Tainter on complexity
Tainter's *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988) treats complexity (more roles, more administration, more control) as a problem-
solving investment. Early investments pay well; later ones return less (diminishing marginal returns), until a shock that the society
once could have absorbed brings collapse, which he describes as a rapid fall in complexity and, for many people, an economising
relief. His cases are the Western Roman Empire, the Classic Maya and Chaco Canyon. Critiques: "complexity" is hard to measure and the
theory risks circularity; it ignores innovation and new energy sources that raise returns (the industrial case); case selection;
and archaeologists in *Questioning Collapse* (McAnany and Yoffee 2010) and Karl Butzer (2012) stress that people and much of their
culture continued, so "collapse" often means the end of an elite, not of a society.

### 6.5 Modern reversals
The Soviet economy's end shows a chain collapse in an industrial society. Russian output fell by about 40 per cent in the 1990s; Olivier
Blanchard and Michael Kremer (1997) explained the fall as "disorganisation": when the plan stopped, firms in long chains of specialised
suppliers could not replace a single missing input or customer, and bargaining failures cut whole chains. The same logic applies to
global value chains under shocks (2020–22 shortages).

### 6.6 How depth is lost: the general mechanisms

| Mechanism | What happens | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| fiscal or redistributive centre fails | the buyer of specialist goods and payer of officials disappears; specialists lose their market | Mycenaean palaces; Western Rome; Soviet plan |
| chain break | one missing input (tin, a component) stops every product downstream | Bronze Age tin; Soviet disorganisation; modern supply shocks |
| transport cost rises | insecurity or loss of ships and roads shrinks markets below the size specialists need (Smith in reverse) | post-Roman west; Late Bronze Age sea routes |
| population falls below a threshold | too few buyers or apprentices to keep a craft; knowledge held by few is lost (Joseph Henrich's Tasmania argument, contested) | post-Roman Britain; Greek writing; the plague's marginal villages |
| environmental degradation | soil, water, forest exhausted under a large population | salinisation in southern Mesopotamia; deforestation debates for the Maya and Easter Island (the latter disputed) |
| elite overproduction and civil war | too many claimants for too few positions | Turchin's secular cycles |
| simplification by choice | people leave burdensome structures for simpler ones | Scott's escape from states; Tainter's economising |

Building depth needs many things in place at once; losing it needs only one link to fail. A collapse that kills people but leaves
institutions, routes and knowledge (the Black Death) can raise income per head; one that breaks institutions and routes (the Bronze
Age, the post-Roman west) lowers it for centuries.

## 8. What a simulator could take from this

This section, at the user's direction, sets aside the limits of scale and cost and asks what each type of society would require a world
to contain. A type will not appear, or will not persist, unless each of these is present; the markers say how one would recognise it in
a run's end state, read against a null arm in which the requirement is absent.

**General principles.**
- Every type is defined by a **surplus and who takes it**, a **means of moving goods and information**, and a **means of making
  promises hold**. A world must hold all three; the type is decided by their shape.
- **Requirements stack.** A city-state needs everything a village needs plus a hinterland, routes and credit. A world can begin at a
  type (as the engine already starts with a history run) or try to grow into it; growing in needs the earlier types' requirements first.
- **Chokepoints decide hierarchy.** A chief, a temple, a lord, a port or a platform all hold something others need and cannot get round:
  water head-works, a mill, a strait, a market, a standard. This is the leverage-and-dependence principle of `docs/scenario_design.md`.
- **Storability decides appropriation.** A harvest that can be seen, measured and stored can be taxed; a world of tubers and game can
  host chiefs and big men but not grain states.
- **Reversals need the same parts.** If a world holds a fiscal centre, long chains and specialists, it can also show collapse; a world
  that cannot lose depth cannot show what depth was worth.

**Type by type.**

| Type | What the world must contain | Markers that it has formed |
|---|---|---|
| Foraging band | wild food spread thin and variable over a large area; game that rewards cooperation; no storable staple; open land to leave to | sharing beyond the household; fission and fusion; no lasting rank |
| Complex foragers and gift economy | a seasonal, concentrated, storable wild resource (a salmon run) at places a group can hold; rival groups; goods that carry status | permanent villages; hereditary rank; competitive feasts and giving |
| Horticultural and tribal society | domesticated plants and animals; land that recovers under fallow; enough land to move; pigs or a feast good | fallow cycles; big men whose following rests on giving; raiding |
| Chiefdom | a capturable control point (an irrigation work, fishponds, a source of prestige goods, a defensible site); a regular surplus; ritual and kin rank | tribute flowing to one lineage; feasts and works funded by it; succession crises |
| Early state and temple economy | an appropriable cereal; land bounded so that subjects cannot easily leave; stores; a means of records (tallies, writing); several thousand people within reach of a centre | rations from central stores; recorded debts and taxes; a permanent staff fed by others; flight from taxed land |
| Agrarian empire | many such states to conquer; an army that must be paid; roads and coin; provinces with different products | tax across regions; a capital fed from afar; frontier wars; secular cycles of growth and crisis |
| Feudal and manorial society | land worth holding, labour scarce or tied; lords with private force and jurisdiction; mills, ovens and courts as lords' capital; a weak centre | labour rents and dues; banalities; peasant flight or revolt; commutation to money rents when markets grow |
| City-state and commercial republic | cheap water transport; a route or strait to sit on; a hinterland or grain supply; credit instruments, partnerships, bookkeeping; craft skills | most people not farming; food imported; merchant councils; public debt; guilds controlling entry |
| Early modern territorial state | rival states and expensive war; taxable money incomes; merchants able to lend; a print or post system; overseas or frontier trade | standing army; funded debt; national market prices converging; chartered companies; proto-industry in the countryside |
| Industrial capitalist society | a mineral fuel; machines that turn it into work; an agriculture productive enough to feed a non-farm majority; secure property and company law; wage labour; schooling; railways and steamships | rising output per worker over generations; factory towns; class organisations; agriculture falling below a tenth of work |
| Socialist planned economy | an industrial base; a party-state with power to set output and prices; collective farms; the absence of a legal private market | targets and quotas; shortages and queues; hoarding by firms; informal markets; fast early growth then stagnation |
| Service and information economy | an industrial base already in place; mass education; cheap communication over long distances; container-scale shipping; intellectual property; global cities where producer services cluster; tradeable stages of production | most work in services; production split across regions in value chains; control concentrated in a few cities; below-replacement fertility; intangible capital |
| Pastoral nomadism | grassland unfit for crops; herd animals; settled neighbours with grain and metal; mobility that makes taxing hard | wealth in herds; seasonal migration; tribute or raids on farmers |
| Irrigation society | a river with silt and flood; canals that need upkeep; water allocation rules | high yields on watered land; disputes and rules over water; capture of head-works |
| Maritime network | coasts or seasonal winds; boats; entrepôts at straits; trust across ports | merchant diasporas; port law; prices joined across distant ports |
| Settler frontier | open land, an expanding state behind it, people already there, a route to metropolitan markets | high wages and early marriage; conflict with prior inhabitants; boom, bust and export rescue |
| Plantation and slave economy | an export crop with scale economies, coercive law, a supply of captives, distant buyers | gang labour; extreme inequality; persistence of inequality after abolition |

**Transitions a world can host, and what each needs it to hold.**
- **Neolithic:** wild plants that respond to cultivation, a stable climate, enough sedentism that birth spacing shortens; read as more
  people, not better-off people.
- **State formation:** an appropriable surplus, bounded land, records, rival polities.
- **Commercial revolution:** peace on the roads, rising population, market places and days, credit, town liberties.
- **Agricultural revolution:** fodder crops and rotations, livestock that convert fodder to manure, land rights that let farmers invest.
- **Industrial revolution:** a mineral fuel, expensive labour, a shared stock of useful knowledge and skilled mechanics.
- **Demographic transition:** falling child mortality, returns to schooling, and a way for income gains to stop becoming births.
- **Service and global transition:** cheap transmission of instructions over distance, so production can split while control gathers.
- **Collapse:** a fiscal centre or a long chain whose failure strands specialists; read depth lost against what survived (people,
  routes, knowledge).
