## Summary

These five domains sit at the edges of the production chains the other parts trace, but each one sets a limit on them. **Disease**
decided where people could live, how many hands there were and what labour cost. The crowd diseases came from herds and needed
towns to persist; the Black Death cut the labour force by a third to a half and raised its bargaining power; Old World pathogens emptied the
Americas and made the plantation economy; malaria and yellow fever drew the map of European settlement in the tropics. Healing grew
from household herb lore and midwifery into licensed trades, a long-distance drug trade and endowed hospitals, before sanitation,
germ theory and the dye firms' chemistry made public health and part 13's drug chain. **Environment** is the other face of part 01's organic economy: the same
land that gave food, fuel and timber could be stripped, worn out, salted or overfished, and every society that lasted found rules
for its commons, while some ran into limits. The collapse stories most often told (Rapa Nui, the Maya, Norse Greenland) are
contested, and the critiques matter as much as the stories.

**Luxury** drove most long-distance trade before the nineteenth century, because only goods with high value for their weight could
pay for the journey: silk, pepper and nutmeg, purple and cochineal, sugar, tea and porcelain each built a chain across continents,
and states tried to fix who might wear or eat them (sumptuary laws). The consumer revolution of the seventeenth and eighteenth
centuries turned some of them into mass goods and pulled households into the market. **Crime and predation** (theft, banditry, piracy, smuggling and fraud) are costs that set how
far trade reaches, and nearly every early institution (watchmen, oaths, frankpledge, guild searches, sealed cloth, convoys, police)
answered one of them. **Measures and standards** are the quiet public good under every exchange: from Indus chert weights and the
silver shekel through Magna Carta's one measure and the assize, to the metric system, standard screw threads, the ISO container and
the redefined kilogram. Each step lowered the cost of trusting a stranger's quantity, and each had its own fraud.

The engine already holds pieces of all five: disease by host and area in the wild and in kept herds (on the `ecology` branch), the
wild ecology and the soil, forests that turn to scrub, theft by `take` without right and raids by any party, poaching against
rights in wild kinds, and extravagance in tiers for buildings. Inside the border the gap is less in physics than in the social side
([[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]]): judging a theft, a sworn measure, a stint enforced. **On the figures.** *Checked*: confirmed against a source this

## 1. Health and medicine

### 1.1 Disease as an economic force

**The ladder of hosts.** Foragers at under one person a square kilometre carried mostly parasites and slow infections that could
persist in small groups (worms, yaws, herpes viruses) and zoonoses caught from the animals they killed. Farming changed three things
at once: people lived with herds, they lived in one place beside their own waste and stores (rats, flies, water fouled by dung), and
they became numerous. The **crowd diseases** (measles, smallpox, mumps, rubella, pertussis) give lasting immunity and kill or
immunise their hosts quickly, so they die out unless a population is big enough to supply new susceptibles by birth every year.
Bartlett's estimate for measles is a connected population of about 250,000 to 500,000 (*unchecked*, a standard epidemiological
figure). No such disease could exist before cities and their hinterlands; once they did, childhood became the season of infection
and towns became reservoirs that reseeded the countryside.

**Zoonoses from herding.** Many human crowd diseases began as animal diseases:

| Disease | Animal source (current reading) | When it became human (as far as known) | Economic note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **measles** | rinderpest of cattle | the two viruses diverged around the sixth century BCE (mean 528 BCE, range about 1174 BCE to 165 CE) by a molecular clock on a 1912 genome (Düx et al. 2020, *checked*) | appears when cities pass the critical size; rinderpest stayed the great cattle plague until its eradication in 2011 (*memory*) |
| **smallpox** | a poxvirus of rodents (closest relatives taterapox and camelpox) | uncertain; aDNA shows Viking-age strains (*memory*) | the leading killer of the early modern city and of the Americas after 1492 |
| **tuberculosis** | long thought from cattle; the genomes suggest humans gave it to cattle more often than the reverse (*contested*) | ancient | the disease of the crowded industrial city and of dairy milk before pasteurisation |
| **influenza** | wild birds via pigs and poultry | recurrent | each pandemic a short, sharp shock to labour (1918: tens of millions dead, *rough*) |
| **plague** (*Yersinia pestis*) | rodents and their fleas | ancient DNA from the late Neolithic and Bronze Age steppe (*memory*) | the Justinianic plague (541 onward) and the Black Death (1347 onward) |
| **brucellosis, anthrax, Q fever** | sheep, goats, cattle | with herding | occupational: herders, butchers, tanners, wool sorters ("wool-sorters' disease" is anthrax) |

The engine's wild layer already has this shape for beasts (§7): murrain in deer and cattle, swine fever in boar and pigs, carried
between wild hosts and kept herds where they share ground.

**The Black Death and wages.** Parts 05 (§6.4) and 06 (§6.3) give the shock: perhaps a third to a half of Europe dead in 1347 to
1352, recurring for three centuries; English real farm wages up by about two thirds; the Ordinance (1349) and Statute (1351) of
Labourers trying and failing to hold wages at their pre-plague level. Three further chains run from it:

- **Land to pasture.** With fewer hands, land went from grain to sheep, which need little labour. English wool and then cloth
  exports grew (part 03, part 05), and the deserted villages of the fifteenth century are partly a plague effect.
- **Consumption rose.** Survivors ate more meat and drank more ale, and labourers dressed above their station. The English
  **Statute concerning Diet and Apparel of 1363** set out, rank by rank, what cloth and food each might use (*checked*): the first
  big English sumptuary law answers a wage rise (§3.3).
- **Labour-saving capital.** Dear labour after 1350 may have hastened labour-saving devices such as printing (David Herlihy,
  *contested*); Voigtländer and Voth's high-income argument is in part 06.

**The Columbian exchange of disease.** Part 11 (§6.3) gives the scale: about 56 million deaths in the Americas by 1600, roughly 90%
of the population (Koch et al. 2019, *contested in detail*), from smallpox, measles, influenza and later malaria and yellow fever
brought with enslaved Africans. Its economic consequence was a continent with land and no labour: encomienda and the mita drafted
the survivors (part 11), and from the late sixteenth century the Atlantic slave trade supplied the plantations (12.5 million
embarked, part 11). Disease went the other way much less (syphilis is the usual candidate, *contested*), because the Americas had
few herd animals and so few crowd diseases.

**Malaria and settlement.** Malaria shaped settlement more slowly but over more of the world:

- **Lowland Europe.** The Roman Campagna and the Pontine Marshes stayed malarial until drainage and quinine in the twentieth
  century. England's Fens and Essex marshes had "ague", with burial rates far above upland parishes (Mary Dobson, 1997,
  *unchecked*); draining them was a land, transport and health project at once.
- **The tropics.** European death rates in West Africa were so high (Philip Curtin's soldiers' series: often half or more of new
  arrivals dead in the first year, *unchecked*) that the coast was called "the white man's grave", and Europeans traded from forts
  and ships rather than settling until quinine prophylaxis (from the 1850s) opened the interior. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson
  (2001) used settler mortality to argue that where Europeans died they built extractive rather than settler institutions
  (*contested*: Albouy's critique of the mortality data).
- **Labour and immunity.** West Africans' partial resistance to falciparum malaria (sickle-cell trait, Duffy negativity) and to
  yellow fever is one strand of why planters in the malarial Caribbean and Lower South bought enslaved Africans rather than hiring
  Europeans (*contested*; Curtin, Coclanis, Esposito).
- **Canals and wars.** The French Panama canal (1881 to 1889) lost perhaps 20,000 workers to yellow fever and malaria (*rough*);
  the American one (1904 to 1914) succeeded after Gorgas's mosquito control. Yellow fever destroyed the French army in
  Saint-Domingue (1802 to 1803), which helped make Haiti independent (*memory*).
- **Hookworm** in the American South: the Rockefeller Sanitary Commission's campaign (1909 to 1914) raised school attendance and
  later earnings in the most infected counties (Bleakley 2007, *unchecked*). It is the clearest modern case of a disease as a tax on
  human capital.

**The urban penalty.** Part 12 (§1.7) gives London's 8,000 net migrants a year and Wrigley's model; part 05 gives life expectancy of
35 to 40 years in early modern England. The penalty grew worse with industrial crowding before it fell: life expectancy in
Manchester and Liverpool in the 1830s and 1840s was well below the English average (Simon Szreter's "urban penalty", *unchecked*),
and heights of British-born men fell for cohorts born in the 1830s to 1850s (*contested*). Cholera did less of the killing than
typhoid, tuberculosis and infant diarrhoea, but it frightened the propertied and moved Parliament (answers in parts 08 and 12).

**Livestock disease.** Herd plagues were economic shocks of their own. Rinderpest reached the Horn of Africa in 1887 to 1889 and
swept south to the Cape by 1897, killing perhaps 80 to 90% of cattle in many places (*rough*), with famine for herding peoples and
the spread of bush and tsetse onto abandoned grazing. Britain's cattle plague of 1865 to 1867 brought compulsory slaughter with
compensation and a state veterinary service (*memory*): a state destroying private property to protect a shared stock, as in the
foot-and-mouth cull of 2001 (*rough*: about six million animals).

### 1.2 Healing as a trade

Most healing, through most of history, was done at home by women with plants from the garden and the hedge. The trades below
grew up beside that household care, each with a different good: knowledge, skill of hand, drugs, or a bed.

| Trade | What it sold | Where it came from | Institutions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **household and herbal healing** | care; simples (single herbs) from garden and wild | every society; the cunning folk and wise women of Europe | none; reputation |
| **midwives** | skill at birth; later a licensed office | universal; in England licensed by bishops from the early 16th century (*memory*) | church licence; oaths (not to swap or hide infants, to fetch help); later the Midwives Act 1902 (*memory*) |
| **barber-surgeons** | hands: bleeding, setting bones, pulling teeth, wounds, amputations | towns and armies; the Company of Barber-Surgeons of London from 1540 (*memory*) | craft guild; apprenticeship; war as training ground |
| **physicians** | learned diagnosis and prescription; fees | Greek and Islamic medicine; universities (Salerno, Montpellier, Bologna, Paris); the London College of Physicians (1518, *memory*) | university degree; college licence; monopoly in a city |
| **apothecaries** | compounded drugs from imported and local materia medica | the grocers and spicers: London's were in the Grocers' Company ("grossers, pepperers and apothecaries") until their own Society was chartered in 1617 (*checked*) | the Rose case (decided by the Lords in 1704) let apothecaries prescribe as well as dispense (*checked*); the Apothecaries Act 1815 made the Society the licensing body for general practice in England and Wales (*checked*) |
| **hospitals** | beds, care and food; later surgery and teaching | the Islamic *bimaristan* (Baghdad, Damascus, Cairo's al-Mansuri of 1284: *memory*); monastic infirmaries; St Bartholomew's (1123, *memory*); the Hôtel-Dieu | endowment (*waqf*, monastic land, charity); later voluntary subscription (Guy's, 1721: *memory*), then rates and the state |
| **quacks and patent medicines** | hope, by advertisement | fairs; then newspapers (the 18th-century patent medicine trade was among the biggest advertisers) | none, then drug acts |

**The chain of the apothecary.** An early modern apothecary's shelves are a map of world trade: pepper, ginger and cinnamon from the
spice routes; rhubarb root from China by caravan; aloes from Socotra; senna from Egypt; opium from Anatolia and later India; sugar,
first sold as a drug (part 11 §4.3); tobacco, guaiacum wood from the Caribbean for syphilis (the Fuggers held a lucrative import
monopoly, *memory*); and **theriac**, compounded publicly in Venice from dozens of ingredients under officials who certified the
batch (*memory*). The trade's shape was luxury's: high value for weight, long
routes, a few ports of entry, adulteration a constant risk, and guild searches of the shops to find it (§4.6).

**Cinchona and quinine** show a drug becoming a strategic good. Andean cinchona bark was taken up by Jesuits in the 1630s as a cure
for fevers ("Jesuits' bark"; *checked* as to the decade). Pelletier and Caventou isolated quinine in 1820 (*checked*). Peru and its
neighbours banned the export of seeds and saplings to keep the trade; the British took seeds to India and Ceylon and the Dutch to
Java, where plantations of high-yield *Cinchona ledgeriana* came to dominate world supply; a Quinine Agreement of 1913 among Dutch
growers and makers is called the first international pharmaceutical cartel (*checked*, secondary). Quinine opened the African
interior to European armies and traders (above), and Japan's capture of Java in 1942 cut the Allies off (*checked*), which pushed
synthetic antimalarials. The pattern (a wild drug, a biopiracy, a plantation, a cartel, a synthetic) repeats with rubber.

**Opium** is the drug trade at the scale of states. The East India Company held a monopoly of opium in Bengal from the late
eighteenth century and sold it at auction in Calcutta to private traders who smuggled it into China, where it paid for the tea the
Company bought at Canton; the silver that had flowed into China (part 11) began to flow out; China's attempt to stop it led to the
Opium Wars (1839 to 1842, 1856 to 1860) and the treaty ports (*memory*). Drug, luxury, smuggling and finance were one chain here
(§3, §4.5).

### 1.3 From healing to public health and pharmaceuticals

The modern chain grew in steps, each resting on the one before. The middle steps are told in parts 08 and 12 and the end in part
13; this list shows how they join.

```
public health and the drug chain (each step needs the one above)
├── quarantine (Ragusa 1377) and lazarettos (Venice 1423): the state isolates ships and goods (in the web)
│   └── plague leaves western Europe by the 1720s (part 05)
├── smallpox inoculation (from the Ottoman world into England, 1721) → Jenner's vaccination (1796): the first mass
│   preventive, a product made from cows (*memory*)
├── statistics: bills of mortality (London, from the 16th century); William Farr's registration (1837 onward) shows which
│   districts kill (*memory*)
├── sanitation: piped water, intercepting sewers, filtration and chlorination (parts 08, 12), boards and medical officers of health
├── germ theory (Snow 1854, Pasteur, Koch: in the web) → diagnostic labs, pasteurised milk, antitoxins (diphtheria, 1890s)
├── the dye firms make drugs: aspirin (1899), Salvarsan (1910), sulfa drugs (1930s): part 08 §3
├── penicillin by deep-tank fermentation (1940s): part 08
├── regulation: proof of safety (1938) and of efficacy (1962) after mass poisonings: part 08
└── the drug as a global chain: R&D, trials, API in India and China, generics: part 13 §2.6
```

**Health as a sector.** Health care was a small share of spending until the twentieth century. By the 2020s it is about 17% of US
GDP and around 9 to 12% in most rich countries (*rough*, OECD), most of it services (nursing, doctors, hospitals) rather than goods.
Payment moved from the purse and charity through friendly societies (part 05) to Bismarck's sickness insurance
(1883) and national systems (*memory*). The economic return showed in the body itself: Robert Fogel's work on height and
mortality argued that better nutrition and health explain a large share of British growth since 1790 (*contested*).

## 2. Environment and depletion

### 2.1 Fuel and forest

Part 01 gives the organic economy's fuel budget (a tonne or two of wood a person a year; 150 to 200 kg of wood for a kilogram of
forest glass) and part 12 the fuel ring round a city (firewood paid only within about 20 km of London). What follows is the same
fact over time: woods near people were used faster than they grew, and each society met the shortage with one of four answers:
**move the industry to the woods, manage the woods, ration them by law, or switch fuel.**

| Case | What ran short | The answer | Figures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **London, 13th century** | firewood and charcoal within carting distance | sea coal from the Tyne for lime-burners, smiths and brewers; complaints of the smoke led to a royal proclamation against burning it in 1306 (*memory*) | coal at a few thousand tons a year then (*rough*); see part 12 |
| **England 1550 to 1650** | fuel and timber near towns and ironworks | coal for houses and many trades; the 1543 Act for the Preservation of Woods; the 1615 ban on wood in glass furnaces (both in the web) | J. U. Nef's "timber crisis" (1932) is **contested**: Michael Flinn and G. Hammersley found ironmasters managed coppices on rotation and the shortage was local and of large timber (part 03) |
| **Venice and the navies** | oak for galleys and ships of the line | state forests (Venice's Montello), surveys, Baltic and American imports (*memory*; in the web: naval oak) | a 74-gun ship took 2,000 to 3,500 oaks (part 03) |
| **Song China** | wood in the north round Kaifeng | coal for iron, kilns and homes (part 11) | |
| **Tokugawa Japan** | old-growth forest, logged out by about 1670 for castles, temples and towns (Totman, *checked*) | a turn from "exploitation" to "regenerative" forestry: felling bans and inventories (the "negative regimen"), then planted forests, partly by coercion of villages and partly by village self-regulation (Totman 1989; *checked* as an argument) | population near 30 million by 1700 (*rough*) |
| **Britain, 1865** | coal itself, in the long run | W. S. Jevons, *The Coal Question*: more efficient engines raise, not lower, coal use (the "Jevons paradox") (*memory*) | |

**The general pattern** (parts 01, 03, 07): a fuel shortage was rarely absolute; it was a rise in the cost of carrying fuel from
further away. Industries that burned many times their weight in fuel (iron, glass, salt, lime) moved to the woods or to the coal,
and the wood famine of one era was the coal industry of the next.

### 2.2 Soil: exhaustion and erosion

**Exhaustion** was the everyday limit. Cropping without returning nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium lowered yields until the land
was rested; part 02 traces the long answer (fallow, legumes, manure, guano, Haber–Bosch) and part 05 the commons that governed
grazing on the fallow. Two cases show exhaustion moving whole economies:

- **Chesapeake tobacco** (17th and 18th centuries). Tobacco drew hard on the soil; planters cropped a field for three or four years,
  then let it go to old field and cleared new ground (*rough*). The economy moved west with the frontier, needed land more than
  capital, and fed the demand for enslaved labour to clear it. Later reformers (Edmund Ruffin's marl, 1830s) answered with lime
  (*memory*).
- **Cotton in the American South**, and later coffee in São Paulo, followed the same "mining the soil" path to new land.

**Erosion** is exhaustion made physical: soil carried off a hillside once its cover is gone. Valley fills in the Mediterranean
(Claudio Vita-Finzi's "Younger Fill") were once read as Roman and medieval erosion caused by clearance; later work separates
climate from land use (*contested*). Plato's *Critias* already describes Attica's hills "like the skeleton of a sick man". The
clearest modern case is the **American Dust Bowl** (1930s): ploughing the southern Plains in a wet run of years, then drought, then
wind; the response was the Soil Conservation Service (1935), contour ploughing, shelterbelts and payments to take land out of
crops (*memory*). Terraces (the Andes, part 11; the engine's `terrace` technique) were the oldest answer to erosion on slopes.

### 2.3 Salt in the fields

Irrigation without drainage brings salt to the surface. Parts 06 and 10 give the southern Mesopotamian case (Thorkild Jacobsen and
Robert Adams, 1958: wheat giving way to more salt-tolerant barley and yields falling between about 2400 and 1700 BCE) and Marvin
Powell's critique of it (*contested*); it is in the web as field salinisation. Modern cases are better measured: large areas of the
Indus basin in Pakistan were waterlogged and salted after canal irrigation expanded, and tube wells were drilled to lower the water
table (*rough*); the **Aral Sea** shrank to a small fraction of its volume after Soviet cotton irrigation took the Amu Darya and Syr
Darya from the 1960s, with salt and dust blown off the dry bed (*memory*). Salinity is a slow, invisible chokepoint: it builds for
generations under a system that works, and it falls on whoever farms at the end of the canal.

### 2.4 Overhunting and overfishing

Wild stocks are common-pool resources (part 01): rival and hard to fence. Taking them for a market rather than for the pot
changed the arithmetic, because demand no longer stopped when the hunters were fed.

| Stock | Chain | What happened | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pleistocene megafauna** | hunting for meat | mammoths, giant sloths and others vanished as people arrived in the Americas and Australia; human "overkill" against climate is **contested** | none |
| **beaver** | fur for felt hats in Europe (in the web: fur trade) | trapped out region by region, the trade moving west and north across North America from the 17th century (*memory*) | the Hudson's Bay Company's later quotas in some districts (*memory*) |
| **American bison** | hides for industrial belting and leather once tanners could use them (1870s), carried by the new railways | from perhaps 30 to 60 million about 1800 to about 500 to 540 counted by Hornaday in 1889 (*checked*) | the American Bison Society (1905); reserves |
| **passenger pigeon** | market hunting by rail and telegraph | from flocks of billions to the last bird dead in 1914 (*memory*) | the Lacey Act (1900) against interstate trade in illegally taken game (*memory*) |
| **whales** | oil for light and lubrication, baleen (in the web: whaling) | right whales and then bowheads, sperm and blue whales hunted down in turn as ships and harpoons improved | the 1946 whaling convention; the moratorium of 1986 (*memory*) |
| **cod, herring** | salt fish, stockfish, then trawlers and factory ships (part 03) | Newfoundland's northern cod collapsed by 1992 (part 03, in the web) | moratoria, quotas, exclusive economic zones (in the web) |

**Old answers to the fishing commons.** Medieval rivers already had them: close seasons for salmon, rules on the mesh of nets and on
weirs that blocked the whole river (Magna Carta's fish-weir clause, part 05), and lords' fisheries let by lease. The engine's river
as "one site with many claims" (02zzk) is this problem.

### 2.5 Smoke and pollution

Pollution is the waste stream of a chain landing on someone outside it: an **externality** (A. C. Pigou, 1920; Ronald Coase's 1960
reply that with clear rights and cheap bargaining the parties can settle it themselves). The history is a series of chains that grew
until their waste was intolerable, then a law.

| Chain | Waste | Where it fell | The answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| tanning, retting, dyeing, slaughter | foul water, offal, lime | the river below the town; fishermen and drinkers downstream | town ordinances putting the trades downstream or outside the walls (part 12; the web's river ordinances) |
| coal burning in London | smoke and soot | the whole city | the 1306 proclamation; John Evelyn's *Fumifugium* (1661) proposed moving the smoky trades out (*memory*); nothing effective for three centuries |
| the Leblanc soda process | hydrochloric acid gas, alkali waste | crops and woods round the works | the Alkali Act 1863: 95% of the acid gas to be absorbed, with inspectors (part 07, in the web); the recovered acid became a product (bleaching powder) |
| city sewage | the river as a sewer | water drinkers; the Great Stink of 1858 | intercepting sewers, then treatment (parts 08, 12); the Rivers Pollution Prevention Act 1876 (*memory*) |
| coal smoke at its worst | smog | London, December 1952: about 4,000 deaths counted at the time, 10,000 to 12,000 in later excess-mortality estimates (*checked*) | the Clean Air Act 1956: smokeless zones and controls on domestic and industrial smoke (*checked*) |
| lead, sulphur, CFCs, carbon | leaded petrol (phased out worldwide by 2021); acid rain; ozone loss; warming | children's blood; lakes downwind; the whole atmosphere | bans; the US SO2 cap-and-trade (1990); the Montreal Protocol (1987); carbon prices (EU ETS, 2005) (*memory*) |

**The pattern of the answer.** The first answers were moving the trade (downstream, outside the walls, to the edge of town); the
next were technical (absorb the gas, sell the by-product); then came inspectors with powers, then priced permits. The waste became
an input surprisingly often: Leblanc acid to bleaching powder, gasworks tar to dyes (part 07), city night soil to farms (part 12).

### 2.6 The commons and their regulation

Part 05 (§2.3) gives Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" and Ostrom's eight design principles, with the medieval village's stint, by-
laws, hayward and court as an example. Three things to add from the environmental side:

1. **Regulated commons were the norm, open access the exception.** Swiss alpine meadows (Törbel), Japanese village forests
   (*iriaichi*, the commons that Totman's villages governed), Spanish irrigation courts (Valencia's Tribunal de las Aguas, meeting
   weekly for centuries, *memory*) and Maine lobster "harbour gangs" (*memory*) all kept a resource for centuries.
2. **Commons failed when a market, a new technique or an outsider broke the boundary.** The bison and the passenger pigeon were
   taken by outsiders for distant markets; the Atlantic cod by new trawlers from many nations; the beaver as the fur frontier moved.
3. **States have answered in three ways:** reserve the resource to the crown (forest law, naval timber), privatise it (enclosure,
   individual fishing quotas, both in the web), or price its use (tolls, permits, carbon prices). Each makes a new owner, a rent and
   a quarrel (02zzj README, finding 4); poaching is the quarrel's everyday form (part 03, part 05; in the engine as [[D2-194 A world may give rights in wild kinds by law at its start|D2-194]]).

## 3. Luxury, status and consumption

### 3.1 Why luxuries carried long-distance trade

Before rail and steam, carriage over land cost so much (part 04: a cart added about 3% to wheat's price every ten miles) that only
goods with a high value for their weight paid for a journey of months: part 01's "luxury and status good", whose demand rises
faster than income. Grain, timber and salt fish moved far only by water and to rich cities (Rome's annona, the Baltic grain trade).
So the first long-distance chains were luxuries, from the prestige goods of part 10 (Spondylus, amber, lapis, obsidian) onward, and
the routes, credit and ships built for them were later used for bulk.

### 3.2 The luxury chains

Three regularities run through the table. **Narrow origins made monopolies**: nutmeg from one small archipelago, porcelain from
one kiln town, cochineal from one region, purple from one coast, and states and companies fought for them (the Bandas; Saxony's
secret; Spain's ban on exporting the insect). **The monopoly ended by transfer or synthesis**: smuggled silkworms, stolen tea plants
and cinchona seeds, Meissen's porcelain, aniline dyes. **Luxuries fell into mass goods** as supply widened: sugar, tea, tobacco,
cotton cloth (part 11) and later chocolate went from courts to labourers within two centuries, which is the consumer revolution of
§3.5.

### 3.3 Sumptuary laws

Sumptuary laws fixed who might wear, eat or display what. They are found in Rome (the *lex Oppia*, 215 BCE), Tang and Ming China,
Tokugawa Japan, the Italian city-states (limits on dowries, funeral pomp and women's dress), and England (the 1363 statute of §1.1,
then a series of acts of apparel to 1604, *memory*). They served four aims, often at once:

1. **To keep rank visible** when wealth no longer matched birth (England after the Black Death; Italian merchants richer than nobles).
2. **To stop money leaving** for imported luxuries (silk, spices, Venetian glass): a mercantilist aim.
3. **To protect a home industry**: Elizabethan caps acts ordering Sunday woollen caps for the cappers; the Calico Acts of 1700 and
   1721 (part 11, in the web) for the wool and silk weavers against Indian cottons.
4. **To restrain display and debt** in the name of religion or order.

They were widely evaded and seldom enforced for long, and most were repealed or forgotten by the 18th century. Their economic
lesson is that status goods are **positional** (Fred Hirsch, 1976): their value is relative, so when wealth spreads the old marks
lose value and the rich move to new ones, which is how fashion turns over.

### 3.4 Feasting and display

Feasts are the oldest status good and an economic institution in their own right: they turn a surplus that cannot be stored into
obligation and alliance. Part 10's monumental feasting at Göbekli Tepe and part 06's potlatch and Melanesian big men are the
examples; Brian Hayden argues that competitive feasting drove early surplus and even domestication (*contested*). The medieval great
household spent most of its income on food, drink, livery cloth for its retainers and hospitality (Christopher Dyer: food and drink
often half or more of a noble household's spending, *rough*); a lord's hall, a church tower, a guild's feast or a wedding were how
wealth became standing. Display in building is the same: the engine's extravagance tiers (fine, rich, grand) cost more materials
and work and are noticed further away, which is exactly what display buys (§7).

### 3.5 The consumer revolution and fashion

From the later 17th century, households in England and the Dutch Republic bought far more goods than before: earthenware and
china, pewter and then cutlery, clocks, looking-glasses, curtains, printed cottons, books, tea things (Lorna Weatherill's probate
inventories, 1660 to 1760: *unchecked*, the counts not re-verified; Carole Shammas). Neil McKendrick named a "consumer revolution"
(1982) and credited entrepreneurs like **Josiah Wedgwood**: royal patronage advertised as "Queen's Ware", showrooms in London,
pattern books, travelling salesmen and fixed prices. Jan de Vries's **industrious revolution** (part 05 §1.9, in the web) ties this
demand to supply: households worked more days and sold more of their labour to buy the new goods, and that labour fed proto-industry
and then the factory. Fashion became an industry with its own chain: Lyon's silk designers changed patterns each season; Paris
fashion dolls and then fashion plates carried styles across Europe (*memory*); the second-hand clothing trade (in the web) let
servants and labourers buy into them.

Theories of why people buy status: **Thorstein Veblen** (*The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899: conspicuous consumption and
leisure as proof of wealth); **Georg Simmel** (1904: fashion trickles down as the lower orders imitate and the upper move on);
**Pierre Bourdieu** (*Distinction*, 1979: taste as cultural capital). Mandeville's *Fable of the Bees* (1714) had already argued that private
vanity set the poor to work.

### 3.6 Status goods today

Personal luxury goods are a market of a few hundred billion euros a year (about €360 billion in 2023 by Bain's estimate, *rough*,
unchecked this session), led by a few conglomerates whose margins come from brands, controlled scarcity (waiting lists, limited
editions, destroying unsold stock) and control of retail rather than from materials. Diamonds show the same logic at the source: De
Beers held most of the world's rough diamond trade for most of the 20th century and marketed scarcity ("a diamond is forever", 1947)
until mined supply diversified and synthetic stones arrived (*memory*). Counterfeits are luxury's shadow trade (§4.5). And the
positional point holds: as incomes rise, more of spending goes on goods whose value lies in their rank (housing in the best
districts, schooling, brands), which is one reason growth does not end scarcity.

## 4. Crime and predation

### 4.1 Predation as a cost that sets the reach of trade

Every exchange and every journey carries a risk of loss to someone who takes rather than trades. That risk is part of transport
cost: a road that is unsafe is long, and a market can grow only as far as goods are safe in it. Frederic Lane (1958) called the
price of safety **protection rent**: Venice, the Hanse and the Portuguese sold protection, and those who could buy it cheaper (by
convoy, by treaty, by their own fleet) took the trade. Mancur Olson (1993) turned it into a theory of the state: a **roving bandit**
takes all he can; a **stationary bandit** who expects to stay takes a regular share and protects the rest, because he gains from his
subjects' output. Charles Tilly made war-making and state-making "organised crime" in the same sense (part 05). The institutions
in this section are the answers to each kind of taking.

### 4.2 Theft and its prevention

| Era | How property was protected | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **villages** | houses with people in them; dogs; neighbours who saw and knew; the hue and cry raised by anyone robbed | in England everyone had to join the hue and cry and follow a thief from village to village (*memory*) |
| **medieval England** | **frankpledge** (tithings of about ten men answerable for each other, in the web); the Statute of Winchester (1285): town gates shut at night, watchmen, brush cleared 200 feet each side of highways to stop ambush (*memory*) | the constable chosen in turn (part 05) |
| **merchants** | locks, strongboxes, seals on bales, sworn brokers, innkeepers liable for guests' goods (*memory*) | |
| **early modern cities** | paid night watch; thief-takers paid by reward (Jonathan Wild, hanged 1725, ran theft and the return of stolen goods together, *memory*); severe penalties (the "Bloody Code"); transportation to America and then Australia | |
| **police** | the Bow Street Runners (1749, *memory*); the Metropolitan Police (1829, in the web); detectives (1842, *memory*) | prevention by patrol replaced recovery by reward |
| **today** | police, private security, alarms, insurance, card fraud systems | private security staff outnumber police in many countries (*rough*) |

Samuel Bowles and Arjun Jayadev counted "guard labour" (police, guards, supervisors, the military, prison staff) and found it a
large share of the US workforce, larger where inequality is higher (*contested* in its definitions). Whatever the share, guarding
is labour taken out of making, which is why theft costs more than what is stolen.

### 4.3 Banditry and robbery on the roads

Bandits flourished where states were weak or borders near: the Balkan *hajduks*, Chinese mountain bandits, Italian *briganti* after
unification in the 1860s, English highwaymen of the 17th and 18th centuries. Eric Hobsbawm's **social bandit** (*Bandits*, 1969),
robbing the rich and protected by peasants, is **contested** by Anton Blok and others, who found most bandits in the service of
landlords. The economic effect is plain either way: caravans travelled armed or with paid escorts; the caravanserai and the funduq
(part 11, in the web) were also strongholds; tolls on the Rhine taken by castle-holders ("robber barons") were robbery licensed by
custom (*memory*); and states that cleared the roads (the Mongol yam, the Inca roads, Roman peace) widened markets (part 04). The
engine has the roving bandit already: a raid by any party, with orders to take and burn (§7).

### 4.4 Piracy and privateering

| Case | Shape | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Cilician pirates** (2nd to 1st century BCE) | raids and slaving across the Mediterranean, feeding Delos's slave market | Pompey's command of 67 BCE cleared the sea in months (*memory*) |
| **Vikings** | raid and trade from the same ships; tribute (Danegeld) | fortified bridges and towns; paying; settling them |
| **Wokou** (East Asian coasts, 14th to 16th centuries) | Japanese and Chinese smugglers and raiders, fed by Ming bans on private sea trade | the Ming lifted the ban in 1567 and the raids fell (*memory*): piracy as the shadow of a trade ban |
| **Barbary corsairs** (16th to 18th centuries) | raids on ships and coasts for ransom and slaves | Robert Davis estimates 1 to 1.25 million Europeans enslaved, 1530 to 1780 (*checked* as his estimate; **contested**); states paid tribute or sent fleets; redemption societies raised ransoms |

The Somali figures show the general rule: the main cost of predation is not what is taken but **the cost of avoiding it**: convoys,
escorts, longer routes, armed guards, insurance premiums (part 04's marine insurance grew partly to spread this risk).

### 4.5 Smuggling

Smuggling is the trade that a duty, monopoly or ban makes profitable; its size measures how far the rule sits above what people
will pay to avoid it.

- **Tea in 18th-century England**: with duties around 119%, much of the tea drunk was run in from the Continent by organised gangs
  (the Hawkhurst Gang); the Commutation Act (1784) cut the duty to 12.5% and legal sales rose (*checked*). The lesson, put by Adam
  Smith, is that a lower duty can raise more revenue.
- **Salt**: France's *gabelle* made salt many times dearer in some provinces than in others, and the *faux-sauniers* who carried it
  across were hunted by a large armed customs force (*memory*). China's salt monopoly (part 11, Han) had the same shadow.
- **Opium into China** (§1.2); **rum and spirits** under American Prohibition (1920 to 1933); **cigarettes, drugs and people** today.
- **Counterfeits** (fake brands and medicines) were put at about 2.5% of world trade in 2016 (OECD and EUIPO, *rough*, unchecked).

### 4.6 Fraud: debasing, short weight, adulteration

| Fraud | How | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **debasing** coin | the issuer puts less silver in: a hidden tax (the denarius, the Great Debasement of 1544 to 1551: part 04, part 05, in the web) | reputation; recoinage; later, central banks and inflation targets |
| **clipping and sweating** coin | users shave silver from hammered coins | milled edges (England from 1662) and the Great Recoinage of 1696 under Newton at the Mint (*memory*) |
| **counterfeiting** | false coin and notes | treason law; engraved notes; watermarks |
| **short weight and measure** | false weights, a bushel "struck" or "heaped" as suits the seller (§5) | sworn standards, market clerks, the muhtasib (in the web), the assize (in the web) |
| **false quality** | bad cloth stretched; base metal sold as silver | guild searchers and seals (cloth aulnagers sealed each cloth; the goldsmiths' hallmark in London from 1300, *memory*) |
| **adulteration** | alum in bread, water in milk and beer, sand in sugar, coloured tea leaves | Friedrich Accum's *Treatise on Adulterations of Food, and Culinary Poisons* (early 19th century; 1820, *memory*); the Bradford sweets poisoning of 1858 (arsenic sold in place of a cheap filler: over 200 poisoned, 21 dead, *checked*) helped pass the Adulteration of Food Act (1860) and then the Sale of Food and Drugs Act (1875, *memory*); public analysts; in the US the Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act (1906, part 02) |

Adulteration grew with the distance between maker and buyer: in a village the baker was known; in a city of a million, food passed
through many hands, each a chance to cheat, and chemistry made cheating easier before it made detecting it possible. Part 13's
melamine in Chinese milk (2008, *memory*) is the same fraud in a global chain.

### 4.7 The institutions that answer, and what each needed

Each answer above needed something before it. **Watchmen** and **frankpledge** needed a community that could be made answerable;
**oaths and sureties** (in the web) needed witnesses and a court; **guild searches** needed a guild with a charter and the right to
enter shops; **convoys and protection** needed a state or a company with ships; **police** needed a tax base and a city dense enough to
patrol; **inspectors and analysts** needed chemistry and a statute. Avner Greif's community responsibility and the Maghribi
traders' reputation (part 05, in the web) were answers for merchants beyond any one state's reach. In the engine's terms (part 09
P4) they are all enforcement by people.

## 5. Measures and standards

### 5.1 Why measures matter

A measure is a promise about quantity that two strangers can both trust without testing. Without it every exchange needs weighing
against the buyer's own goods, haggling over the vessel, or a trusted third party; with it, prices can be compared between markets,
taxes assessed, rations issued and contracts written at a distance. Witold Kula (*Measures and Men*, 1986) showed that pre-modern
measures were also **social bargains**: the bushel heaped for rent paid in and struck level for wages paid out, the ell longer in
one town than the next, and changing a measure was a way of changing a price. Measurement is a public good in part 01's sense: it
costs someone to maintain and benefits everyone who trades.

**Local measures survived the kings.** On the eve of the Revolution France had hundreds of units under the same names and, by an
often-quoted count, about a quarter of a million local variants of them (*unchecked*). Uniformity had to wait for states strong
enough to override local custom and for trade wide enough to want it.

### 5.3 The metric system and the international standard

The French Academy designed the metric system in 1790 to 1795: the metre as a ten-millionth of the meridian quadrant, the gram from
a cubic centimetre of water, decimal throughout; the platinum *mètre des Archives* and *kilogramme des Archives* were deposited in
1799 (*memory*). Napoleon relaxed it, and France made it compulsory only in 1840 (*memory*). The **Metre Convention** was signed in
Paris on 20 May 1875 by seventeen states, setting up the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) at Sèvres with new
prototypes (*checked*); Britain signed in 1884 (*checked*) but kept imperial units at home (the Weights and Measures Act 1824 had
made them uniform, *memory*). The International System (SI) followed in 1960, and in 2019 every base unit was redefined from fixed
constants of nature (the kilogram from Planck's constant), ending the reign of a metal cylinder in a vault (*memory*).

### 5.4 Industrial standards

The industrial economy needed standards of fit, time and interface, not only of quantity. Each was a coordination problem with
network effects: once enough users chose one, it locked in.

| Standard | What it made possible | Told in |

**Standards wars** are fought because the winner sets the platform: AC against DC, VHS against Betamax, competing mobile standards.
Paul David's QWERTY story (1985) made lock-in famous; whether the losers were better is **contested** (Liebowitz and Margolis).

### 5.5 Fraud in measures, and its regulation

Every measure invited a fraud (§4.6): the false weight, the thumb on the scale, the bushel with a false bottom, the shrunken loaf.
The answers moved from the market clerk and the muhtasib through local inspectors of weights and measures (English counties and
boroughs appointed them under 19th-century acts, *memory*) to national laboratories (Britain's National Physical Laboratory 1900;
the US National Bureau of Standards 1901, *memory*) and trading standards officers. The modern frauds are frauds of measurement at
scale: Volkswagen's emissions "defeat device" (2015), which ran clean only on the test bench, and the rigging of LIBOR, a benchmark
interest rate that priced trillions of dollars of contracts (2012). Both are short weight in a new form.

### 5.6 Measurement as a public good

States measured in order to tax (Domesday, cadastres and censuses, in the web), and the measures then served markets. National accounts, accounting standards, time signals and
satellite positioning follow the same logic: each lowers the cost of decisions for all who read it, and is supplied by a state or a
standards body because no single trader would pay for it. A measure is also a lever: the
one who keeps it can bend it, which is why the keeping was entrusted to sworn officers, to courts and finally to physics.

## 6. How the five margins meet

They meet each other as often as they meet production. **Disease and luxury**: the Black Death raised wages and with them the
display that the 1363 statute tried to stop; the apothecary sold spices as drugs. **Luxury and crime**: high-value goods drew
pirates and smugglers, and the duties and monopolies that made them dear made smuggling pay. **Crime and measures**: short weight,
debasing and adulteration are thefts by measure, and the market's officers answered both. **Environment and health**: drainage cured
ague; sewage fouled the river; smoke killed in 1952. **Environment and luxury**: beaver hats, whale oil, ivory, plumes and
nutmeg emptied wild stocks for distant buyers. The common shape is part 01's: a good worth carrying far escapes the local rules that

**Inside the border, as 02zzk would weigh it.** Herd disease, worn soil and stripped woods are built and already make the
dependence 02zzk's first test asks for: the common grass where murrain spreads, the coppice that goes to scrub if grazed. What the
map adds inside the border is mostly rows on chains the engine has: herbs and simples gathered from the wild plants (a healer's
knowledge with no new handler), the balance and a set of weights as village instruments beside the tally (02zzk already lists them
as new, with [[L-103 Crafting and discovering new things|L-103]]), and the river's fouling by tanning and retting (02zzk, the river as one site). Human sickness by place and
season is the one gap in physics here, and it is already an open question ([[L-9 Disease (mortality, the sick, immunity, nursing, a healer)|L-9]]).

**Behind the social primitives of OPEN [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]].** Most of these domains are social, and wait where 02zzk's lordship and commons wait:
a theft judged and punished rather than only seen; frankpledge and the hue and cry as duties; stints on the common enforced by
impounding; a close season for fish or a ban on cutting the lord's wood; a sworn measure and an assize of bread and ale kept by a
court; a midwife's or a healer's standing held by reputation. Each needs enforcement by people (part 09 P4), visible records (P5)
and rules that create obligations (P7).

**At the border: the market town.** The market clerk with the town's standard measures and the toll; guild searchers and sealed
cloth; the apothecary as a shop in a market town, selling imported drugs and spices; pedlars carrying small luxuries; sumptuary
rules as a town's or a lord's code; highway robbery on the roads between towns. Each needs a market as a place (P6), an institution
with a code (P1, P7) or carts and roads with traffic worth robbing (P14), which 02zzk already places here.

**Past the border: map only.** Epidemics of the Black Death's scale and their wage effects (they need populations by place, P10);
the Columbian exchange; malaria and the geography of settlement; hospitals as endowed institutions; quarantine; the drug trade and
pharmaceuticals; regional deforestation, salinisation and soil erosion over generations; overfishing at sea; pollution law;
long-distance luxury chains and their monopolies; the consumer revolution and fashion; piracy, privateering, smuggling against
customs, police forces; national and metric standards, industrial standards and the container. These need people who are not
agents, a hierarchy of places, edges by sea, or centuries of time (part 09 additions 6 to 9), and they stay on the map.
