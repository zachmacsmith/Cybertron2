## Summary

For almost all of history, people found techniques without knowing why they worked. Cooking that removed poison, heat that made
stone flake better, malt, cheese, tanning, smelting and glass were all found by practice, accident and trial. They were kept by
selection: what failed was dropped, and what worked was copied from the most successful. They were held in hands and households
rather than in words. Such knowledge needs people. Joseph Henrich's "collective brain" argument holds that a craft lasts only while
enough people practise it and learn from the best of them. Tasmania, cut off by the sea, is his case: by contact its toolkit had
about 24 items, with no bone tools and no fish taken (*checked* for the count; *contested* for the cause). Kline and Boyd found that
island population predicts the size and complexity of fishing toolkits across ten Oceanic societies (*checked*). Know-how was
property. It was passed down through households, castes, apprenticeships and guilds, and it was guarded by secrecy (Murano's
glassmakers, the Meissen arcanum, the Chamberlen forceps) and stolen by espionage (Lombe's silk mill, Slater's head full of
Arkwright).

Instruments extended the senses, and each one had a supply chain of its own and opened a field to discovery. The tally and the
token kept counts beyond memory. The balance and its weights judged mass to a fraction of a gram; the touchstone and cupel judged a
coin's gold and silver. Calendar marks and the gnomon read the year, measuring rods the land, water and mechanical clocks the hour,
and compass, quadrant and astrolabe a position at sea. Spectacles came out of clear Venetian glass and lens grinding about 1286.
The telescope (1608) and the microscope came out of the spectacle trade. Barometer and thermometer were filled with mercury from the
silver mines' amalgamation supply. The still and its glassware were the chemist's bench. Later, precision machining, the dividing
engine and national standards made measurement itself an industry. Records became instruments in the same way. Graunt read
London's weekly bills of mortality in 1662, Snow mapped cholera against two water companies in 1854, Lawes and Gilbert laid out
Broadbalk at Rothamsted in 1843, and breeders kept herd and stud books. Counts kept for other purposes turned into a way of finding
things out.

Joel Mokyr's frame joins the two halves. Prescriptive knowledge (how to do something) can grow on its own by trial. It grows faster
and does not stall when it rests on a widening base of propositional knowledge (what is so in nature), and when the cost of reaching
that knowledge falls: books, societies, journals, lectures, the Industrial Enlightenment of about 1700 to 1850. The institutions
that paid for knowledge moved from patrons (Alexandria, the House of Wisdom, Maragha, Uraniborg, the Medici) and religious houses, to
universities, academies and societies, to prizes and patents, to the firm's research laboratory (Edison, the German dye firms, GE,
DuPont, Bell Labs), to state science (the Manhattan Project, about $2.2 billion and about 130,000 people at peak, *checked*; ARPA
from 1958), and finally to R&D as a sector of its own: close to $3 trillion a year, nearly 2% of world GDP, three quarters of it
done by business in the OECD (*checked*). The closing section sets this against Zach's lean of 16 Nov: the engine keeps know-how
and perception, and agents keep knowing-that in their own memories and records. On that line the medieval village needs only the
oldest layer of this history: small grids of trial, surprising signs, local facts, and the tally, balance and calendar mark.

**On the figures.** As in the other parts: *checked*, confirmed against a source this session; *rough*, an order of magnitude or a
range; *contested*, scholars disagree materially; *unchecked*, a standard figure not re-verified here; *memory*, recalled from
standard works and to be checked before use. The shared search budget was small, so many standard dates are *memory*; the standard
reference is named where it helps.

## 1. How techniques were found before science

### 1.1 Practice, accident and trial

Most of the techniques in parts 01 to 03 were found by people who could not have explained them. The few that can be dated show a
common pattern:

- **Heat treatment of stone.** At Pinnacle Point on South Africa's south coast, silcrete was heated before flaking so that it broke
  more cleanly. Brown and colleagues (2009) dated the earliest instances to about 164,000 years ago and routine use to about 72,000
  (*memory*). It is a technique with a narrow window: too little heat does nothing, and too much shatters the stone. It belongs to
  the long chain from the hearth to the kiln to smelting (part 10).
- **Detoxifying food.** Bitter manioc, acorns, cycads and some beans are poisonous raw. People who grated, soaked, leached, fermented
  or cooked them in the right order were safe, and nobody knew about cyanide or tannin. **Nixtamalisation**, cooking maize in an
  alkaline lime or ash solution, released niacin and made maize a sufficient staple in Mesoamerica from roughly 1500 to 1200 BC
  (*rough, memory*). When maize crossed to Europe without the method (part 11's Columbian exchange; `maize_old_world`), pellagra
  followed among the poor who lived on it. Gaspar Casal described it in Asturias in 1735 (*memory*). The know-how travelled less
  well than the crop because nobody could say why it mattered. This is Henrich's favourite example of a cultural adaptation that
  works without being understood.
- **Fermentation and curing.** Malt, ale, cheese, sourdough, salted and smoked fish and tanned hide each rest on microbial or
  chemical processes that were not described until the nineteenth century. What people knew was a sequence of steps, each with a
  range that worked: the steep, the germination, the kiln's heat (part 02); the months in the pit and the strength of the ooze
  (part 03).
- **Metals.** Copper smelting probably came out of high-temperature work with coloured minerals, in pottery kilns or with glazes and
  pigments (part 10; *contested*). Arsenical copper, and then tin bronze, were found by trying ores and mixtures. Iron came from
  furnaces run hot enough, with ore that happened to suit (part 03). Each step was a small change to a known process.
- **Recipes without reasons.** The earliest written craft knowledge reads like a list of steps wrapped in ritual. The Assyrian glass
  texts from Ashurbanipal's library (seventh century BC) give batches, furnaces and rites side by side, and the monk Theophilus's
  *De diversis artibus* (about 1120) covers glass, pigments and metalwork (*memory*). Vannoccio
  Biringuccio's *Pirotechnia* (1540) and Georgius Agricola's *De re metallica* (1556) put mining, smelting, assay and glass into print
  as methods, with little theory (*memory*).

### 1.2 What trial and error looked like

Trial before science was neither blind nor random. It had four features that matter for any reading of discovery:

1. **A small number of settings per technique.** A maltster could vary the days in steep, the days on the floor and the heat of the
   kiln. A tanner could vary the strength of the bark liquor and the months in the pit, a smelter the ratio of charcoal to ore and
   the rate of the bellows, a potter the fuel and the hours of firing and the air let in. A haymaker chose the day to cut and how long
   to leave the swath, and a cheesemaker the rennet, the heat and the salt. Each is a grid of perhaps three to five choices, each with
   a few values.
2. **Delayed and noisy feedback.** A sowing date shows its result months later, mixed with the weather. A tanning shows its result a
   year later. One good season proves little, so practices were tested over many years and many households.
3. **Selection by copying the successful.** Henrich and Boyd's models of cultural learning, and the ethnography they draw on, find
   that people copy the prestigious and successful, and copy the majority when unsure (*rough*). Good practice spreads without anyone
   knowing why it is good, and so does bad practice tied to a successful person.
4. **Local and contingent results.** Most of what trial found was true of one place: which field drains, where the good clay lies,
   when the frost leaves the valley, which oak stand gives the best bark, when the salmon run, which spring is safe. These were the
   largest part of a farmer's or craftsman's knowledge, and they did not transfer.

Michael Polanyi's "we can know more than we can tell" (1966) names what made this knowledge hard to move: **tacit knowledge**, held
in the hand and the eye (*memory*). Harry Collins's study of the TEA laser in the 1970s showed it lasted into modern science.
Laboratories that had only the published papers could not build a working laser. Those that sent someone to work beside a
successful builder could (*memory*). That is apprenticeship's logic, two thousand years on.

### 1.3 The collective brain and the Tasmanian loss

- **The argument.** Henrich (2004) models a skill whose copies are a little worse than the original on average, with occasional
  copies that are better. In a large, connected population, learners copy the best practitioner and the occasional improvement is
  kept, so the skill climbs. In a small or isolated one there are too few chances for improvement, the average loss wins, and complex
  skills decay while simple ones survive (*checked*, as summarised).
- **The case.** Rising seas cut Tasmania off from the mainland roughly 10,000 to 12,000 years ago (*rough*). At contact the Tasmanian
  toolkit had about **24 items** (*checked*): one-piece spears, clubs, stone scrapers, and no hafted tools, no bone tools, no
  boomerangs and, by the late Holocene, no fish taken, although bone points and fishing appear earlier in the island's archaeology
  (*contested*). Henrich reads this as loss through small population, about 4,000 people in a few dozen bands (*rough, memory*).
- **Other cases.** The Polar Inuit of north-west Greenland are said to have lost the kayak, the bow and the leister after an
  epidemic in the 1820s killed the old people who made them. They regained these from Baffin Island migrants in the 1860s (*memory;
  contested*).
- **The cross-section.** Michelle Kline and Robert Boyd (2010) compared marine foraging toolkits in ten Oceanic societies at contact.
  Both the number of tools and their average complexity rose with population. Contact between islands mattered more for small
  populations, but only marginally (p about 0.05 to 0.08), and environmental controls showed nothing (*checked*). Michael Kremer
  (1993) made the same argument for the whole world: if new ideas come in proportion to people, then technology and population grow
  together, and the rank of the separate landmasses at 1500 (Eurasia and Africa, then the Americas, then Australia, then Tasmania and
  Flinders Island) follows their size (*memory*).
- **The critique.** Vaesen, Collard, Cosgrove and Roebroeks (2016, *PNAS*) found that population does not predict toolkit complexity
  in wider samples of foragers, and that the Tasmanian loss is doubtful in detail. Henrich and colleagues replied that the argument is
  about connected populations sharing skill, not census size (*checked* that the dispute exists). Treat the effect as real but small
  and conditional.

**What it means for this map.** Depth of know-how is held by numbers and connection. It is the most fragile thing an economy has, and
it is lost faster than it is built (02zzj README, finding 9; part 06's collapse table cites the Tasmanian case). Part 12's finding,
that patents and inventions scale faster than city size, is the modern face of the same mechanism.

### 1.4 Transfer between peoples

Techniques moved with people more than with goods. Sericulture reached Byzantium about 552 when monks carried silkworm eggs from
Central Asia, by Procopius's account (*memory*). Papermaking spread from China across the Islamic world (part 11; `islamic_paper_mill`)
and reached Italy by the thirteenth century (`fabriano_paper`). Indian cotton printing was copied in Europe after 1700 (part 07).
Gunpowder reached Europe as a recipe without the long Chinese practice behind it (part 01). Where only the product travelled, as with
Chinese porcelain, the technique took centuries to follow.

## 2. Holding and passing on know-how: households, secrecy, apprenticeship and guilds

### 2.1 Households, lineages and castes

Most know-how passed from parent to child. Hereditary crafts were the rule in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, and in India the
*jati* system tied crafts to endogamous groups for two thousand years. Even in medieval Europe, many masters were masters' sons (part
05 §1.7). The household held know-how as it held land, and it passed both to heirs. This made know-how cheap to transmit (children
watched for years) and narrow (a craft went where the family went).

### 2.2 Secrecy as property

Where a technique could be kept from others, its holders could charge a rent for it:

| Secret | Held by | How it was kept | How it got out |
|---|---|---|---|
| silk (sericulture and reeling) | China | state prohibition on exporting eggs | carried to Byzantium about 552 (*memory*); in Italy by the twelfth to thirteenth centuries |
| Greek fire | the Byzantine state, from about 672 | knowledge split among a few families and officials | lost; its composition is still argued (*memory*) |
| Venetian glass | Murano's furnaces, moved there in 1291 (*memory*) | laws from the 1290s forbade glassmakers to emigrate, on pain of punishment to their families (*memory, unchecked*) | glassmakers fled to France, England and the Netherlands in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; *façon de Venise* glass made across Europe |
| Chinese porcelain | Jingdezhen (part 11) | the kaolin and petuntse body and the firing | Johann Böttger and Ehrenfried von Tschirnhaus found a hard-paste body at Dresden about 1708, and the Meissen works opened in 1710 under oath-bound "arcanists" (*memory*), who then defected to Vienna and elsewhere |
| the obstetric forceps | the Chamberlen family in England, from about 1600 | the instrument kept in a box and used under a sheet | kept for over a century and published in the 1730s (*memory*): a medical secret held as family property |
| Italian silk throwing | Piedmont, whose state forbade export of the machines | | John Lombe worked in an Italian mill and drew the machines (1716 to 1717). The Lombes' Derby silk mill (about 1721) is often called England's first factory, and Thomas Lombe's patent of 1718 protected it (*memory*) |
| Arkwright's water frame | Britain, which forbade the emigration of artisans until 1825 and machinery exports until 1843 | | Samuel Slater carried it to Rhode Island in his head in 1789 (part 07 §4.4) |

Secrecy cost something. A secret held by a few could die with them, which is Henrich's mechanism again: Greek fire and the
recipes for Damascus (wootz) blades were lost (*contested* for wootz). Secrecy also slowed improvement, because nobody outside could
add to the method. Petra Moser found that most inventions shown at the Crystal Palace in 1851 were protected by secrecy rather than
by patent (part 07 §7.3). Secrecy was the normal state of know-how, and patents were the exception.

### 2.3 Apprenticeship

Apprenticeship was the main route by which hard know-how moved between households (part 05 §1.7 covers its terms and the
completion rates in London). What it gave the knowledge economy:

- **Years beside a master,** the only way to pass on tacit skill. The master's cost came first and the apprentice's useful labour
  last (Wallis), so the contract had to hold for years, which needed guilds, towns or courts behind it.
- **Mobility:** journeymen travelled between masters and towns (the German *Wanderjahre*), carrying methods with them. S. R. Epstein
  argued that this movement was the main channel of technical diffusion in pre-industrial Europe (*contested*; Ogilvie disagrees on
  the guilds' role).
- **A test:** the masterpiece showed the skill before the right to practise was granted. It was an early form of certification, and
  the professions' licences are its heirs (part 05 §1.11).

### 2.4 Guild knowledge and the written craft

Guilds held knowledge collectively. They set standards, searched workshops, sealed approved goods, limited apprentices per master
and kept outsiders out (part 05 §1.7). Whether this spread skill (Epstein) or protected rents and blocked new methods (Ogilvie) is
the central quarrel. For the history of knowledge, three points stand:

1. **The guild made know-how a club good:** shared among members, closed to others.
2. **Writing came late and from outside the guild.** The books of secrets (the *Secreti* of "Alessio Piemontese", 1555, ran to
   dozens of editions; *memory*), Biringuccio and Agricola, and then Diderot and d'Alembert's *Encyclopédie* (1751 to 1772, with
   eleven volumes of plates of trades; *memory*) published what guilds held close. The Royal Society's project for a "history of
   trades" in the 1660s mostly failed because craftsmen would not tell (*memory*).
3. **Print lowered the cost of access** (Mokyr's term; §5). A method in a book reached any literate reader, which is why the
   encyclopaedias and their plates belong to the Industrial Enlightenment.

### 2.5 What this made, economically

Each way of holding know-how traded a rent against reach. The household passed skill cheaply but narrowly. The secret earned a
monopoly and risked loss on a death. Apprenticeship moved tacit skill at the price of years and a contract that had to be enforced.
The guild set standards and closed the shop. Print lowered access costs and ended the holder's rent. The patent (§6.6) published a
method in exchange for a temporary monopoly. All six recur today: family firms, trade secrets, residencies and PhDs, professional
licences, open publication, patents.

## 3. Instruments as extended senses, and their supply chains

An instrument turns a property that bodies judge badly (a weight to a grain, the hour at night, the gold in a coin, the angle of a
star, the heat of a mash) into a sign a person can read. Every instrument needed materials, a maker's skill and a standard to read
against, so each rode on earlier trades. Each made something new discoverable. And each made new trades and new frauds: short
weight, clipped coin, false measures (weights and measures as regulation are part 18's).

### 3.2 Counting and weighing: the oldest instruments

The tally and the balance are older than writing, and both are instruments of trust as much as of measurement. A split tally had two
halves that had to match, so neither party could alter it (part 04 §5.2). The English Exchequer used tallies as receipts from the
twelfth century until 1826. Burning the old stock in 1834 set fire to the Houses of Parliament (*memory*). The balance needed a
standard weight, and so an authority to keep it. The Indus chert weights, consistent across sites hundreds of kilometres apart in a
binary series around a 13.7 g unit (part 10), show a standard held without, as far as anyone can tell, a king. The steelyard, a Roman beam with one sliding weight (*memory*), did for
bulk goods at markets what the equal-arm balance did for silver. By the sixteenth century, Agricola's assay balances were kept in
glass cases against draughts (*memory*), and Lavoisier's chemistry of 1789 rested on balances made to measure fractions of a grain
(*memory*). Conservation of mass was found with a better scale.

### 3.3 Reading the year

Farming needs the date more than the hour. Before written calendars, people read the year from the sky and the land: the solstice
light entering Newgrange's passage about 3200 BC (*memory*), the heliacal rising of Sirius before the Nile flood, Hesiod's
instruction to reap when the Pleiades rise and plough when they set (*memory*), and the leafing of particular trees. The gnomon
measured the sun's noon shadow, whose length through the year gives the solstices. Guo Shoujing's 40-foot gnomon at Dengfeng
(1276 to 1279) measured the tropical year to within seconds of the modern value (*memory*). Calendars kept by temples and states set
the dates for planting, rents, fairs and taxes (part 05's quarter days). They were among the first instruments of state power.

What matters for the border is the difference between a **universal fact** (the solstice), which any reader of the sky can find, and
a **local fact** (the last frost in this valley, the week the barley is safe to sow on this soil), which only years of watching one
place give. Most farming calendars are the second kind, kept as lore and proverb.

### 3.4 Assay: touchstone and cupel

Coinage rests on assay (part 04). The **touchstone** is a fine black stone on which a gold object leaves a streak. The streak is
compared with streaks from touch-needles of known fineness, sometimes with acid to dissolve the base metal. The method is quick,
cheap and nearly non-destructive, and it was good to a few parts in a hundred in practised hands (*rough*). The **cupel** is a
shallow dish of bone ash. Lead and a weighed sample are heated on it, the lead oxidises and soaks into the cupel taking the base
metals with it, and the silver or gold bead left is weighed (part 03's `cupellation`, from about 2500 BC). With an assay balance,
fire assay was accurate to a part in a thousand or better (*rough*). It let mints be audited (England's Trial of the Pyx, recorded from 1282, *memory*), lets ores be
valued before they are bought, and let mining companies raise money on assays. **Parting** gold from silver with aqua fortis (nitric
acid, from the still) joined assay to the chemist's glassware by about 1300 (*memory*).

### 3.5 Clocks: from the hours of prayer to the factory day

- **Water clocks** served temples and courts for three thousand years. Su Song's tower at Kaifeng (finished 1092) drove an armillary
  sphere and a celestial globe through a water-wheel escapement (*memory*).
- **The mechanical clock** appeared in Europe in the late thirteenth century, regulated by a verge and foliot. Its first users were
  monasteries ringing the canonical hours and cathedrals; then towns put clocks in towers from the mid-fourteenth century (*memory*).
  David Landes (*Revolution in Time*, 1983) argued that public clock time changed work: wages by the hour, the bell of the cloth
  towns. E. P. Thompson (1967) traced the long tightening of time discipline into the factory (*memory*).
- **Clockmaking as a school of machinery.** Clockmakers made wheels, pinions and precise iron and brass parts. Arkwright hired
  clockmakers to build the water frame (part 07), and the early millwrights and machine builders came from clock and instrument
  trades. Time-keeping and machine-making shared their skills.
- **Accuracy:** a verge-and-foliot clock might drift by many minutes a day. Huygens's pendulum (1656) brought errors under a minute a
  day, and later under ten seconds; the anchor escapement (about 1670) and the balance spring (1675) followed (*checked* for
  Huygens's figures; *memory* for the rest).
- **Longitude.** Parliament's Longitude Act (1714) offered up to £20,000 for a method good to half a degree (*checked*). John Harrison,
  a carpenter-clockmaker, built four timekeepers over thirty years. H4 lost about 5 seconds in 81 days on the voyage to Jamaica in
  1761 to 1762 (*checked*). The Board demanded more trials, and Harrison received about £8,750 after petitioning Parliament in 1773
  (*checked*), never the full prize. The rival method, lunar distances, needed precise sextants and printed tables (Maskelyne's
  *Nautical Almanac*, 1767, *memory*). Both methods were in use until chronometers became cheap in the nineteenth century.

### 3.6 Finding the way: compass, astrolabe, sextant, chart

Navigation shows instruments joining a chain with charts and records. Shen Kuo described the magnetic needle in 1088, Chinese ships used it by about 1100, and Alexander Neckam wrote of it in Europe about 1190 (*memory*). The compass needed a lodestone and good steel to magnetise.
The astrolabe and quadrant needed brass, engraving and accurately divided scales. Portolan charts were drawn from compass bearings
and pilots' records, and the first surviving one, the Carta Pisana, dates from about 1275 (*memory*). Pilots' rutters wrote down
the local facts: soundings, landmarks, tides. Ramsden's dividing engine (1775) divided sextant scales mechanically and accurately,
and the Board of Longitude paid him to publish its design (*memory*), so precise instruments could be made in quantity. Polynesian
navigators show the alternative: a star compass, swell patterns and bird flights held in trained memory, with Marshall Islands stick
charts as teaching aids rather than instruments carried at sea (*memory*). That is know-how without instruments, held by an
apprenticeship of years.

### 3.7 Glass, spectacles and the lens trade

The optical chain begins with glass clear enough to see through, which took two thousand years from glass beads and vessels (part
01; `glass`):

1. **Forest glass and Venetian crystal.** Northern forest glass was green from iron and made with potash from wood ash (part 01).
   Venice's furnaces used purer soda ash from Levantine plants and selected sand. Angelo Barovier's *cristallo*, about 1450, was
   nearly colourless (*memory*). Rock crystal ground in Venice and elsewhere was the first lens material.
2. **Spectacles.** In a sermon in Florence on 23 February 1306, the Dominican Giordano da Pisa said the art of making spectacles had
   been found less than twenty years before, and that he had met the man who first made them. Historians put the invention at about
   1286 in Pisa or Tuscany; the earliest evidence of organised manufacture is in Venice in 1300 to 1301 (*checked*). Convex lenses
   for reading came first, and concave lenses for short sight by the fifteenth century (*memory*). Landes argued that spectacles
   roughly doubled the working life of scribes and fine craftsmen, whose close sight fails in their forties (*contested*).
3. **The trade.** By the sixteenth century spectacles were cheap goods made in Venice, Nuremberg and the Low Countries and sold by
   pedlars (*memory*). A trade of lens grinders and spectacle makers had settled in the Dutch towns, and it gave rise to the
   telescope.
4. **Lead crystal.** George Ravenscroft's lead glass in London (patented 1674, *memory*), born partly of coal-fired furnaces (part
   01's `coal_glass_furnace`), gave a dense, brilliant glass. Later its dispersion mattered for achromatic lenses: Dollond's patent of
   1758 paired crown and flint glass to cancel colour fringes (*memory*).

### 3.8 The telescope and microscope from the spectacle trade

On 2 October 1608 Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker of Middelburg, petitioned the States-General for a thirty-year patent on a
"looker". Within days Jacob Metius of Alkmaar claimed the same, and the patent was refused, by most accounts because the device was
too easy to copy. The States-General paid Lipperhey 300 guilders for an instrument instead (*checked*). Galileo heard of it and
built his own in 1609, grinding his own lenses. He reached about 20 magnifications and published the moons of Jupiter in *Sidereus
Nuncius* in March 1610 (*memory*). The telescope's economic uses came at once (at sea, in war, and for the merchant watching for a
ship), and its scientific ones changed astronomy. Jupiter's moons became a clock in the sky. At sea the ship's motion defeated the method; on
land Cassini's tables (1668) let the French survey fix longitudes (*memory*).

The **microscope** came out of the same trade in the 1620s. Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* (1665) showed cells in cork and the
structure of insects. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Delft draper who ground tiny single lenses to inspect cloth, reached perhaps 270
magnifications and reported "animalcules" to the Royal Society in 1676 (*memory*). He kept his lens-making method secret. Here the
instrument came long before the theory: microbes were seen in the 1670s and tied to disease only in the 1860s to 1880s (part 08's
`germ_theory`). An instrument opens a field. It does not by itself give the reading.

**Optical glass** became an industry of its own. Pierre-Louis Guinand found how to stir glass to remove striae (about 1800).
Joseph von Fraunhofer, at Benediktbeuern from 1806, made the best lenses of his day and found the dark lines of the solar spectrum
(1814). Otto Schott, Ernst Abbe and Carl Zeiss founded the Jena glassworks in 1884, with new glass compositions designed for optics
(*memory*). The chain still runs: part 13 records Carl Zeiss as the sole maker of the optics in ASML's EUV lithography
machines. The chokepoint of the twenty-first-century chip passes through a lens maker descended from the spectacle trade.

### 3.9 Mercury, the barometer and the thermometer

The weather glass and the thermometer needed mercury, and mercury came from the silver chain. Almadén in Spain, Idrija in
Slovenia (from about 1490) and Huancavelica in Peru (from 1564) supplied the amalgamation of silver at Potosí and in Mexico (parts 03
and 11; `huancavelica_mercury`, `crown_mercury`, `patio_process`). Instruments took a tiny share of it.

- **The barometer** (Torricelli, 1643) showed that the atmosphere has weight and that a vacuum can exist. Pascal's brother-in-law
  carried one up the Puy de Dôme in 1648, and it read lower at the top. Otto von Guericke's air pump and his Magdeburg hemispheres
  (1650s) and Boyle and Hooke's pump (1659) made a vacuum a laboratory object (*memory*). Newcomen's engine (1712; part 07) worked by
  atmospheric pressure on a piston over condensed steam, and Mokyr cites it as a technique whose epistemic base was this new
  knowledge (§5; *contested* as to how far Newcomen relied on it).
- **The thermometer** took a century to become a measuring instrument rather than an indicator. It needed sealed tubes of even bore,
  a liquid that expands evenly (mercury, by Fahrenheit, 1714), and fixed points for a shared scale (Fahrenheit 1724, Celsius 1742)
  (*memory*). Brewers were among its first industrial users: Michael Combrune's *Theory and Practice of Brewing* (1762) made the
  thermometer the mash-tun's instrument, and John Richardson's saccharometer (1784) let brewers buy malt by the extract it gave
  (*memory*). At Glasgow, Watt was the
  university's instrument maker beside Joseph Black, who found latent heat (part 07; *contested* how much the condenser owed to Black).

### 3.10 Stills, glassware and the chemist's bench

The still is the instrument that most directly turned craft into chemistry. Alexandrian stills are described by Zosimos (about 300
AD). Islamic chemists (the Jabir corpus, al-Razi) refined the alembic and its glassware, and the perfume trade distilled rosewater on
a commercial scale (*memory*). Distilling wine into *aqua vitae* is recorded at Salerno in the twelfth century, and spirits became a
medical then a drinking trade (*memory*). Mineral acids from the still (aqua fortis, oil of vitriol) served assay, dyeing and
metalwork; part 07's lead-chamber sulphuric acid (1746) was their industrial form. Laboratory glass then had its own chain. Bohemian
potash glass was used for chemical ware. Schott's borosilicate glass (Jena, 1880s to 1893) resisted heat and acids, and Corning's
Pyrex (1915) took it to American kitchens and laboratories (*memory*). The research laboratory of §6.7 is built of this glassware.

### 3.11 Precision and standards: measurement becomes an industry

Part 07 covers the machine-tool line: Wilkinson's boring mill (1774) for Watt's cylinders, Maudslay's screw-cutting lathe and
micrometer, Whitworth's standard threads (1841), the American system of gauges and interchangeable parts. The knowledge side of that
line:

- **Precision makes precision.** A good screw lets one divide a scale. A divided scale lets one make a better screw. Ramsden's
  dividing engine (1775) and Maudslay's lathe are steps on that ladder, and the engineers saw it so: Whitworth's measuring machine of
  the 1850s claimed a millionth of an inch (*memory*).
- **Gauges replaced fitting.** Go and no-go gauges, used at the US federal armouries from the 1820s, made interchangeable parts
  checkable by an unskilled hand (part 07). Carl Edvard Johansson's gauge blocks (1896) gave every workshop a set of standard lengths
  (*memory*).
- **Standards became institutions.** The metric system (the metre of 1799), the Metre Convention (1875) and its International Bureau,
  Germany's Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt (1887, founded with Siemens's backing), Britain's National Physical Laboratory (1900)
  and the US National Bureau of Standards (1901) made units a public good kept by states (*memory*). Electrical units in particular
  needed them before a grid could be sold by the kilowatt-hour (part 08).

### 3.12 Three patterns

**Instruments came from luxury and craft trades**: brass from the founders, glass from
Murano, lenses from spectacle makers, wheels from clockmakers, mercury from the silver mines. **The instrument came before the
theory**, often by generations (microbes, the vacuum, heat). And **instruments made new trades of their own**: the London instrument
makers of the eighteenth century were a skilled industry exporting across Europe (*rough*), and their workshops trained the
mechanics of the industrial revolution.

## 4. Records and counting as instruments

### 4.1 Records kept for one purpose, read for another

Most records were made to collect something: a tax, a rent, a tithe, a burial fee. The discoveries came when someone read a long run
of them for a pattern. Writing began as bookkeeping (part 10: tokens, bullae, numerical tablets), and its first scientific use was
the same. Babylonian scribes kept **astronomical diaries** of the sky, the weather, river levels and market prices night by night
from about 652 BC to the first century BC (*memory*). It is the longest run of observations from antiquity, and the period
relations of Babylonian astronomy were found in it.

### 4.2 Estate and parish records

- **Domesday** (1086) counted land, ploughs, mills and people for a king's tax (part 05).
- **Manorial accounts.** The Bishop of Winchester's pipe rolls survive from 1208 (*memory*). With the accounts of other great
  estates they give the seed, yield, prices and wages from which parts 02 and 05 take medieval yield ratios. The reeve's annual
  account to the lord's auditors was a control instrument, and its long run is a record of experiment by accident.
- **Husbandry treatises.** Walter of Henley's *Husbandry* (about 1280) and its fellows advised on oxen against horses, seed rates and
  checking reeves (*memory*). They are the first farm management books, and they lean on the accounts.
- **Parish registers** were ordered in England in 1538 (*memory*). They are the source of English historical demography (part 05's
  Wrigley and Schofield).

### 4.3 Graunt, the bills of mortality and political arithmetic

London's weekly **bills of mortality** were compiled by the parish clerks. Graunt says they began in 1592, a year of plague, and
were resumed in 1603. They were printed every Thursday, with a yearly summary before Christmas (*checked*). John Graunt, a haberdasher,
read seventy years of them and published *Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality* (1662). He found the
regularities of a population: more boys born than girls, deaths by season and cause, plague years, and the city's dependence on
migrants. He estimated London's people at about **384,000**, against contemporary guesses of two million (*checked*). His friend
William Petty named the method **political arithmetic**. Edmond Halley built the first life table from Breslau's records in 1693,
and used it to price annuities (*memory*). That line runs into actuarial science and life insurance (part 05; `life_insurance`):
the Equitable Life (1762) priced policies by age from such tables (*memory*).

### 4.4 Snow's map and the grand experiment

In 1854 John Snow mapped cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump in Soho and had its handle removed. The well was later found
near an old cesspit (*checked*). His stronger evidence was a natural experiment in south London. The Southwark and Vauxhall company
drew its water from the Thames below the sewage outfalls, and the Lambeth company had moved its intake upstream. Both supplied rich
and poor houses in the same streets. Houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall had **315 cholera deaths per 10,000 houses**, Lambeth's
**37**, and the rest of London 59 (*checked*). Snow's case rested on records kept by others: the General Register Office's
death returns, which William Farr organised from 1839 (*memory*). It fed the long chain from water to sewers to filtration (part 08's
`germ_theory`, `water_filtration`, `bazalgette_sewers`). The cholera germ itself waited for Koch's microscope in 1883.

### 4.5 Trials

- **Lind's scurvy trial** (1747) gave twelve sailors with scurvy six different treatments in pairs. The two given oranges and lemons
  recovered (*memory*). The navy issued lemon juice generally only in 1795.
- **Rothamsted.** John Bennet Lawes, who patented superphosphate in 1842 (part 02; `superphosphate`), laid out field experiments on
  his estate at Rothamsted with the chemist Joseph Henry Gilbert from 1843. **Broadbalk**, winter wheat with different manures in
  long strips, is the oldest continuing field experiment in the world (*checked*). The strips settled the dispute with Liebig over
  nitrogen: wheat needed nitrogen from manure or salts, not only minerals (*memory*). The company's fertiliser sales and the
  experiment's evidence grew together.
- **Statistics from industry.** William Sealy Gosset, a brewer at Guinness in Dublin, published the *t*-test as "Student" in 1908 to
  judge small samples of barley and hops (*memory*). R. A. Fisher was hired at Rothamsted in **1919** to make sense of its
  seventy-six years of records. He stayed to build the theory of experimental design, with replication, blocking and the analysis of
  variance (*checked*). Fisher's randomisation came from the same work (*memory*). The randomised clinical trial (the Medical Research
  Council's streptomycin trial, 1948, *memory*) carried it into medicine, and part 13's drug pipeline depends on it
  (`pharma_rnd_trials`).
- **Experiment stations.** The first state-funded agricultural experiment station opened at Möckern in Saxony in 1852 (*memory*).
  The United States tied stations to land-grant colleges (Morrill Act 1862, Hatch Act 1887, *memory*), which is how hybrid maize
  (part 02) came out of public research.

### 4.6 Breeding: discovery by selection and pedigree

Every domestic plant and animal was bred by selection long before anyone kept records (part 10). What changed in the eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries was the record:

- **Robert Bakewell** of Dishley, Leicestershire, from the 1760s, bred sheep and cattle by close breeding and by letting rams for the
  season to other farmers, which let him see the rams' offspring and judge the sires (*memory*). His New Leicester sheep fattened
  fast, and the method was the point.
- **Stud and herd books** made pedigree a recorded good: the *General Stud Book* for thoroughbreds (1791) and Coates's *Herd Book* for
  Shorthorns (1822) (*memory*). A beast with a recorded line fetched more. The book was the instrument.
- **Progeny testing in plants.** Louis de Vilmorin, in the 1850s, selected sugar beet by the sugar content of each mother's offspring,
  raising beet sugar substantially (*memory, rough*: commonly from about 6 to 7% to well over 12%). That chain fed the European beet
  sugar industry that competed with cane (part 11).
- **Mendel** (1866), rediscovered in 1900, gave breeders a theory. Rowland Biffen bred rust-resistant wheat at Cambridge from 1905
  (*memory*), hybrid maize came in the 1920s and 1930s, the Green Revolution's dwarf wheats in the 1960s (part 02), and broiler
  genetics firms later (part 13).

**The general point:** a record kept over many years and many cases turns slow, noisy feedback into evidence. That is exactly what
trial and error (§1.2) lacked.

## 5. Mokyr: what is known, how to do it, and what it costs to find out

Joel Mokyr (*The Gifts of Athena*, 2002; *The Enlightened Economy*, 2009; *A Culture of Growth*, 2016) splits useful knowledge in
two (*memory* for the terms; the frame is standard):

| | Propositional knowledge (Ω, "what") | Prescriptive knowledge (λ, "how") |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | statements about nature: regularities, properties, laws, catalogues, maps, measurements | techniques: recipes, instructions, methods |
| **Where it lives** | books, journals, minds, records, collections | hands, workshops, manuals, routines |
| **Example** | air has weight; nitrogen limits wheat; chlorine bleaches | the Newcomen engine; superphosphate; Tennant's bleaching powder |

- **The epistemic base.** Every technique rests on some propositional knowledge, wide or narrow. A technique with a narrow base,
  "it works and nobody knows why", can be improved only by trial around the known method. It tends to plateau and can be lost. A wide
  base lets people improve it in a directed way, recombine it with others, and transfer it to new uses. Medieval and Song Chinese
  techniques were often brilliant but rested on narrow bases. Mokyr's argument is that sustained growth needs the base to widen
  along with the techniques (part 06's table of theories).
- **Access costs.** What matters is not only what is known somewhere, but how cheap it is for a practitioner to find. Mokyr lists
  the things that lowered access costs in the eighteenth century: print and cheap books, encyclopaedias and their plates, scientific
  societies and their journals, public lectures, the coffee houses (part 12), mechanics' institutes, correspondence networks, and a
  common language of measurement.
- **Tightness.** Knowledge is accepted when it can be checked: by experiment, demonstration or measurement. Instruments and
  repeatable experiments made claims tight and therefore usable by people who did not trust the author.
- **The Industrial Enlightenment** (roughly 1700 to 1850) was, in this account, the joining of the Baconian programme (knowledge for
  use) with the craft world. Societies such as the Lunar Society, the Society of Arts and the Manchester Literary and Philosophical
  Society brought natural philosophers, manufacturers and engineers together (part 07 §7.3; `useful_knowledge_societies`). Examples
  run both ways: Berthollet's chlorine to Tennant's bleaching powder (science to technique), and the steam engine to thermodynamics
  (technique to science: Sadi Carnot, 1824).
- **Critiques.** Robert Allen: incentives (dear labour, cheap coal) chose which techniques paid, and science mattered little before
  1850 (part 07). Many eighteenth-century inventions (Cort's puddling, the mule) owed little to science. Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda
  answer that Britain's advantage lay in a broad stock of skilled mechanics, which is tacit knowledge again (part 07 §4.4). Most
  historians now read the scientific base as decisive after about 1850 (chemistry, electricity) and contributory before (*contested*).

**The Needham question** (why Song China, with printing, the compass, gunpowder and coal-fired iron, had no scientific
revolution) has many answers and none settled (*contested*). For this map the useful point is Mokyr's: techniques can flourish for
centuries on a narrow base, but they plateau.

**How Zach's line reads in Mokyr's terms.** Prescriptive knowledge (techniques, recipes, the dependencies between them) is what the
engine holds as data, gates by level, and lets agents learn by teaching, watching and trying. Propositional knowledge (what is so,
why, and where) is what agents hold in their own memories and records. The models already carry most of the world's universal
propositional knowledge. What they cannot know is the local, contingent kind (§1.2, §3.3).

## 6. The institutions of knowledge-making

### 6.1 Patronage

| Patron | Where and when | What it funded | Why it paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| the Ptolemies | the Museum and Library at Alexandria, from about 280 BC (*memory*) | scholars on stipends; Euclid, Eratosthenes, Ctesibius, Hero | prestige; the court's engineers and astronomers |
| the Abbasid caliphs | the House of Wisdom, Baghdad, ninth century (*memory*) | translation of Greek, Persian and Indian works; astronomy | administration, the calendar and prayer times, prestige |
| Hulagu, with a waqf | Maragha observatory, 1259 (*memory*) | Nasir al-Din al-Tusi's instruments and tables | astrology for the court; the endowment kept it after him |
| Frederick II of Denmark | Tycho Brahe's Uraniborg on Hven, from 1576 (*memory*) | the best naked-eye instruments ever made; decades of observations | prestige; a fief to Tycho; reportedly a sizeable share of crown income (*contested*) |
| the Medici | Galileo as court mathematician, 1610 (*memory*) | his work, after he named Jupiter's moons the "Medicean stars" | prestige exchanged for a dedication |

Patronage science was a courtly
gift economy, discoveries given and repaid with position (Biagioli, *memory*). It paid for instruments and observers, not craft.

### 6.2 Temples and monasteries

Temples were the first record-keepers and calendar-keepers (part 10; `temple_palace_economy`). Monasteries kept literacy alive in the
early medieval West, copied books, and ran estates large enough to improve them. The Cistercians (from 1098) ran granges with lay
brothers, used water power widely, and had ironworks (*memory*). The canonical hours may have driven the first mechanical clocks
(§3.5). Buddhist monasteries in China and Korea printed scripture: the Diamond Sutra of 868 is the oldest dated printed book
(*memory*). Part 16 covers religious houses as economies; here the point is that they were the longest-lived holders of literacy,
records and some craft knowledge in societies with no other institution that outlived its members.

### 6.3 Universities

The medieval university (Bologna, traditionally 1088; Paris and Oxford by about 1200; perhaps 60 to 80 in Europe by 1500, *rough,
memory*) taught law, medicine, theology and the arts. It trained the clerks, lawyers and physicians of church and state and created a
pool of the literate. It did not teach crafts, and most technical change happened outside it. That changed in the nineteenth century.
Justus von Liebig's teaching laboratory at Giessen (from the mid-1820s) trained chemists by the hundred (*memory*). Wilhelm von
Humboldt's University of Berlin (1810) joined research to teaching (*memory*). German universities and technical colleges supplied
the PhD chemists of the dye industry (part 08 §3.1). In the United States, land-grant colleges (1862) and Johns Hopkins (1876)
followed (*memory*).

### 6.4 Academies, societies and journals

- **Academies:** the Accademia dei Lincei (Rome, 1603), the Accademia del Cimento (Florence, 1657), the **Royal Society** (London,
  1660, chartered 1662) and the **Académie des sciences** (Paris, 1666) (*memory*). The French academy paid salaried members and
  advised the state on inventions. The Royal Society was a private club of gentlemen and depended on members' subscriptions.
- **Journals:** the *Journal des sçavans* (January 1665) and the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* (March 1665) made the
  printed report, with priority by date, the unit of scientific credit (*memory*). Henry Oldenburg's correspondence network was the
  journal's raw material. The "Republic of Letters" was a postal network (part 04's `postal_service`).
- **Societies for useful knowledge:** the Society of Arts (London, 1754) paid premiums for improvements in agriculture,
  manufactures and the colonies. The Lunar Society (Birmingham, about 1765 to 1800) joined Boulton, Watt, Wedgwood, Priestley and
  Darwin. The Royal Institution (1799) gave public lectures and Davy's and Faraday's laboratory. Mechanics' institutes spread in the
  1820s, and the British Association met from 1831 (*memory*). These are the access-cost reducers of §5.

### 6.5 Prizes

Prizes pay for a solution to a stated problem. They suit cases where the target is known and the route is not:

| Prize | Date | Target | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longitude Act | 1714 | longitude at sea to half a degree, up to £20,000 | Harrison's chronometers and lunar tables; the Board paid many smaller rewards; Harrison got about £8,750 by petition in 1773 (*checked*) |
| Académie des sciences | 1775 (*memory*) | soda from salt | Nicolas Leblanc's process (patent 1791; part 01, `leblanc_process`); he never received the prize, and his works were seized in the Revolution |
| Napoleon's government | 1810 (*memory*) | preserving food for the army; 12,000 francs to Nicolas Appert | bottling, then the tin can (part 02, `canning`) |
| Ansari X Prize | 2004 (*memory*) | a private crewed suborbital flight twice in two weeks; $10 million | SpaceShipOne |

Prizes work for a goal everyone can recognise. Patents work for inventions nobody can name in advance. The two co-existed from the
eighteenth century, and advance market commitments for vaccines are the modern prize.

### 6.6 Patents

Parts 05 (§2.6) and 07 (§7.3) cover the law and the cost: Venice's statute of 1474 (`intellectual_property`), England's Statute of
Monopolies (1624; `patent_system`), the high English fees until 1852, the German Patent Act of 1877, and Moser's finding that most
inventions were not patented. What this part adds:

- **The patent trades a monopoly for disclosure.** The specification publishes the method, and it can be read once the term ends. This
  lowers access costs (§5), slowly, in return for a rent.
- **Patents fail for easily copied devices.** Lipperhey's telescope is the classic refusal (§3.8).
- **The United States built a cheap, examined patent system** (Patent Acts of 1790 and 1836, with examination from 1836; *memory*).
  Naomi Lamoreaux and Kenneth Sokoloff found an active market in patent rights in the nineteenth century, with patent agents and
  lawyers matching inventors to firms. Patents became tradeable goods, and invention a specialised trade (*memory*). The Paris
  Convention (1883) began international protection (*memory*).
- **Collective invention without patents.** The Cornish engine reporters after Watt's patent lapsed (part 07 §1.3; Nuvolari), and
  Robert Allen's case of the Cleveland blast furnaces in the 1850s to 1870s, where ironmasters shared design data and furnace heights
  and temperatures rose fast (*memory*), show the other road. The same road runs through open-source software.

### 6.7 The industrial research laboratory

Part 08 §3.1 gives the core: the German dye firms' laboratories turned invention into a staffed, paid search, and GE (1900), DuPont
(1903) and Bell (1925) followed. Additions:

- **Edison's Menlo Park** (1876) was an independent "invention factory": machinists, glassblowers, chemists and a library, aiming, in
  Edison's words, at "a minor invention every ten days and a big thing every six months or so" (*memory*). The electric lighting
  system came out of it (part 08). Edison sold invention as a service before firms made it a department.
- **Brewing again.** The Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen (1875), founded by the brewer J. C. Jacobsen, produced Emil Christian
  Hansen's pure yeast culture (1883) and Søren Sørensen's pH scale (1909) (*memory*). Industrial laboratories began in the oldest
  food trades as well as in the newest chemical ones.
- **Scale.** In the United States, firms founded more than 1,100 laboratories between 1919 and 1936. Research staff rose from about
  3,000 engineers and scientists in 1921 to more than 27,000 by 1940 (*checked* via a secondary source citing the National Research
  Council surveys; the exact counts are in NRC Bulletins 60 and 91). Mowery found large firms no more research-intensive than small
  ones in 1921 to 1946 (*checked*).
- **Bell Labs** was formed on 1 January 1925 from the Western Electric and AT&T engineering departments, with about 3,600 to 4,000
  staff at its founding (*checked*; the two figures differ by source). It produced the transistor (1947; part 08), information theory
  (1948) and the solar cell (1954) (*memory*). Under the 1956 consent decree AT&T had to license its patents, the transistor's among
  them, at low royalties, which is part of why the semiconductor industry spread beyond AT&T (*memory*).
- **Why firms did it** (Chandler, Mowery): new technical knowledge had become an input that a large firm could not buy reliably on
  the market. Patents were contested, inventors were independent, and integrating research with production and marketing paid. The
  in-house laboratory is a case of Coase's make-or-buy answer (part 05).

### 6.8 State science

States had long paid for knowledge they needed: calendars, maps (the Cassini survey of France, the Ordnance Survey from 1791,
*memory*), arsenals and dockyards, the École polytechnique (1794), state agricultural stations (§4.5). The twentieth century made it
large:

- **The First World War** made chemistry a war industry: Haber–Bosch nitrogen for explosives (part 02's `synthetic_nitrogen`), gas,
  optical glass (*memory*).
- **The Second World War** joined universities, firms and the state. Vannevar Bush's Office of Scientific Research and Development
  (1941) contracted universities and firms for radar (MIT's Radiation Laboratory), the proximity fuze, and the scale-up of penicillin
  (part 08, `penicillin_fermentation`) (*memory*).
- **The Manhattan Project** spent about $2.2 billion by the war's end and employed about 130,000 people at peak, around June 1944.
  Over 90% of the cost went to building plants (uranium enrichment at Oak Ridge, plutonium reactors at Hanford), not to laboratories
  (*checked*). It was an industrial programme built on a few years of physics, and it produced the nuclear industry (part 08,
  `nuclear_power`).
- **After 1945:** Bush's *Science, the Endless Frontier* (1945) argued for public funding of basic research, and the National
  Science Foundation followed in 1950 (*memory*). After Sputnik came ARPA (February 1958) and NASA (1958) (*memory*). ARPA funded
  the ARPANET (1969; part 08's `internet`) and much early computing, and later work funded by the defence department led to GPS
  (*memory*). Military procurement bought the first integrated circuits in quantity (part 08).
- **The planned economies** built science cities (Akademgorodok, 1957, *memory*) and academy institutes separate from production.
  Joseph Berliner's studies found that Soviet enterprises had weak reasons to adopt innovations, because plans rewarded output and
  punished disruption (*memory*). Strong science combined with weak diffusion is the socialist planned branch's knowledge profile
  (part 06).

### 6.9 R&D as a sector

- **Size.** WIPO estimates world R&D at more than **$2.75 trillion in 2023**, nearly three times the level of 2000, and nearly **2% of
  world GDP**, up from under 1.5% in 2000. An OECD release puts 2024 at about $3.8 trillion on a different basis (*checked*; these are
  estimates built on UNESCO, OECD and Eurostat data).
- **Who does it.** Business performed **74%** of OECD R&D in 2023 (66% in 2010), and 66% in the EU (*checked*). Israel (6.3% of GDP)
  and South Korea (5.0%) are the most R&D-intensive economies; the OECD as a whole spends about 2.7% (*checked*).
- **Its structure.** States pay for most basic research, done in universities and government laboratories; firms do most
  development. Venture capital (from 1946, *memory*) finances firms whose main asset is know-how. The largest spenders are in
  software, pharmaceuticals, cars and semiconductors (*rough*).
- **Its chokepoints.** Part 08 calls ASML's EUV lithography "a chokepoint of knowledge, built over decades with no substitute", and
  part 13 lists Zeiss as its sole optics supplier. Drug development runs through randomised trials that cost hundreds of millions
  to billions per approved drug (part 13; *contested* in its size).
- **What persists from the village.** Tacit knowledge still moves with people, so firms cluster where skilled workers change jobs;
  trade secrets still protect most process know-how; apprenticeship survives as the postdoc and the residency.

## 7. How knowledge-making joined the economy's chains

Knowledge institutions sit at the joints between chains. Five long chains show it:

1. **Glass to lithography.** Glass beads and vessels (part 10) → forest glass and Venetian *cristallo* (part 01) → spectacles (about
   1286) → the lens trade → the telescope and microscope (1608, 1620s) → optical glass (Fraunhofer, Jena 1884) → microscopes for
   bacteriology, rangefinders, cameras → photolithography optics (part 08) → Zeiss's EUV optics for ASML (part 13). A luxury trade
   became the narrowest point of the modern economy.
2. **Silver to steam.** Silver amalgamation needed mercury (Huancavelica, Almadén) → the barometer and air pump (1643 to 1659) → the
   knowledge that air has weight → Newcomen's atmospheric engine (1712) → Watt (who was an instrument maker) → thermodynamics (Carnot
   1824).
3. **Prayer to the factory.** Monastic hours → the mechanical clock (late thirteenth century) → town clocks and time discipline →
   clockmakers' gear-cutting → the water frame and mill machinery (part 07) → the pendulum and chronometer → precision instruments →
   the dividing engine and machine tools.
4. **Burials to sewers.** Parish burial counts → the bills of mortality → Graunt (1662) → life tables (Halley 1693) → life insurance;
   and → civil registration (1837) → Snow (1854) and Farr → public health acts, sewers and filtration (parts 07, 08, 12).
5. **Beer to statistics.** The brewer's thermometer (1762) and saccharometer (1784) → the Carlsberg Laboratory (1875) and pure yeast
   (1883) → Gosset's *t*-test at Guinness (1908) → Fisher at Rothamsted (1919) → randomised trials → the pharmaceutical pipeline.

And one farm chain: estate accounts and husbandry books → Bakewell's progeny tests and herd books → Rothamsted's plots (1843) and
superphosphate → experiment stations → Mendelian breeding → hybrid maize → the Green Revolution → broiler genetics firms (parts 02,
13).

**Findings across this part.**

1. **Know-how came long before knowing-that.** Most techniques were found by trial and kept by selection without being understood;
   they can be lost when the know-how stops travelling (nixtamalisation, Tasmania).
2. **Depth of know-how is held by numbers and connection** and lost faster than it is built (§1.3).
3. **Know-how was property,** held by households, secrets and guilds. Every way of opening it up (print, patents, societies,
   journals) lowered access costs at someone's expense.
4. **Instruments rode on luxury and craft trades** and often came generations before the theory they made possible.
5. **Records kept for other purposes became instruments of discovery** once someone read a long run of them.
6. **Who paid moved** from patrons and religious houses to academies and prizes, to firms, to states, and to R&D as a sector
   performed mostly by business.
7. **Knowledge chokepoints recur:** the arcanum, the guild, the patent, the industrial laboratory, the sole supplier of lithography
   optics.

## 8. What the border would take, and what stays map only

**Zach's current lean (16 Nov).** The engine keeps **know-how**, the interface by which people act on the world: techniques, recipes
and the dependencies between them, held per person at four levels (aware, rough, capable, known) and learned by teaching, watching and
trying, with no blind crafting (`cybertron2/engine/knowledge.py`; proposals/knowledge.md §3, §4c, §6). The engine also keeps
**perception**, with instruments as extended, targetable senses. **Knowing-that** stays in agents' own memories and records. Discovery
comes through **small grids of choices in about a dozen techniques**, **leaps through surprising signs**, and **local, contingent
facts** rather than universal laws the models already know (OPEN [[L-103 Crafting and discovering new things|L-103]]; 02zzk §2, "Discovery: inside the border, small"). Read
against this history, that line is Mokyr's split (§5): the engine holds λ (prescriptive knowledge), and the agents hold Ω.

**Instruments as targetable senses**, in engine terms, means this: what the person points the instrument at (a lot, a coin, a post's
shadow) is the target, its reading is a sign at the state's resolution, and the person's skill gates how plain the reading is. All
three already exist as attention, `knack` and the noticing rule. An instrument is then a good or a fitting with a chain behind it.
That is how the history reads too. None of this is proposed here: it is what 02zzk's "Next" row and [[L-103 Crafting and discovering new things|L-103]] would take if a question
called for them.

### Behind the social primitives ([[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]])

- **Apprenticeship as an agreement:** years of labour for teaching, with notice and remedies when either side leaves early (part 05
  §1.7). agreements.md lists it as a preset. The engine can pass the skill; it cannot yet hold the years.
- **Paid teaching and refused teaching,** with a reputation for either: the knowledge proposal's "when teaching pays" (proposals/
  knowledge.md §4c) needs agreements that hold.
- **Secrets kept by oath or rule:** a household's or a guild's ban on teaching outsiders, enforced by people.
- **A written recipe as a document,** read by the literate: spec/information/letters_and_documents.md (S); reading is not built.
- **A standard weight held by a party** (a lord, a market, a church) and the assize that checks measures. It needs institutions with a
  store and offices (part 09 §9, addition 2; part 18 on measures).

### At the border: the market town

The guild with its masterpiece and searches; the market's standard weights and the assize of bread and ale; the church's sundial and
bell as public time; a book of husbandry or of secrets in a literate house; the miller's toll measured in the lord's bushel; the
monastery as a party that holds records and land (part 16). Each needs the institutions of [[L-106 MAJOR what the engine remembers and settles for agents, and…|L-106]] and part 09's additions 1 to 3, as
02zzk's "At the border" row says.

### Past the border: map only

Spectacles, lenses, telescopes and microscopes; mechanical and pendulum clocks; compass, astrolabe and chronometer; the barometer,
the thermometer and the still as a chemist's instrument; printed craft books and encyclopaedias; academies, societies, journals,
prizes and patents; political arithmetic and life tables; field trials, experiment stations and statistical design; pedigree
breeding with books; precision machining and standards laboratories; industrial research laboratories, state science and R&D as a
sector. These need literacy and documents at scale, institutions beyond the household, populations large enough to hold deep skill
(§1.3: a village of a few dozen people is Tasmania, not Florence), and eras no run reaches (02zzk "Past the border"; part 09
additions 6 to 8).

One general caution comes from this history. Discovery of **universal** knowledge (that air has weight, that microbes cause
disease, that nitrogen limits wheat) is what the models already carry, so making it a mechanic would test nothing. The history's
discovery before science was overwhelmingly **local** and **prescriptive**: the setting that works here, the clay that fires on this
hill, the sign that the run has come. That is the kind Zach's lean keeps.
