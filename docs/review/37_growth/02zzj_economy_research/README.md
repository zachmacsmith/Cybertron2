## What the parts find, across all of them

1. **Land set the ceiling until coal** (01, 02, 07). Food, fodder, fuel, timber, fibre and fats all grew on the same ground, at a
   few tonnes of dry matter a hectare a year. England around 1300 got 1,500 to 1,800 kcal of grain a day a person from wheat at
   three or four times the seed. Each person burned one to two tonnes of wood a year, and a forest glassworks burned 150 to 200
   kg of wood for each kilogram of glass. The industrial revolution was above all the switch from this organic economy to a
   mineral one (Wrigley): coal was half of England's energy by 1700 and over 90% by 1850.
2. **Nitrogen held farming back for a thousand years** (02). Grain needs nitrogen; nitrogen came from dung; the beasts' grass
   competed with grain for land. Legumes, leys, clover and turnips, guano and finally synthetic nitrogen (which now feeds 40 to 50%
   of people) each closed the gap a little more, and the last one separated herds from fields.
3. **A few intermediates hold an economy together, and chains merge where they share one** (01, 03, 07, 08). Fuel and charcoal,
   cord and cloth, leather, worked wood, containers, lime, salt, fats and malt appear in almost every pre-industrial chain. Iron and
   tanning competed for oak; fulling, bellows, hammers and grinding competed for the same stream. Each big change was a substitute
   that freed one chain from a shared bottleneck, after which it moved and grew: coke for charcoal (iron to the coalfields), chrome
   salts for bark (leather to the chemical works), synthetic soda for ash and kelp, Haber–Bosch for manure.
4. **Each paradigm switch makes a centre, an owner, a rent and a quarrel** (02, 03, 05). The quern became the lord's mill (tenants
   owed suit of mill and paid a sixteenth to a twenty-fourth of their grain; England had about 10,000 mills by 1300); the fulling
   mill made a lord's monopoly; the blast furnace made a capitalist's works with a hired crew; the deer park and warren turned a
   commons into guarded property. The same pattern recurs industrially: the four largest US beef packers went from a quarter of
   slaughter in 1976 to 85% in 2019.
5. **Transport and information set the size of markets** (04, 12). Before rail, land carriage cost many times water carriage (a
   medieval English cart added about 3% to wheat's price every ten miles; firewood paid only within about 20 km of London), so
   cities sat on water and markets a day's walk apart. Each later fall in cost (canals, rail, steamships, containers, air freight)
   widened markets and deepened specialisation; prices converged slowly from the late 15th century and fast with the telegraph.
6. **Money began as credit, and promises were held by people** (04, 05, 09). Mesopotamian silver and barley served as units of
   account with fixed interest rates long before coins; even coined economies ran mostly on tallies, shop books and running debts
   enforced by witnesses and courts. Every later form of money rested on whoever stood behind the promise. This is the gap the
   engine has most clearly: it settles agreements itself (OPEN L-106), and part 09 ranks fixing that first.
7. **Joint products and chokepoints made industries more than inventions did** (08, 13). Gasoline was a waste by-product of
   kerosene before the car; coal tar from gasworks became dyes, drugs and plastics; asphalt is a refinery residue. Control of a
   narrow step (Standard Oil's refineries, Chile's nitrate, ASML's lithography, rare-earth separation) shaped each industry more
   than control of its raw material.
8. **Each network rides on the one before** (08, 12). The telegraph ran beside the railway; roads took the refineries' asphalt;
   fibre runs beside rail and pipelines. Cities grew in steps gated by infrastructure: streets and a market, clean water, drainage,
   bulk transport, fuel networks, mechanical transport, electricity and lifts, sewage treatment.
9. **Specialisation compounds wealth and fragility together** (10, 13, 06). Bronze-using societies depended on tin routes a
   thousand kilometres long; the Late Bronze Age collapse ran those dependencies backwards. Today one firm makes the EUV lithography
   machines and one most leading-edge chips, and shocks at single points cross the whole tree in weeks. Collapse loses depth faster
   than it was built.
10. **Deep economies did not need Europe's path** (11). Song China made iron in tens of thousands of tonnes with coal and issued
    paper money; India clothed much of the Old World in cotton; the Islamic world ran transferable credit across three continents;
    the Inca fed millions with no markets and no money. What none did, and Europe did late, was switch to mineral energy.
11. **Farming did not win by paying better per hour** (10). Early cereal farming returned about 63% of foraging's food per hour
    (Bowles); it won because it fed more people per hectare, could be stored and defended, and raised birth rates.
12. **Cities are supply problems first, and density pays once they are fed** (12). Pre-industrial cities killed more than were
    born (London around 1700 needed about 8,000 net migrants a year). Once fed, a doubling of city size raises productivity by
    roughly 2 to 8%, and outputs such as wages and patents scale faster than population.
13. **The modern economy is mostly coordination** (13). US farm work fell from 41% of workers in 1900 to under 2%; manufacturing
    peaked at 32% of non-farm jobs in 1953; services passed half of the world's jobs. By 2008 about half of world trade was in
    intermediate goods inside global chains.

## What it means for the engine

Part 09 maps every layer onto the engine. Its reading: the village economy is complete as physics; above it, the next layers
(lordship, the market town, merchants) need **social primitives** more than goods, and most are designed or were built in
Cybertron 1; past the market town the obstacle is **representation** (people who are not agents, aggregate production and
markets, a hierarchy of places, time at more than one rate). Its ten additions, in order:

1. agreements made social, not automatic (L-106): goods credit paid by the debtor; deals and defaults seen by witnesses; tallies
   and bonds as documents;
2. parties beyond the household: institutions with offices, a seat, a store and claims;
3. the decided presets made real: notice, remedies, per-unit legs (multure, piece rates), ledgers at a seat, legs set by an
   institution's rule (dues, tolls, taxes);
4. standing orders for adults, and agents called less than every day;
5. edges by kind (carts on tracks, boats on rivers) and the market as a place, with prices remembered with place and date;
6. rule-run households as full entities;
7. populations as numbers by place, on the ecology's pattern;
8. a hierarchy of places;
9. history runs that start a world at a later layer;
10. technology as capabilities, and discovery.

The other parts' closing sections agree on the near layers: the coppice, metal bound to charcoal (part 03: the engine's charcoal
input is far too light; a bloomery took 10 to 18 kg of charcoal a kilogram of iron), the carcass's joint products, the river as one
site with many claims, the mill as a chokepoint with a toll, ale as barley's sink, prices that travel and go stale, a market day,
water much cheaper than land, credit with reckoning days, and famine as loss of entitlement rather than of food (parts 01 to 05).
Part 01 also notes the engine lacks fodder for draught animals and fuel for salt, glass and lime works.

budget ran out part-way for several authors, and some sites were blocked (Wikipedia in some sessions, PubMed Central, some
university hosts), so:

- **part 01** tags each figure [V] checked, [C] its own arithmetic or [M] memory;
- **part 05** lists several standard figures as unchecked (Muldrew's litigation levels, the licensing share, the size of gig
  work, British tax shares), and its household labour year is an illustration, not a sourced series;
- **part 12**'s decimals for Bettencourt's scaling exponents come from secondary summaries;
- **part 13**'s IEA minerals-per-car figures and Pfizer supplier count are from memory, marked rough;
- **part 11** names the figures recalled from standard works (the Kanesh volumes, the Han iron-office count, Song coin output,
  Cotapachi's storehouses, Mundy's ox train, al-Bakri's salt tax);
- **part 06**'s figures rest mostly on summaries of papers rather than the papers.

Before any figure is used to calibrate the engine, check it against its primary source.
