## Summary

For more than 99% of the human story the economy was foraging: small bands moving across territories, taking wild food with
tools of stone, wood, bone and fibre, and sharing almost everything that came in. That economy was not simple. It already had
long skill (knapping, tracking), chokepoints (good stone, ochre, water), time capital (traps, weirs, stored nuts and fish),
pulses (salmon runs, acorn falls, migrating herds), commons (game and shellfish beds) and long-distance exchange of a few
light, valued goods (shell beads, obsidian, ochre). Ethnographic return rates show its logic: big game pays ten to thirty
thousand kilocalories an hour once found, small seeds a few hundred, so people take the best and drop down the list only when
the best runs short. The fall to low-ranked seeds and nuts after the large game declined, together with storage and settling
down, made the ground ready for farming. Farming itself did not at first pay better per hour (Bowles 2011 puts early cereal
cultivation at about 63% of the wild-food mean); it won because it paid more per hectare, could be stored, and supported more
children.

From that start, the chains of the first economies formed and joined in a recognisable order. Domestication (in at least seven
centres, between about 11,500 and 4,000 years ago) gave grain and herds; over the next five thousand years herds yielded milk,
wool, traction and transport as well as meat (Sherratt's secondary products, now known to have come in piecemeal). Fire, once
it could be made at will, became a chain of rising temperatures: cooking, heat-treated stone, lime plaster, pottery kilns, and at
last copper smelting around 5000 BC. Exchange networks for obsidian, flint, shell, amber and salt were already in place when
metal arrived, and bronze turned them into a chokepoint economy: copper and tin almost never occur together, so every
bronze-using society depended on routes a thousand kilometres or more long. The first cities and states (Uruk, Egypt, the Indus,
the Aegean palaces) were machines for gathering grain and wool, paying workers in rations, and trading textiles for the timber,
metal and stone their river plains lacked; writing began in Mesopotamia as bookkeeping for that machine. Each step needed the
last, and each opened several others: the dependency trees in §6 show which.

**How to read the numbers.** Dates are calibrated: *ka* is thousands of years ago, *Ma* millions, *BP* before present (1950),
*BC* for the last ten thousand years. **[firm]** means widely agreed; **[rough]** an estimate or order of magnitude;
**[contested]** disputed in the literature. Sources follow each section and are listed at the end. Where a figure is quoted from
a secondary summary and not checked against the original here, it says so.

## 1. Foraging economies

### 1.1 Stone tools: industries and their raw materials

| Industry | From | What it is | Raw material and sourcing | Skill | What it enabled |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lomekwian** | ~3.3 Ma (Kenya) **[contested as a separate industry]** | big cores struck on an anvil; crude flakes | local cobbles | low | cutting, pounding |
| **Oldowan** | ~2.6 Ma (Ledi-Geraru, Gona); perhaps 2.9 Ma (Nyayanga) **[firm for 2.6]** | sharp flakes knocked off cobbles; choppers | mostly within a few km; at Nyayanga cobbles carried **up to 13 km** for chosen stone (2025 study) **[firm]** | low to middling: needs an eye for angles | butchery of large carcasses (cut-marked bones), marrow; meat and fat enter the diet |
| **Acheulean** | ~1.76 Ma to ~0.2 Ma | the hand-axe and cleaver: a planned shape, worked on both faces | usually local (under ~20 km), but chosen for quality; large flakes struck from boulders | middling to high: months to learn a good hand-axe (experimental knappers) **[rough]** | a long-lasting all-purpose tool; the same form for over a million years (shallow, but with a high skill ceiling) |
| **Prepared-core (Levallois), Middle Stone Age / Middle Palaeolithic** | ~0.3 Ma | a core shaped so that one blow gives a flake of planned form; points for hafting | at Olorgesailie (Kenya) obsidian from **25 to 95 km** about 300 ka (Brooks et al. 2018) **[firm]** | high | **hafting**: stone points on wooden shafts, glued with tar or gum; composite tools |
| **Blade industries, Upper Palaeolithic / Later Stone Age** | ~50 to 40 ka (earlier in Africa) | long, thin blades from prismatic cores; burins to work bone and antler | good flint moved 100 to 200 km and more in Europe **[rough]** | high | far more cutting edge per kilo of stone; bone needles, harpoons, spear-thrower hooks |
| **Microliths** | sporadic from ~70 ka (South Africa, Pinnacle Point ~71 ka); general after ~20 ka and in the Mesolithic | tiny geometric pieces set in rows in a shaft | small nodules suffice: **frees people from big, good stone** | middling | arrows, barbed spears, sickles; replaceable parts (a broken barb is swapped, not the whole tool) |
| **Ground stone** | grinding stones from ~30 ka in places; polished axes with farming (~10 ka) | stone shaped by pecking and grinding | tough rocks (basalt, jadeite, greenstone), often from one quarry traded far | low skill per hour, very many hours | felling forest (axes), milling seeds (querns), the farming landscape |

**Chain shape.** Stone working is **long skill** (knapping takes years to master and failures waste stone), sits on a **chokepoint**
(fine-grained flint, chert, obsidian or quartzite outcrops are patchy), and is **accretive** over hundreds of thousands of years:
hafting, heat treatment, pressure flaking and microliths each added to what came before. Microliths are a substitute that
relaxed the chokepoint: poor small stone was enough once the tool became a composite of many small parts.

**How sourcing changes.** Distance travelled for stone grows by orders of magnitude: a few kilometres in the Oldowan (13 km at
most), tens of kilometres in the Middle Stone Age, a hundred or more in the Upper Palaeolithic, and with farming, thousands
(Alpine jade axes in Scotland, §3). Early long moves are probably people walking to the outcrop; later ones are exchange
between groups.

*Sources:* Nyayanga transport (Science Advances 2025, "Selective use of distant stone resources by the earliest Oldowan
toolmakers"); Ledi-Geraru >2.58 Ma; Brooks et al. 2018 (Science) for Olorgesailie; Brown et al. 2012 (Nature) for Pinnacle Point
microliths.

### 1.2 Fire and cooking

| Step | When | Evidence | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| fire used (perhaps taken from wildfire) | ~1.0 Ma **[contested]** | burnt bone and ash at Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa | warmth, protection, first cooking |
| repeated hearths | ~790 ka | Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, Israel (burnt flint in clusters) | hearths as a place: a camp centre |
| habitual fire | ~400 to 300 ka **[firm in outline]** | Qesem Cave (Israel), many European sites | cooking as normal; long evenings; heat for other work |
| **making fire at will** | **~400 ka** | Barnham, Suffolk: a heated hearth and **iron pyrite**, a mineral foreign to the site, struck on flint to make sparks (Davis et al., Nature, Dec. 2025); previously the oldest firm evidence was ~50 ka (Neanderthal strike-a-lights, France) | fire no longer needs to be carried or found; anyone with flint and pyrite (or a fire drill) can start one |
| heat-treating stone | ~164 ka (Pinnacle Point, South Africa) **[firm]** | silcrete heated to ~350 °C flakes better | the first use of fire to change a material: a two-step chain (fuel + stone → better stone) |
| adhesives | birch-bark tar from ~200 ka (Neanderthals, Italy); compound gum and ochre glues in South Africa ~70 ka | tar residues on tools | hafting: a stone point, a shaft and a glue are **assembled** from three chains |
| landscape burning | late Pleistocene and Holocene (Australian "firestick farming", North American prairies) | charcoal records, ethnography | fire as a tool on the land: fresh growth draws game; a managed commons |

**Cooking as an energy technology.** Richard Wrangham's argument (Catching Fire, 2009) is that cooking raised the energy people
get from the same food (it gelatinises starch, denatures protein, softens fibre and kills pathogens), allowing smaller guts and
larger brains. Carmody and colleagues (2011) found mice gain more on cooked than raw food of the same weight **[firm for the
effect; its size in humans is rough]**. Raw-food diets in modern people commonly lead to weight loss and, in many women, lost
menstruation **[firm]**. For an economy the point is simple: **fire multiplies food**, and every hearth is a fitting that every
other food chain passes through.

**What fire costs.** Fuel: a forager hearth burns a few kilograms of wood an hour **[rough]**, and wood collection is a daily
job that grows longer as a camp stays and strips the nearby ground: one of the push factors for moving camp.

**The pyrotechnology ladder.** Fire's later history is a ladder of temperatures, each a new chain:

| Temperature | Process | First |
|---|---|---|
| ~350 °C | heat-treating silcrete and flint | ~164 ka |
| 500 to 800 °C | an open fire; roasting, drying, smoking | Palaeolithic |
| ~750 to 900 °C | burning limestone to lime for plaster | Pre-Pottery Neolithic B Levant, ~9,000 BC; huge fuel cost per tonne |
| 600 to 1,000 °C | pottery (open firing, then kilns) | ~20 ka (China, §2.4) |
| ~900 °C | faience and glazes | Egypt and Mesopotamia, 5th to 4th millennium BC |
| 1,085 °C (copper melts) to ~1,200 °C | **smelting copper**: needs a closed furnace, charcoal and forced draught (blowpipes, later bellows) | ~5000 BC (Belovode, Serbia) |
| 1,150 to 1,300 °C | iron bloomery | ~1200 BC in Anatolia and the Levant (outside this file) |

Each rung needed the one below in fuel handling and furnace building, and each gave the next one something (pottery kilns gave
the crucibles and the draught control that smelting used).

*Sources:* Davis et al. 2025, "Earliest evidence of making fire", Nature (doi 10.1038/s41586-025-09855-6); Natural History
Museum and UCL press releases, 10 Dec. 2025; Brown et al. 2009 (Science) for heat treatment; Wrangham 2009; Carmody et al. 2011
(PNAS).

### 1.3 Hunting technologies

| Technology | Earliest firm evidence | How it works | Range and effect | Inputs (other chains) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| scavenging and close killing with clubs and stones | >2 Ma | taking carcasses from predators; dispatching trapped or weak animals | a few metres | none beyond stone |
| **wooden thrusting and throwing spears** | Schöningen, Germany: eight or more spruce and pine spears, long dated ~300 ka, re-dated in 2025 to ~200 ka **[contested date]**; Clacton spear tip ~400 ka | sharpened, fire-hardened or carefully shaped shafts | thrust, or thrown ~10 to 20 m | straight young trees; a stone tool to shape them |
| **stone-tipped spears** | ~500 ka, Kathu Pan, South Africa **[contested]**; common by ~300 ka | a point hafted to a shaft | deeper wounds | stone, shaft, glue and binding (assembly) |
| **spear-thrower (atlatl)** | firm finds from ~20 to 17 ka (Solutrean and Magdalenian, France, e.g. Combe-Saunière); probably older **[contested]** | a lever that lengthens the arm | darts thrown ~30 to 50 m with far more energy | a carved hook (antler), light darts |
| **bow and arrow** | Sibudu, South Africa, bone point ~61.7 ka, read as an arrowhead **[contested]**; Grotte Mandrin, France, tiny points ~54 ka, damage matching bow shots (Metz et al. 2023) | stored energy in a stave | accurate at ~20 to 50 m; silent; one person can hunt alone | a stave, **cord** (sinew or plant fibre), feathers, microlith or bone points: an assembly of four chains |
| **nets** | cord impressions in clay at Pavlov and Dolní Věstonice, Czech Republic, ~29 to 26 ka; a Neanderthal three-ply cord fragment, Abri du Maras, ~50 to 40 ka; cordage at Ohalo II, Israel, 19.4 ka | communal net drives of hares and small game; fish nets | brings women, children and elders into hunting | **cord** in quantity (many hours of twisting fibre) |
| **traps and snares** | inferred in the Upper Palaeolithic; wooden fish traps preserved from the Mesolithic (Ireland, Denmark, ~7,000 to 5,000 BC) | set gear works while people do something else | **time capital**: built once, checked daily | cord, withies, stakes |
| **drives and kills** | buffalo jumps in North America (Head-Smashed-In, used ~5,800 years); "desert kites" (stone funnels many km long) in the Near East and Arabia, many from ~9,000 to 5,000 BP; Great Basin antelope drive fences | a crowd moves a herd into a trap, pound or over a cliff | hundreds of animals in a day: a **pulse** | many people, leadership, years of building; the meat needs processing and storage at once |
| **dogs** | domestication well before 15 ka **[contested in detail]** | trackers and bay-holders | raise hunting returns and safety | food for the dogs |

**How the chains joined.** The bow is the clearest example of an **assembly**: it needs a wood chain (staves), a fibre chain
(strings), a bird chain (fletching), a stone or bone chain (points) and a glue chain (tar or gum and fire). Its effect on the
economy was to make hunting a one-person task with small game reachable, so returns per hunter evened out and small, fast prey
(birds, rabbits) entered the diet. Nets and drives went the other way: they made hunting a crowd's work and created the first
occasions where a group had to be organised, led and fed in one place.

*Sources:* Metz et al. 2023 (Science Advances) for Grotte Mandrin; Lombard and Phillipson 2010 and Backwell et al. 2018
(Antiquity) for Sibudu; Hardy et al. 2020 (Scientific Reports) for the Abri du Maras cord; Soffer, Adovasio and colleagues on the
Pavlov impressions; Thieme 1997 (Nature) for Schöningen, with the 2025 re-dating (Leder et al., Science Advances).

### 1.4 Fishing and shellfish

| Step | When and where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| shellfish as a staple on a coast | Pinnacle Point, South Africa, ~164 ka (Marean et al. 2007) **[firm]** | low risk, predictable, gathered by anyone; tidal timing to learn |
| barbed bone points for fish | Katanda, DR Congo, ~90 ka **[contested date]** | large catfish taken in seasonal shallows |
| deep-sea fishing | Jerimalai, East Timor: tuna and other open-water fish ~42 ka; a shell fish-hook ~23 to 16 ka (O'Connor et al. 2011) | needs boats and lines |
| shell fish-hooks | Sakitari Cave, Okinawa, ~23 ka (Fujita et al. 2016) | |
| weirs and fixed traps | Mesolithic Europe and the Holocene everywhere; Brewarrina fish traps in Australia (age disputed, perhaps thousands of years) | **time capital** and a **commons**: a weir affects everyone downstream |
| large shell middens | from the Holocene on all coasts (Jōmon Japan, Ertebølle Denmark, California, South America's sambaquis) | the mounds themselves become territorial markers |
| salmon economies | North-west coast of North America, at least ~5,000 BP | the richest foragers known: villages, plank houses, slaves, ranked chiefs, potlatch |

Fish and shellfish matter to economic history because they are the forager resources most like crops: **dense, predictable and
seasonal**. Where they were rich (the North-west Coast, Jōmon Japan, the Baltic), foragers became sedentary, stored large
surpluses, and developed inequality, property in fishing stations and specialists, without farming. These "complex
hunter-gatherers" are proof that **storage, not farming, is what opens the door to wealth and rank** (Testart 1982).

### 1.5 Food storage

| Way | Where and when | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| caching meat in cold ground or under stones | Upper Palaeolithic Eurasia; Arctic ethnography | short time capital |
| storage pits beside mammoth-bone houses | Mezhyrich and other Ukrainian sites, ~15 ka | a camp revisited for winters |
| drying, smoking, rendering fat, pounding pemmican | ethnographic everywhere; archaeologically inferred | turns a **pulse** (a kill, a run) into food for months |
| stored nuts and acorns | California (acorns: leaching tannins with water takes hours per kilo), Jōmon (chestnut, horse chestnut pits), Mesolithic Europe (hazelnut roasting pits) | a pulse in autumn, eaten all winter |
| smoked salmon in plank houses | North-west coast | the economic base of rank |
| **purpose-built granaries for wild grain** | Dhra', Jordan, ~11,300 to 11,175 cal BP: raised floors for air and against rodents, between houses, perhaps shared; about **1,000 years before domesticated cereals** (Kuijt and Finlayson 2009) **[firm]** | storage came first; farming followed |

**The social effect.** James Woodburn's distinction (1982) is the key one: **immediate-return** foragers (Hadza, !Kung) eat what
they get within days, have few possessions and strongly levelled sharing; **delayed-return** foragers (anyone with stores, traps,
boats or weirs) have assets that must be guarded and inherited, so they develop property, binding kin rules and, often, rank.
Storage turns sharing from a norm about food into a question about who owns the store.

### 1.6 Sharing and exchange norms

| Norm | Where | How it works | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **demand sharing** (Peterson 1993) | Australian Aboriginal, Hadza, !Kung | food is shared because others ask, and refusal is shameful | spreads large, chancy kills across households |
| **tolerated theft** (Blurton Jones 1984) | | a big kill is more than a family can use; defending it costs more than letting others take some | meat flows widely without a rule |
| **meat shared, plant food kept** | Ache (Paraguay): most of a hunter's kill is eaten outside his own family (Kaplan and Hill 1985) **[firm in outline]** | high-variance foods are pooled; low-variance foods stay in the household | **risk pooling**: a band is an insurance pool |
| **show-off hunting** (Hawkes 1991) | Hadza, Ache | men target big game for prestige even when smaller game pays better | status as a currency; mates and allies |
| **hxaro** gift partnerships | Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) San, Botswana and Namibia: on average **16 partners** (range 2 to 42) spread within **~200 km**, rising to ~24 in mature adulthood and ~12 in old age (Wiessner 1977, 1982; Santa Fe summary) **[firm]** | delayed gifts (beads, arrows, ornaments) keep a partnership alive; in drought a family goes to live with a distant partner for months | a regional safety net; goods move far as a side-effect of insurance |
| **formal exchange networks** | Aboriginal Australia: ochre (Wilgie Mia, Parachilna), pituri (a stimulant), and greenstone axes from Mount William quarry, Victoria, carried hundreds of kilometres **[firm in outline; distances rough]** | exchange at ceremonies, along kin routes | specialised quarries owned by one group; goods valued by distance |
| **Sahlins' reciprocity spectrum** (1972) | general | generalised (no account kept: close kin), balanced (equivalents: neighbours), negative (haggling, theft: strangers) | the kind of exchange depends on social distance |

**The point for an economy.** Forager sharing is not altruism without accounts; it is an insurance system with known rules, and
it weakens as soon as food can be stored or a household's returns become predictable (farming). The first exchange goods
across long distances are **light, durable, valued and hard to get**: shell beads (Nassarius shells pierced as beads at ~100 to
75 ka, Skhul and Blombos), ochre, fine stone. Ostrich-eggshell beads in southern Africa travelled ~100 km and more as hxaro-like
gifts from at least 33 ka (Stewart et al. 2020, Lesotho) **[firm in outline]**.

|---|---|---|
| typical residential band | **~25 to 30 people** (median ~28 across a sample of warm-climate foragers) **[firm]** | Birdsell's "magic number 25"; Marlowe 2005 |
| regional "tribe" or dialect group | **~500** **[rough]** | Birdsell; Wobst |

| moves a year | from a few (coastal, stored food) to 30 or more (arid zones) | Kelly 2013 |

Two consequences: a band is too small to breed alone (it needs marriage partners from the regional group, so regional links are
a biological necessity), and too small to insure itself (so it needs partners elsewhere). **Exchange begins as a way to get
spouses and insurance, not goods.**

### 1.8 Foraging returns in numbers

Optimal foraging theory's **diet-breadth model** ranks resources by **post-encounter return rate** (calories gained per hour of
pursuit and processing once a resource is found). A forager takes a resource if its return beats the overall average rate of
searching for better ones. As top-ranked resources become scarce, the average falls and lower-ranked ones join the diet.

**Great Basin (western USA), experimental returns** (Simms 1985, 1987, as tabulated in Kelly 1995/2013; values in kcal/hour;
the pinyon figures are confirmed in Simms's pinyon study, the rest are quoted from Kelly's table as commonly reproduced
**[rough: experimental, wide ranges]**):

| Resource | kcal/hour | Rank |
|---|---|---|
| deer, bighorn sheep | ~18,000 to 31,000 | top |
| pronghorn antelope | ~16,000 to 31,000 | top |
| jackrabbit | ~13,000 to 16,000 | high |
| cottontail rabbit | ~9,000 to 10,000 | high |
| ground squirrel | ~5,000 to 6,000 | middle |
| cattail pollen | ~2,700 to 9,400 (by season) | middle, seasonal |
| ducks | ~2,000 to 2,700 | middle |
| pinyon nuts | ~850 to 1,500 | low but storable |
| shadscale seeds | ~1,200 | low |
| tansy mustard, sunflower, bluegrass, ricegrass seeds | ~300 to 1,300 | low |
| cattail roots | ~130 to 270 | lowest |

**Ache (eastern Paraguay), observed** (Hawkes, Hill and O'Connell 1982; Hill et al. 1987) **[firm, but depends on how tracking
time is counted]**:

| Resource | kcal/hour |
|---|---|
| white-lipped peccary | ~5,300 (with tracking time) to ~8,800 (after encounter) |
| oranges (feral) | ~4,400 |
| honey | ~3,200 |
| palm larvae | ~1,800 |
| palm starch and heart | ~1,000 |
| men's hunting overall | ~750 |
| mean of all foraging | ~1,340; women ~1,220 |

**Thresholds.** A forager needs very roughly **~370 kcal per hour of foraging** to stay alive, given usual work hours and family
size (a 2017 estimate from Kelly's and Binford's data) **[rough]**. Among the Ache, resources returning under ~870 kcal/hour are
predicted to be ignored, however often met.

**Reading the numbers.** Three facts carry over to any model:
1. **The spread is two orders of magnitude.** Big game beats small seeds by fifty to a hundred times per hour of handling. The
   reason anyone ever eats seeds is that big game is found rarely, and grows rarer under pressure.
2. **Seeds are low-ranked because of processing, not gathering.** Harvesting wild grass seed can be fast; threshing, winnowing,
   parching and grinding are slow. Grinding stones, baskets, pottery and later the quern are all tools that raise a low-ranked
   resource's rate. Jack Harlan (1967) cut wild einkorn in Turkey with a flint sickle and argued that a family could gather in
   about three weeks more grain than it could eat in a year (often summarised as about 1 kg of clean grain an hour) **[widely
   quoted; Harlan's figures not re-checked here]**. Gathering is quick; the year is spent processing and guarding.
3. **The broad spectrum revolution** (Flannery 1969; Stiner 2001): across the Near East and Europe after ~20 ka, diets widened
   from large game to small, fast animals (birds, hares), fish, shellfish and seeds. This is what the diet-breadth model predicts
   when people grow more numerous and top prey declines, and it is the base from which plant cultivation began.

**Foraging against early farming.** Samuel Bowles (2011, PNAS), using data on 15 cultivars like the first crops and five
foraging peoples, estimated that **early cereal cultivation returned about 63% of the calories per hour of the wild-food
mean**, including storage and processing **[rough; large uncertainties, admitted by the author]**. Farming spread not because
it paid more per hour but because it paid more per hectare (more people on the same land), could be stored, and, as Bowles and
Choi later argued, came with private property in fields and stores that farmers could defend.

*Sources:* Simms 1985 (pinyon; Journal of California and Great Basin Anthropology); Simms 1987 (BAR S381); Kelly 1995 and 2013,
The Lifeways of Hunter-Gatherers; Hawkes, Hill and O'Connell 1982 (American Ethnologist); Hill et al. 1987 (Ethology and
Sociobiology); Bowles 2011 (PNAS 108: 4760); Bowles and Choi 2013 (PNAS) and 2019 (Journal of Political Economy).

## 2. The Neolithic package

### 2.1 Centres of plant domestication

At least seven, perhaps over a dozen, independent centres. Domestication was slow: in the Near East the domestic forms of wheat
and barley (ears that do not shatter) took **2,000 to 3,000 years** to become dominant after cultivation began (Tanno and Willcox
2006; Fuller 2007), against experiments suggesting 20 to 200 years under strong selection (Hillman and Davies 1990). People
cultivated wild plants for millennia before domesticated ones appeared.

| Centre | Plants | Domestic forms from about | Animals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fertile Crescent** (Levant, upper Tigris and Euphrates, Zagros) | einkorn, emmer, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch, flax | ~10,500 to 9,500 BP **[firm]** | goat, sheep, cattle, pig (~10,500 BP), cat (commensal) |
| **North China** (Yellow River) | foxtail and broomcorn millet | ~10,000 to 8,000 BP | pig, dog; later chicken |
| **South China** (Yangzi) | rice (japonica) | cultivation from ~9,000 BP; non-shattering forms dominant ~7,000 to 6,000 BP **[contested pace]** | pig, water buffalo (later) |
| **New Guinea highlands** (Kuk swamp) | taro, banana, yam | drainage ditches and mounds ~7,000 to 6,500 BP; perhaps from ~10,000 BP **[contested]** | none (pigs arrive later) |
| **Mesoamerica** | squash (~10,000 BP, Guilá Naquitz), maize from teosinte (~9,000 BP, Xihuatoxtla), beans, chili, later cotton | maize cobs evolve over thousands of years | turkey, dog |
| **Andes and Amazonia** | potato, quinoa, oca; manioc, squash, peanut, chili, cotton | ~10,000 to 5,000 BP | llama, alpaca (~6,000 BP), guinea pig |
| **Eastern North America** | squash, sunflower, goosefoot (Chenopodium), marsh elder | ~5,000 to 4,000 BP | |
| **Sahel and West Africa** | pearl millet (~4,500 BP), sorghum, African rice, cowpea, yams | **herding preceded crops** in the Sahara | cattle herding from the Sahara (~8,000 BP) with Near Eastern stock; donkey |
| **Ethiopian highlands** | teff, finger millet, ensete, coffee | uncertain **[contested]** | |
| **South Asia** | some millets, mung bean, indica rice (hybrid with japonica), cotton (~7,000 BP Mehrgarh, earliest known cotton) | zebu cattle ~8,000 BP **[firm for zebu]** | zebu, water buffalo, chicken (debated) |

Crops spread fast once a package existed: farming moved into Europe at an average of about **1 km a year** (Ammerman and
Cavalli-Sforza's "wave of advance", 1971) **[firm in outline; uneven in practice]**, carried mainly by migrating farmers
(ancient DNA has confirmed this since 2012).

### 2.3 The secondary products revolution

Andrew Sherratt (1981, 1983) argued that for the first three or four thousand years of herding, animals were kept for meat
(primary products) and that a **package of secondary products** arrived together in the 4th millennium BC, spreading from the
Near East across Europe: **milk, wool, the plough, the wheeled cart, and riding**. He saw this as a second revolution that
changed land use (animals worth keeping alive longer, plough agriculture opening heavier soils), the division of labour by sex
(ploughing as men's work), and wealth (herds as inheritable capital).

**What the evidence now says.**

| Product | Sherratt's date | Evidence now | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **milk** | 4th millennium BC | milk fats in pottery from **~7000 BC** in north-west Anatolia (Evershed et al. 2008, over 2,200 vessels); Barcın Höyük, 6600 to 6000 BC, farmers arrive already knowing dairying (2024); cheese-strainer sherds in Poland ~5000 BC | **much earlier**: dairying is almost as old as herding |
| **lactase persistence** (adults digesting milk) | | spread in Europe mostly in the last ~3,000 years, long after dairying began (Evershed et al. 2022, Nature) | milk was first eaten as cheese, yogurt and butter, which have little lactose |
| **wool** | 4th millennium BC | woolly fleeces come with selection; wool textiles and large wool flocks appear in 4th-millennium Mesopotamian texts and later Bronze Age Europe | **about right**, but gradual |
| **traction** | 4th millennium BC | ard marks under long barrows (South Street, England, ~3500 BC); cattle foot and spine pathologies from draught work earlier in places (5th millennium) | **about right, with earlier beginnings** |
| **wheel and cart** | 4th millennium BC | Bronocice pot (Poland) cart image ~3500 BC; wooden wheel from the Ljubljana marshes ~3150 BC; Mesopotamian pictographs | **right**: the wheel appears in several regions within a few centuries |
| **riding** | 4th millennium BC | Botai bit wear disputed; firm riding and chariots in the late 3rd to early 2nd millennium BC | **later than he thought** |

**The economic reading.** The animal became a set of **joint products** (meat, milk, hide, dung, fibre, power) taken over its
lifetime rather than at one killing: **time capital**. A herd that is milked and worked must be kept alive, so herd management,
winter fodder, and inheritance of beasts follow. Traction was an **increasing-returns** threshold: an ox team and plough paid
only for someone with enough land, which (Sherratt and later Goody argued) concentrated land and wealth in families that owned
teams. The textile side made wool a commodity that could be gathered from many herders, spun and woven by many hands, stored and
carried far, and that is exactly what the first cities ran on (§5).

### 2.4 Pottery, weaving and ground stone

**Pottery.**

| Where | When | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Xianrendong Cave, south China | **~20,000 cal BP** (Wu et al. 2012, Science) **[firm]** | made by foragers, probably for cooking shellfish and boiling |
| Jōmon Japan, Russian Far East | ~16,000 BP | foragers; fish processing (lipid residues) |
| Saharan Africa (Ounjougou, Mali) | ~11,800 BP | foragers collecting wild grasses |
| Near East | ~7000 BC | **farming came first**: two thousand years of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, with plaster vessels and stone bowls instead |

Pottery is a **paradigm switch for cooking**: boiling (soups, porridges, rendering fat, extracting grease from bone) extracts more
food from low-ranked resources, and a pot stores grain, oil and milk against pests. It was invented by foragers several times
and was not part of the Near Eastern package at first. Clay is everywhere, but good clay, temper and fuel are local, and
potting takes long skill; it becomes a specialist craft (the potter's wheel, ~4th millennium BC in Mesopotamia) as soon as
demand is concentrated.

**Fibre and weaving.**
- Cord is Palaeolithic (§1.3). Wild flax fibres at Dzudzuana Cave, Georgia, ~30 ka (Kvavadze et al. 2009) **[contested
  reading]**.
- Woven linen, baskets and nets from Nahal Hemar cave, Israel, ~8,500 BP; textiles at Çatalhöyük, ~7000 BC.
- Spindle whorls (a weighted spindle) and the warp-weighted loom (loom weights) spread with the Neolithic; the horizontal ground
  loom in Egypt and Mesopotamia.
- **Labour arithmetic** (a widely used reconstruction): spinning, not weaving, is the bottleneck. With a hand spindle a spinner
  makes roughly 50 to 100 m of fine yarn an hour, and one square metre of cloth needs one to several kilometres of yarn, so a
  single garment can take **hundreds of hours** of spinning **[rough; from experimental archaeology, e.g. the Centre for Textile
  Research, Copenhagen]**. Cloth was one of the most labour-dense goods of the ancient world, which is why it was a store of
  value and the main export of the first cities.

**Ground stone.**
- Grinding slabs and pestles for seeds and pigments from ~30 ka; querns (saddle querns, then rotary querns much later, ~5th
  century BC) with farming.
- Polished stone axes for clearing forest: a farmer needs to fell trees; experiments show a polished stone axe fells a
  medium oak in hours, not minutes (many times slower than steel) **[rough]**.
- Axes became exchange goods: **Alpine jadeitite axes** from Monte Viso and Monte Beigua (Italy), 5300 to 4000 BC, carried up to
  ~1,800 km to Brittany, Britain, Ireland and Denmark (Projet JADE, Pétrequin et al. 2012) **[firm]**; **Langdale** (Cumbria) tuff
  axes across Britain. These were too valuable to be just tools: many were never used, and were deposited in bogs and graves.

### 2.5 Sedentism and storage

| Site | When | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Ohalo II, Sea of Galilee | 23,000 BP | brush huts; wild barley and wheat, ground on a stone; cord; perhaps small-scale cultivation of wild cereals |
| Natufian villages (Ain Mallaha, Hayonim) | ~15,000 to 11,700 BP | **sedentary foragers**: stone houses, cemeteries, sickles with gloss from cutting grass, mortars; dogs |
| Dhra', Jordan | ~11,300 cal BP | granaries before domestication (§1.5) |
| Göbekli Tepe, Turkey | ~9600 to 8200 BC | monumental carved pillars built by people still largely foraging; feasting (large quantities of wild cereal processing and beer **[contested]**); a gathering place that needed food for many workers |
| Jericho (PPNA) | ~9,000 BC | a stone wall and tower; a few hundred to a thousand people **[rough]** |
| Çatalhöyük, Turkey | 7100 to 5950 BC | dense town of mud-brick houses entered from the roof; **3,500 to 8,000 people** **[rough]**; each household its own store; no sign of a central authority; obsidian from Cappadocia 140 km away |

**What sedentism cost.** Skeletons from the early farming centuries show shorter stature, more dental caries, more
iron-deficiency marks and more infectious disease than among late foragers (Larsen 1995, 2006) **[firm in general, varies
regionally]**. Births rose: the **Neolithic demographic transition** (Bocquet-Appel 2011) is a rise in the share of children in
cemeteries, read as birth intervals falling from ~4 years among mobile foragers to ~2 to 3 among farmers **[firm in outline]**.
More people, worse health: the Malthusian trade-off at its origin.

### 2.6 Labour and yields

| Quantity | Figure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| early cultivation per hour | ~63% of wild-food mean (Bowles 2011) | §1.8 |

| intensity of early fields | stable isotopes show **manuring from the start** in Europe's first farmers (Bogaard et al. 2013, PNAS) **[firm]** | not slash-and-burn but fixed, fertilised plots near the house |

| Mesopotamian irrigated barley | very high early ratios claimed (§5.1) | |

**Why farming won anyway.** Per hectare, a cereal field yields many times the edible energy of wild land, so a farming
population could reach ten to a hundred times the density of foragers. Farmers outnumbered and displaced foragers (ancient DNA
in Europe shows this clearly), whatever the return per hour. And farm assets (fields, stores, herds) are defendable and
inheritable, which suited households rather than bands.

### 3.2 Grimes Graves in detail

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| shafts | **at least 433** recorded **[firm]** |
| depth | up to ~12 to 14 m to reach the best seam ("floorstone") |
| dates | the deep galleried shafts from about the third quarter of the 27th century BC; mining on and off to ~1400 BC |
| output per medium shaft | **~60 t of flint nodules** (the figure most sources repeat) **[rough]** |
| total output | **~16,000 to 18,000 t** by extrapolation **[rough; method unpublished in the sources found]** |
| tools | red-deer antler picks (hundreds per shaft), chalk lamps, bone shovels; antler is a **joint product** of the herd and the hunt |

A shaft was a crew's work for a season (removing hundreds of tonnes of chalk with antler picks) **[rough]**, so the mine was a
**threshold** work: one group, organised and fed, investing labour months ahead of any return. Recent dating (Healy et al. 2018)
places the main phase late in the Neolithic, when polished flint axes were already partly status goods. Krzemionki's thousands
of shafts and Spiennes show the same pattern on the continent: **the first industrial sites in Europe were mines of a good that
had substitutes (local flint) but not equivalents (large, flawless nodules)**.

### 3.3 Obsidian and how networks are read

Colin Renfrew, J. E. Dixon and J. R. Cann (1964, 1968) traced Near Eastern obsidian to source by its trace elements and found a
pattern that became the standard model of early trade:
- a **supply zone** near a source (roughly 300 km), where obsidian makes up most of the chipped stone **[rough: Renfrew's
  figure as usually quoted]**;
- a **contact zone** beyond, where the share falls off **exponentially with distance**;
- read as **down-the-line exchange**: each community keeps some and passes the rest to the next, so no one travels the whole
  route.

Later work complicates it: some southern Levantine sites 700 to 800 km away have more obsidian than down-the-line exchange
could deliver, implying **directional trade** (people travelling to sources or between distant partners) as well. The sources
also shift over time (central Anatolian in the early Neolithic, eastern Anatolian in the Pottery Neolithic). Tell Mozan in Syria
took 97% of its obsidian from eastern Anatolia (Frahm 2014).

**Three kinds of network, by how goods move:**

| Kind | How goods move | Signature in the ground | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **down-the-line** | passed hand to hand between neighbours | smooth exponential fall-off | most Neolithic obsidian |
| **directional** | carried by specialists along a route to a central place | peaks far from source | Anatolian obsidian at some Levantine sites; Uruk colonies (§5.1) |
| **prestige-good chains** | passed between leaders as gifts; held, not used | rich graves and hoards far away | jade axes, amber, Spondylus |

### 3.4 Salt

Salt is a chokepoint because humans and herbivores need sodium and because it is the main means of keeping meat and fish (and
later cheese). Grain-fed farmers eat less salt in meat than hunters, so farmers and herders need added salt more than foragers
do **[firm in outline]**. At **Provadia-Solnitsata**, brine from a spring was boiled in ceramic vessels in domed kilns (the
pottery called **briquetage**); production rose from ~25 kg of dry salt a load around 5500 BC to perhaps **4 to 5 t** a cycle by
4700 to 4500 BC **[rough; reports differ]**, and the site grew a stone wall (the earliest walled town in Europe by its
excavator's reading). Fifty kilometres away lies the Varna cemetery, the richest gold-bearing burial ground of its age (§4.1):
**salt wealth and metal wealth in one region**, on the Black Sea coast. Salt-making burns fuel heavily (boiling brine), so it
joins the fuel chain; it also needs pottery, so it joins the clay chain.

(Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society) for Grimes Graves; English Heritage, History of Grime's Graves; Nikolov 2021 and BTA
2025 for Provadia; Pétrequin et al. 2012.

## 4. The first metals

### 4.1 From native copper to smelting

| Step | When and where | What it took |
|---|---|---|
| **cold-hammered native copper** and malachite beads | Çayönü and other Anatolian sites, ~8th to 9th millennium BC; Old Copper Complex around Lake Superior, ~4000 to 1000 BC (never followed by smelting) | native metal (rare), a hammerstone; annealing (heating to soften) |
| **smelting copper from ore** | **Belovode, Serbia, ~5000 BC** (49th century BC; ~5 g of slag, the earliest secure smelting known) (Radivojević et al. 2010) **[firm]**; Tal-i Iblis (Iran), 5th millennium BC | ore (malachite, azurite), charcoal, a closed hearth or furnace reaching ~1,100 to 1,200 °C, blowpipes |
| **copper mines** | Rudna Glava (Serbia), ~5400 BC; Ai Bunar (Bulgaria), ~5000 BC, with open-cast trenches tens of metres long | stone mauls, antler, fire-setting |
| **a metal-rich society** | Varna cemetery, Bulgaria, ~4600 to 4300 BC: ~3,000 gold objects, ~6 kg of gold in all, most in a few graves **[rough]** | metal as rank: the first sharp inequality in Europe's graves |
| **lost-wax casting, arsenical copper** | Nahal Mishmar hoard, Judaean desert, ~3700 BC: over 400 objects, many arsenical copper, cast by the lost-wax method | wax, clay moulds, controlled pouring |
| **the Iceman's axe** | ~3300 BC, Alps: 99.7% copper, smelted from Tuscan ore (Artioli et al. 2017) | a trade link of several hundred km |

**Arsenical copper** (copper with ~2 to 5% arsenic, from smelting mixed ores, perhaps first by accident) is harder than pure
copper and casts better. It dominated the 4th and early 3rd millennia across the Near East and Europe. Its fumes are toxic,
which has been offered as one origin of the lame smith-god (Hephaistos) **[speculative]**.

### 4.2 Bronze and the tin trade

Tin bronze (copper with ~10% tin) is harder than arsenical copper, casts well, and can be made to a recipe. Tin is the
problem: **tin and copper deposits rarely lie near each other**, and tin is scarce in the whole of the Near East.

| Tin source | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| central Anatolia (**Kestel** mine and **Göltepe** smelting village, Taurus) | small, low-grade, worked in the 3rd millennium BC (Yener) | **[contested]** scale; probably local |
| Central Asia (**Mushiston**, Tajikistan; **Karnab**, Uzbekistan) | Bronze Age tin mines; some Uluburun tin isotopically matched here (Powell et al. 2022 and later studies) | **[firm for mining; share of trade rough]** |
| Afghanistan and eastern Iran (**Deh Hosein**, western Iran; Herat region) | Bronze Age workings; the likely source of the Old Assyrian tin, which came to Assur from the east | **[rough]** |
| **Erzgebirge** (Saxony and Bohemia) | tin isotopes point to its Central, then Western, plutons for Central European bronzes | **[firm in outline]** |
| **Cornwall and Devon** | tin ingots from shipwrecks off Israel (Hishuley Carmel) and southern France matched to south-west Britain (Williams et al. 2025, Antiquity); it is the largest tin province in Europe | **[contested for the Mediterranean, but growing]** |
| Brittany, Iberia (Galicia, northern Portugal) | placer tin; Atlantic routes | **[rough]** |
| Southeast Asia (Thailand, Yunnan) | Ban Chiang and Non Nok Tha bronze from the 2nd millennium BC | **[firm]** |

**When bronze took over.** Tin bronze appears in Mesopotamia in the early 3rd millennium BC (the Royal Cemetery of Ur, ~2600 BC,
has much); full tin bronze reached central Europe and Scandinavia by ~1800 BC, and the Aegean and Egypt fully by ~1500 BC
(from Williams et al. 2025's summary) **[firm in outline]**.

**The Old Assyrian trade (~1950 to 1750 BC).** The best-documented trade of the ancient world, from ~23,000 letters and
contracts found in the merchants' quarter (*kārum*) of **Kanesh** (Kültepe) in central Anatolia.
- **Route:** Assur on the Tigris to Kanesh, ~1,000 km over the Jezirah and Taurus, by **black donkey caravans**, about six weeks.
- **Goods out:** **tin** (bought in Assur from eastern traders) and **woollen textiles** (some woven by Assyrian merchants' wives
  in Assur, some bought from Babylonia). **Goods back:** **silver and gold**, which were money.
- **Loads:** a donkey carried around 130 minas (~65 kg) of tin with about four textiles packed round it, or ~25 to 30 textiles
  **[rough]**.
- **Volume:** a working estimate of **~1,000 donkey loads a year** (one third tin, two thirds textiles) in the busiest
  decades; estimates range from a few hundred to several thousand (the Leiden study of the "golden interval"); **~100,000
  textiles over thirty years** in one sample-based estimate **[rough; contested]**.
- **Profit:** typical mark-ups of roughly 100% on tin and more on textiles at Kanesh before costs and taxes **[rough]**.
- **Institutions:** the ***naruqqum*** ("bag"): a long-term investment partnership of some 15 or more investors putting gold into
  a merchant's fund for ten or twelve years, with the merchant taking a share of profits; family firms with agents in Anatolia;
  a merchants' court at the *kārum*; **treaties** with local Anatolian rulers fixing taxes (a share of textiles taken at entry, a
  right of first purchase) and guaranteeing protection for caravans. It is an economy of **long-distance credit, partnerships and
  merchant law in the early 2nd millennium BC** (Larsen 2015, Veenhof).

**The Uluburun ship (~1320 BC).** Wrecked off south-west Turkey; its cargo is a snapshot of Late Bronze Age exchange (Pulak 2008):
- **~10 t of copper**, as about 350 "oxhide" ingots (counts of 348 and 354 are given) plus over 100 bun ingots, from **Cyprus** by
  lead isotopes;
- **~1 t of tin**: the 10:1 ratio of bronze, as if the ship carried a bronze recipe;
- ~175 ingots of blue and other coloured **glass** (the earliest intact glass ingots);
- ebony, ivory, terebinth resin in Canaanite jars, ostrich eggs, Baltic amber, a Mycenaean, Canaanite, Cypriot, Egyptian and
  Mesopotamian mix of goods; a gold scarab of Nefertiti.

**Metalworking specialists.** Smelting happened near the ore and fuel; casting and finishing near the buyer. Smiths are often
the first full-time specialists in an archaeological record: hoards of scrap metal for recycling, moulds, and the "founder's
hoards" of the European Bronze Age suggest itinerant smiths **[contested: itinerant or attached to elites]**. Recycling mattered:
bronze could be melted and recast, so metal circulated as a stock.

### 4.3 The Bronze Age palace economies (Linear B)

The Mycenaean palaces of Greece and Crete (Knossos ~1400 BC; Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes to ~1200 BC) kept accounts on clay tablets
in **Linear B** (an early Greek, deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952). The tablets survive only because the palaces burned,
baking them; they cover the last months of each palace.

| Chain | What the tablets show | Figure |
|---|---|---|
| **wool** | Knossos tracks flocks by place and shepherd, with a target of wool per flock and the "deficit" owed | **at least ~80,000 sheep**, perhaps ~100,000, mostly castrated males kept for wool **[rough; Killen]**; a wool unit of about 3 kg, ~4 sheep per unit **[rough]** |
| **textiles** | women workers in named groups (by place or by origin, some "captives" from the east Aegean), receiving monthly rations of wheat and figs; targets for cloth | Pylos lists ~750 women with ~1,000 children **[rough]** |
| **bronze** | the Jn series lists **bronzesmiths** in some 25 places, each group with or without an allocation (*ta-ra-si-ja*) of metal to work up | ~270 smiths **[rough]**; allocations per group ~0.75 to ~4 kg on single tablets; many smiths listed "without allocation", a sign of metal shortage |
| **the ta-ra-si-ja system** | the palace issues raw material and expects finished goods back, the smith working at home; the same for textiles | it is a **putting-out** system 3,000 years before proto-industry (02zzi §3.6) |
| **perfumed oil, chariots, furniture, feasts** | specific luxury and military chains | the palace records what it controls, not the whole economy |
| **land** | Pylos E-series: landholding by "damos" (community) and by individuals in return for service | |

**What the palaces were.** They were **selective redistributors**: they tracked a few high-value chains (wool and cloth, bronze,
perfumed oil, chariots, feasting goods) closely and left most farming and exchange to villages that appear only when they owe
the palace something (Halstead 1992; Nakassis). They depended on imports (copper and tin by sea; ivory, gold, glass) and on
labour gathered as dependants. Around **1200 to 1150 BC** the whole eastern Mediterranean system (Mycenaean palaces, the Hittite
state, Ugarit) collapsed within a few decades, and Greece lost writing for four centuries. The causes are debated (drought,
earthquakes, raiders, revolt, the breaking of trade routes), but one reading fits the supply chains: **an economy built on long,
interlocking chokepoints (tin, copper, grain shipments) is brittle when several fail at once** (Cline 2014) **[contested
causes]**.

the Iceman's axe; Larsen 2015 (Ancient Kanesh); Veenhof, The Archives of Old Assyrian Traders; Barjamovic and others, The golden
interval of Old Assyrian trade (Leiden); Pulak 2008 (Beyond Babylon); Williams et al. 2025 (Antiquity); Powell et al. on

## 5. The first cities and states

### 5.1 Uruk and Mesopotamia

**The setting.** Southern Mesopotamia is flat alluvium between the Tigris and Euphrates: rich soil, too little rain to farm
without irrigation (under ~200 mm a year), and almost nothing else: **no stone, no metal, no good timber**. Rivers that flood at
the wrong time (spring, before harvest) and shift course. Marshes rich in fish, reeds and birds. Date palms. Sheep and goats on
the steppe edges.

**The sequence.**

| Phase | When | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Ubaid | ~6500 to 4000 BC | villages and small towns with temples; irrigation by small canals off river levees |
| **Uruk** | ~4000 to 3100 BC | Uruk grows to ~250 ha by the late 4th millennium and perhaps **~600 ha with 40,000 to 50,000 people by ~2900 BC** **[rough; contested]**; the Eanna temple precinct; **writing**; colonies upriver (Habuba Kabira) |
| Early Dynastic | ~2900 to 2350 BC | city-states (Ur, Lagash, Umma, Kish) with temple and palace households, war over canals and fields |
| Akkad | ~2350 to 2150 BC | the first territorial empire |
| **Ur III** | ~2112 to 2004 BC | a centralised state with the densest administrative record of the ancient world (~100,000 tablets) |

**Temple and palace households.** The economy's core institutions were great households (*e2*, "house"): a temple or palace that
owned land, herds and workshops, employed dependants, and fed them in **rations** (Gelb's "ration system"). Many people also
farmed their own or family land; the great households were not the whole economy, but they are almost all the records.

**Rations** (Ur III, from granary records at Umma and elsewhere) **[firm]**:

| Worker | Barley a month |
|---|---|
| adult man (*guruš*) | **60 sila** (~60 litres; ~1.8 to 2 kg a day) |
| adult woman | **30 sila**, sometimes 40 to 60 for valued work |
| children | 10 to 25 sila |
| also | oil (a few sila a month), wool (a few minas a year), occasionally beer, fish, dates |

At ~3,300 kcal per kg of barley, 60 litres (~38 to 40 kg) a month is ~4,000 kcal a day: enough for a worker and some to share
or trade **[rough arithmetic]**.

**Bevelled-rim bowls.** Crude, mould-made bowls of a near-standard size, found by the hundreds of thousands at Uruk-period sites
from Iran to Syria, often discarded after one use. The usual reading: they held a daily ration of grain or bread, mass-produced
for workers **[contested: also read as bread moulds or for yogurt]**. If so, they are the archaeological sign of **labour paid
in standard measures by an institution**.

**Writing as an accounting tool.**
- **Tokens** of clay in simple shapes (cones, spheres, discs) from ~8000 BC, then sealed inside clay envelopes (*bullae*) in the
  4th millennium BC, then impressed on the outside: Denise Schmandt-Besserat's theory that writing grew from counting tokens
  **[contested in its strong form; the bullae and numerical tablets are firm]**.
- **Numerical tablets** (~3500 BC): impressed numbers and a seal.
- **Proto-cuneiform** (Uruk IV, ~3350 to 3200 BC): ~5,000 to 6,000 archaic tablets from Uruk, the great majority
  **administrative** (allocations of grain, beer, bread, sheep and workers; field areas; labour), the rest lexical lists for
  training scribes (Nissen, Damerow and Englund 1993, Archaic Bookkeeping) **[firm in outline; share rough, often quoted as
  ~85 to 90%]**.
- **Different number systems for different goods**: one for discrete objects (sexagesimal), another for grain rations
  (bisexagesimal), another for capacities of grain, another for areas, another for time. **Counting was tied to the commodity**
  before it was abstract.
- Writing then let institutions do new things: plan a year's rations against a harvest, track debts and deficits over years,
  hold a worker to a quota, audit an official. It was **an information good that made the redistributive household possible at
  the scale of thousands**.

**Textiles: the export industry.** Wool from temple and palace flocks, spun and woven by dependent women. In the province of
Lagash in the 48th year of Šulgi (Ur III), one text records **more than 10,000 people working in the weaving establishments,
about two-thirds of them women weavers** (Waetzoldt; secondary summary) **[rough]**. Guabba, a coastal town of Lagash, supplied
the largest group (one summary gives ~4,300 women and ~1,800 children **[unverified]**). Wool cloth was Mesopotamia's main export,
traded for what the plain lacked.

**Irrigation and yields.**
- Canals were dug and cleaned by *corvée* labour gangs; disputes over canals and fields between Lagash and Umma lasted for
  generations (the "Stele of the Vultures", ~2450 BC).
- Early Dynastic and Ur III field texts have been read as giving very high barley yields (seed-to-yield ratios of 20:1 and more,
  even 70:1 in Girsu texts by one reading) **[contested readings]**; at Larsa around 1700 BC yields averaged roughly **900 litres
  per hectare** (Jacobsen and Adams 1958; Jacobsen 1982).
- **Salinisation:** Jacobsen and Adams argued that irrigation without drainage raised the water table and salted the fields,
  driving a shift from wheat to salt-tolerant barley and a fall in yields; **Powell (1985)** disputed the textual readings and
  argued barley was preferred for its own merits and that leaching and fallow controlled salt **[contested]**.
- The Malthusian edge appears here as an environmental one: **intensification can degrade its own base**.

**Imports and routes.**

| Lacked | Got from | Route |
|---|---|---|
| timber (cedar, pine, hardwoods) | Lebanon, Amanus, the Zagros | river rafts downstream |
| copper | **Magan** (Oman) by the Gulf, ~3rd millennium; Anatolia | sea; Euphrates |
| tin | the east (Iran, Afghanistan, Central Asia) | overland; Elam |
| stone (diorite, limestone, flint) | Magan, the Zagros | sea, rivers |
| lapis lazuli, carnelian, gold, ivory | Badakhshan; **Meluhha** (the Indus); **Dilmun** (Bahrain) as entrepôt | the Gulf |

**The Uruk expansion** (~3500 to 3100 BC): Uruk-style settlements planted far upriver in Syria and Anatolia (Habuba Kabira,
Jebel Aruda, Hacınebi), read by Guillermo Algaze as a **world system** where the alluvium's cities traded cloth and manufactures
for the periphery's metal, timber and stone through colonies at key points on the routes **[contested as a model; the colonies
are firm]**.

### 5.2 Egypt: the Nile and redistribution

**The setting.** A narrow strip of floodplain in a desert, a river that flooded **every year at a predictable time** (late
summer, from Ethiopian monsoon rains) and left new silt. **Basin irrigation:** the flood filled earth-banked basins, soaked the
soil and drained; one winter crop (emmer, barley, flax) was sown in the mud. Little canal-cleaning or drainage needed; no
salinisation to speak of. The risk was **a low or a too-high flood**, which the state measured with **nilometers** and used to
assess taxes.

**Scale.** Old Kingdom Egypt (~2686 to 2181 BC) had perhaps **1 to 1.5 million people** (Butzer 1976) **[rough]**, almost all
farmers, along ~1,000 km of river: **the river was the road**, so grain could be moved by boat cheaply from any field to any
granary.

**Redistribution.** Taxes in grain, flax and animals flowed to royal and temple granaries; out of them came **rations of bread
and beer** to workers, officials and priests. Workers on royal projects were paid in rations:

| Case | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| **Giza pyramid builders** (~2550 BC) | a workers' town (Heit el-Ghurab) with bakeries, breweries and fish-processing; a workforce of perhaps **8,000 to 10,000** at a time (Lehner and Hawass) **[rough]**; one estimate of food needed **per ten days** for 10,000 workers: ~3,800 kg barley, ~3,900 kg emmer, 10 cattle, 36 sheep and goats, ~1,700 kg fish (AERA and Murray) **[rough]**; cattle bones from estates in the Delta show meat was sent in |
| **Merer's diary** (Wadi al-Jarf papyri, ~2560 BC) | the logbook of an inspector leading a crew of ~40 men who carried limestone by boat from the Tura quarries to Giza along canals during the flood: **the oldest papyrus known, and a work log** |
| **Deir el-Medina** (New Kingdom, ~1550 to 1070 BC) | the village of the royal tomb builders; a workman's monthly wage of **4 khar of emmer and 1.5 khar of barley** (one khar ~76 litres, so ~420 litres a month: far more than one man eats, enough for a household and for trading surplus) **[firm for the khar counts; litre conversion rough]**; plus fish, vegetables, oil, clothing |
| **the first recorded strike** | year 29 of Ramesses III (~1157 BC): the Deir el-Medina crew stopped work and sat in at temples when rations were weeks late (the Turin Strike Papyrus) **[firm]** | a redistributive state that fails to deliver rations meets organised labour |

**Exchange outside the state.** Village markets and barter existed throughout, with values reckoned in units of account: in the
New Kingdom the **deben** (~91 g of copper) and the **sack** of grain priced goods that were then paid in a mix of other goods (a
bull "worth 120 deben" paid in cloth, oil and grain) **[firm]**. Money as a **measure of value** came long before coin.

**Imports.** Cedar and pine from **Byblos** (Lebanon) by sea from the Old Kingdom on; **copper** and turquoise from **Sinai**
(Wadi Maghara, Serabit el-Khadim, by expeditions); **gold** from Nubia and the Eastern Desert; incense, ebony and exotic animals
from **Punt** (the southern Red Sea) by ship; later tin and silver through the Levant. Royal expeditions, not merchants, dominate
the Old Kingdom record: **trade as a state activity**.

### 5.3 The Indus civilisation

**The setting and scale.** The Mature Harappan phase (~2600 to 1900 BC) along the Indus and the now-dry Ghaggar-Hakra, over
~800,000 km²: the largest of the first civilisations in area. Over 1,000 known sites; cities of **Mohenjo-daro** (~200 to 250 ha,
perhaps 40,000 people **[rough]**), Harappa, Ganweriwala, Rakhigarhi, Dholavira. Grids of streets, covered drains, wells,
standardised baked bricks in a **1:2:4** ratio. Crops: wheat and barley in winter, with millets and rice in summer in places
(two seasons); cotton; zebu cattle.

**Weights and measures.** The best-standardised system of the Bronze Age:
- cubes of banded grey **chert**, cut and polished, found at sites hundreds of kilometres apart with very small variation;
- a **binary** series for small weights: **1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64**, the smallest ~0.856 g and **the commonest, the "16", ~13.7 g**;
- then **decimal** multiples for large weights (160, 200, 320, 640 ... up to about 100 times the 13.7 g weight) **[firm]**;
- shell and ivory scales for length.

Used, on Kenoyer's reading, for trade, for taxing goods entering cities, and for allocating craft materials **[rough reading]**.

**Craft production.**

| Craft | What it involved | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **long carnelian beads** | bead blanks heated to redden, then drilled end to end with a special hard-stone drill ("Ernestite") over many days: one long bead could take **a week or more** of work **[rough; Kenoyer's experiments]** | Chanhudaro, Harappa, Dholavira; exported to Mesopotamia |
| **shell bangles** | *Turbinella pyrum* conch from the Gujarat and Makran coasts, sawn into rings | Nageshwar, Balakot; worn widely |
| **stoneware bangles** | a high-fired, controlled ceramic, perhaps a state monopoly | Harappa, Mohenjo-daro |
| **steatite seals** | carved, fired; animal motifs and short inscriptions in the undeciphered Indus script; used to seal goods (sealings on clay show cord and packages) | everywhere |
| **faience** | glazed paste | |
| **copper and bronze** | copper from Rajasthan (Khetri) and Oman; tin rare (much Indus metal is copper or arsenical) | |
| **cotton cloth** | earliest cotton textiles known | |

**Trade.** The Indus is probably the **Meluhha** of Mesopotamian texts, which sent carnelian, ivory, timber, gold and exotic
animals to Akkad and Ur; Meluhhan traders lived in Mesopotamia (a "Meluhhan village" at Guabba). **Lothal** in Gujarat has a
large brick basin read as a dock **[contested]**; **Shortugai** on the Amu Darya in north Afghanistan is a Harappan outpost near
lapis and tin. **Dilmun** (Bahrain) used weights on the Indus standard.

**What is different.** No palaces, royal tombs or monumental depictions of rulers have been found; little evidence of large-scale
warfare. The Indus suggests that **standardisation, planning and long-distance trade could be achieved without an obvious
redistributive king** **[firm observation; its meaning is contested]**: perhaps merchant elites, councils, or a priesthood.
Decline after ~1900 BC is linked to shifts in monsoon and rivers (the drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra), and to the fading of the
Mesopotamian trade **[contested]**.

|---|---|---|---|---|


## 7. How supply chains formed and joined: the pattern

1. **Chains began as one person's work and one step.** An Oldowan flake: pick up a cobble, strike it, cut meat. The first
   chains were **shallow**, local and open to anyone.
2. **They grew longer by assembly.** The hafted spear needed three or four chains (stone, wood, glue, cord); the bow five. Each
   new assembly made one chain's product an input to another, and the joints (cord, glue, fire) became hubs.
3. **Fire was the first general-purpose hub.** Almost every later chain passes through a hearth or a kiln: cooking, heat
   treatment, glue, smoking, plaster, pottery, salt, metal.
4. **Storage turned pulses into time capital.** Once a glut could be kept (nuts, salmon, grain), people stayed put, owned stores,
   and ranked themselves. Farming came after storage, not before.
5. **Farming and herding added land and animals as capital.** The field and the herd are **time capital**: work now, eat
   later. Herds became **joint-product** machines (milk, wool, traction, dung, hides), and the plough and cart **increasing-returns
   thresholds** that rewarded those with land and teams.
6. **Chokepoints made exchange necessary, and exchange came before markets.** Obsidian, good flint, salt, shell, jade, amber and
   later copper and tin were patchy. They moved first through gift partnerships and down-the-line passing, then through
   directional trade by specialists.
7. **Metal made chokepoints into the structure of whole societies.** Bronze needs two rare ores from different places, so
   every bronze-using economy depended on long routes, pack animals or ships, credit and treaties. Control of routes became
   power; the breaking of routes ended a civilisation.
8. **Cities were redistributive machines with export industries.** Their core was grain and wool turned into rations and cloth by
   dependent labour, with cloth traded for what the plain lacked. Writing, weights and units of account were the information
   goods that held the machine together.
9. **Different settings made different institutions.** A river that floods predictably (Egypt) gave a single state and
   expeditions; an unpredictable one with salt (Mesopotamia) gave city-states, canal wars and records of debts; a vast, varied
   plain with two seasons (the Indus) gave standardisation without visible kings; an island and peninsula world dependent on sea
   imports (the Aegean) gave selective palaces that tracked a few chains closely.

## 8. What a simulator could take from this

Possibilities to think with, in the folder's spirit; not a build list.

1. **A return-rate ladder from the data.** Wild resources ranked by post-encounter return over two orders of magnitude (big game
   ~15,000 to 30,000 kcal/hour, small game ~5,000 to 15,000, fish and fruit ~2,000 to 5,000, nuts ~1,000 to 1,500, seeds ~300 to
   1,300, roots ~100 to 300), with search time growing as the top ranks are depleted. Agents' choices then show diet breadth
   widening under pressure, which is the broad spectrum revolution and the road to farming.
2. **Processing as the real cost of low-ranked food.** Seeds and acorns are cheap to gather and dear to make edible. Tools that
   cut processing time (grinding stones, pots for boiling, the quern, the mill) are what move a food up the ranking. This fits the
   engine's grain-to-bread chain (economy_web.md §2.1) and pushes the switch point earlier, to the quern and the pot.
3. **Storage before farming, and storage as the source of property.** A world where a pulse (a fish run, a nut fall, wild grain)
   can be stored lets agents discover sedentism and ownership before they farm. The measured outcome to look for is Woodburn's
   switch from immediate-return sharing to delayed-return property.
4. **Sharing as insurance with rules.** High-variance foods (big kills) pooled across the band, low-variance foods kept by the
   household; demand sharing and tolerated theft as behaviours, not engine rules; hxaro-like partnerships with households in
   other places as the safety net in a bad year. Watch whether sharing fades once food becomes storable and predictable, as it did.
5. **Farming that pays less per hour but more per hectare.** Bowles's 63% is a useful calibration: early cultivation should not
   beat good foraging per hour of labour. It should win on density, storability and defendability, and should cost health
   (more disease, shorter stature) while raising births. If farming in a world pays better per hour from day one, the transition
   is too easy to be interesting.
6. **Herds as joint products over a lifetime.** A beast kept alive yields milk, wool, traction, dung and hides; killed, it yields
   meat once. The choice between them is a time-capital choice. Wool and cloth are the most labour-dense goods (hundreds of hours
   of spinning per garment), which is exactly why they make good stores of value and exports.
7. **The pyrotechnology ladder as one chain.** Hearth, heat treatment, lime, pottery kiln, smelting furnace, each needing more
   fuel, higher temperatures and better draught control. A world could let metal smelting be reachable only by people who already
   run kilns and burn charcoal, so the metal chain grows out of the fuel and pottery chains rather than appearing whole.
8. **Two-ore chokepoints.** Bronze needs copper and tin that are never in the same place; tin is rarer. A region where one ore is
   a day's walk and the other a week's makes the whole economy depend on a route, carriers, credit and safe passage: Kanesh's
   donkeys, the naruqqum and the treaties are a template for what agents would have to invent.
9. **Exchange without markets.** Down-the-line passing (an exponential fall-off with distance), directional trade by specialists,
   and prestige-gift chains between leaders leave different traces. A simulator could read which kind its agents produce from the
   distribution of a good by distance from its source: Renfrew's method applied to a run.
10. **Mines and drives as the first threshold works.** A flint mine or a game drive needs a crew fed for weeks before any return:
    the first case where a group must pool surplus and accept direction. They are smaller, earlier versions of the mill and the
    weir.
11. **Rations as the first wage, and writing as the first information good.** A great household that holds the granary, pays
    workers in standard measures, and keeps records of quotas and deficits is the Mesopotamian and Aegean model of
    redistribution (02zzi §3.3). Records that let an institution plan, audit and hold people to account are what made it scale
    past a few hundred people. A late ration (Deir el-Medina's strike) is a ready-made test of whether labour organises.
12. **Settings that select institutions.** Predictable flood and cheap river transport (Egypt), unpredictable flood and salt
    (Mesopotamia), two seasons and wide trade (the Indus), sea imports to a hill country (the Aegean): the same goods under
    different physical constraints gave different institutions. Varying the land and the water between worlds, rather than the
    rules, is the experiment history ran.
13. **Brittleness of interlocking chokepoints.** A system that depends on several long routes at once (tin, copper, grain) can
    fail together (~1200 BC). Shocks to several routes at once are a test of how deep an economy can grow and how fast it falls
    back.
14. **Units of account before coin.** Egypt's deben of copper and Mesopotamia's shekel of silver and measure of barley priced
    goods that were paid in other goods. Prices can exist, and be compared, without money changing hands; the engine's coin is
    one of several ways to get there.
