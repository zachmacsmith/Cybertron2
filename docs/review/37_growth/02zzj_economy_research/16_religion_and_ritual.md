## Summary

Through most of history the largest holder of stores, land, records and trust after the household was a ritual institution: the
feasting lineage, the temple, the sanctuary, the monastery, the parish, the waqf. The reasons are economic. A sacred place is a
**secure store** (theft from it is sacrilege, and the god does not die or flee), a **central place** people visit on a calendar,
a body with **literate staff and long memory**, and an owner that outlives its members and so can hold land, lend and keep
promises across generations. Feasts were the first redistribution and the first politics: they moved food from good years to bad,
mobilised labour (the work feast) and turned surplus into obligation and rank. Temples in Sumer, Egypt, Greece, South India and
Tang China were landlords, granaries, workshops, record offices and lenders; writing began in a temple household's accounts (part
10), and Apollo's temple on Delos lent to cities on written contracts with sureties.

In medieval Europe the church took a tenth of the harvest in the field, owned perhaps a fifth to a quarter of England's land and
over two-fifths of its powered mills, and set the year's rhythm: Sundays and forty to fifty holy days, fasts that made salt fish a
continental trade, fairs on saints' days at shrines, quarter days on which rents and debts fell due. Monasteries, above all the
Cistercians with their granges, ran some of Europe's most technical estates: export wool, iron forges, mills on diverted rivers,
drained marsh. Their dissolutions (Tang China 845, England 1536 to 1541, France 1789, Spain 1836) were each among the largest
land transfers in the country's history. The ban on usury shaped lending rather than stopping it: bills of exchange, rent charges,
partnerships, the pawn bank, the licensed Jewish lender, the Islamic credit sale, the prozbul. Endowments locked capital into
purposes for centuries. Religious orders and minorities (Templars, Jesuits, Quakers, Armenians, Jains) were trust networks, and
Weber's thesis is still argued in data: Becker and Woessmann find Protestant Prussia richer through literacy, Cantoni finds no
effect on city growth.

For the border (02zzk), what bears on the village is small and concrete: days of rest and feast in the calendar, a feast where food
is given out, a church as a building a group holds, the tithe owed to that group's store, and alms from it in dearth. All but the
first need the social primitives of OPEN L-106 (part 09 §3: P1, P5, P7). Everything above the parish stays map only.

## 1. Feasting and ritual among foragers and early farmers

### 1.1 Feasts as economics

Anthropologists and archaeologists now treat the feast as one of the oldest economic institutions, alongside sharing and gift
(part 04 §1; part 06 §2.3, §3.6). Brian Hayden's argument (1990s to 2014) is the strongest version: once some places yielded a
storable surplus, ambitious people ("aggrandisers") used feasts to turn it into debts, alliances, marriage partners and labour,
and the drive to produce food for feasts pushed domestication itself (*contested*: critics find feasting a consequence, not a
cause; part 06 §4.1 lists it among five explanations of the Neolithic). Michael Dietler's typology is the most used:

| Kind of feast (Dietler 1996, 2001) | Who gives, who comes | What it does economically |
|---|---|---|
| **empowering** | rivals compete in giving | converts surplus into prestige and debts; ranks givers |
| **patron-role** | a centre gives to dependants, without return expected | legitimates a standing inequality; redistribution (chiefs, temples, lords) |
| **diacritical** | an elite feasts apart, with special foods and vessels | marks a class; creates demand for luxuries (wine, spices, fine pottery) |
| **work feast** | a household feeds those who come to work for a day | mobilises labour beyond the household without wages |

**The work feast** is the most widespread: the host brews beer and cooks, neighbours clear a field or bring in a harvest, and the
meal is the payment (Dietler and Herbich 1998, on the Luo of Kenya; *unchecked*). It is part 05's reciprocal work party with the
beer made explicit. The Andean *minka* used maize beer (chicha); the Inca state scaled it into the mit'a, feeding and giving chicha
to households working state and temple fields (part 11 §6). English **boon days** were the same under a lord: tenants reaped the
demesne and were fed bread and ale, the quantity sometimes written in the custumal (*memory*, Bennett). The cost of a work feast
is grain and fuel turned into ale and bread before the day, which is why brewing and the feast travel together.

**Signalling and insurance.** Men on Mer (Torres Strait) who hunted turtle for public feasts gained reputation and mates rather
than calories (Bliege Bird and Smith; *memory*). Suttles read the potlatch as spreading food between villages whose salmon runs
failed in different years (part 06 §3.6); the moka stored a good year's pigs as claims on others' pigs; the !Kung *hxaro* (part
04) did the same with gifts. The store is a set of obligations held in memory, kept alive by repeated ritual giving.

### 1.2 Göbekli Tepe and monumental feasting

Part 10 (§2) lists **Göbekli Tepe** (south-east Turkey, about 9600 to 8200 BC) as monumental pillars raised by people still
largely foraging, with large-scale feasting (wild cereal beer *contested*). This session's search confirms the outline and the
revision:
- The earliest exposed enclosures date to about **9500 to 9000 BC**, the later layers to the Early Pre-Pottery Neolithic B, about
  8700 to 8200 BC (*checked*, secondary summaries of the excavators' radiocarbon work).
- Klaus Schmidt read the site as a sanctuary without houses, built and periodically buried amid great feasts of gazelle and
  aurochs, with beer from wild grain (*checked* as his reading).
- Since about 2019 the excavators (Lee Clare and colleagues) have found middens, hearths, cisterns and channels for rain water and
  many grinding tools, and now call the large enclosures "special purpose buildings" in a settlement rather than a temple on an
  empty hill (*checked*, secondary). Laura Dietrich's work on the grinding stones points to cereal processing on a large scale,
  porridge and possibly beer (*contested*: the oxalate tests in the limestone vats are not conclusive).

Either way, raising pillars of several tonnes needed labour beyond a band, gathered most likely with food and drink at the site:
a **surplus pooled through ritual** before full domestication. Later cases: Durrington Walls by Stonehenge, where pig bones from
as far as Scotland mark midwinter feasts drawing people across Britain (Madgwick and others 2019; *memory*), and Poverty Point in
Louisiana (about 1700 to 1100 BC), earthworks raised by foragers.

### 1.3 Destruction and deposit

- **Destruction as a sink.** Potlatch givers burned oil or broke coppers: the most credible signal of wealth, and a way to keep
  valuables scarce. Canada's ban (1885 to 1951, part 06) treated it as labour wasted that should go to wage work (*unchecked*).
- **Hoards.** Bronze Age Europeans deposited axes, swords and rings in rivers and bogs and never took them back: offerings, stores
  or a way to keep metal scarce (Bradley, *The Passage of Arms*; *contested*). Either way it was demand for metal outside use, like
  Varna's grave gold (part 10).
- **Feasts and inequality.** Feasting is what surplus does before there is a tax collector (part 06 §3.6), and what elites do after:
  Roman *evergetism* (the rich paying for games and buildings), lords' Christmas feasts for tenants.

### 1.4 Feasting: what it needed, what it enabled, how it interacts

| Element | Needed | Enabled | Interacts with production and exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| work feast | a surplus of grain or meat; brewing; a calendar of tasks | labour beyond the household without wages | turns grain into labour; brewing as the conversion step |
| competitive feast | a storable surplus; goods that carry status | rank, alliance, marriage; debts held in memory | pushes production above need; demand for luxury goods |
| patron feast | a centre with stores | legitimacy for tribute | the return leg of redistribution |
| ritual gathering at a site | a known place and date; a draw (a monument, a cult) | exchange of goods and partners at the gathering | the first fairs (§7) |
| destruction and deposit | surplus that can be spared | credible signals; scarcity of valuables | a sink in the metal and craft chains |

## 2. Temples as the first stores, banks, landlords and record-keepers

### 2.1 Mesopotamia

Parts 04 (§3.1), 06 (§2.5), 10 (§5.1) and 12 already describe the temple and palace "great households" of Uruk and Ur III: land,
herds, workshops of thousands of weavers, rations of barley, oil and wool, and writing invented to account for them. This part adds
how the temple's role changed over three thousand years.

- **Girsu (Lagash).** The household of the goddess Ba'u (about 2400 BC) is the classic "temple-state": its tablets record fields
  allotted to dependants, others farmed directly or let for a share, herds, fisheries and rations (*memory*). Deimel's view that the
  temple owned all the land was overturned (Diakonoff, Gelb): the temple was one great household among several (*contested* in
  degree; part 10 agrees).
- **Old Babylonian Sippar: the naditu.** Women dedicated to Shamash lived in a cloister but bought and leased fields, lent silver
  and barley at interest and hired labour, with contracts witnessed at the temple (Harris 1975; *memory*): a religious status that
  let a person invest capital (her dowry) outside the family's control. The temple of Shamash itself lent, sometimes to the poor at
  low interest, sometimes at the Hammurabi rates (part 04 §3.2), with the god as witness (*memory*).
- **Neo-Babylonian temples (626 to 484 BC).** The Eanna at Uruk and the Ebabbar at Sippar held date and barley estates worked by
  temple dependants (*širku*) and hired men, let estates to "rent farmers" for fixed amounts, and received a **tithe** (*ešrû*) of
  produce from people across the region and from the king (*memory*, Jursa): the oldest well-documented tithe to a temple.

### 2.2 Egypt

Part 10 (§5.2) gives Egypt's redistribution through royal and temple granaries. The temples' share grew over time.

- **Temples as landlords.** By the New Kingdom the great temples, Amun's at Karnak above all, held estates the length of the Nile,
  with herds, fleets, mines and dependent villages. **Papyrus Harris I** (about 1150 BC), Ramesses III's list of gifts to the
  temples, is the main source. One summary has the Theban temple administering about 80% of the temple land and 86% of the
  temples' livestock (*checked* as the summary's figure; the underlying count is from Breasted's translation and is argued). The
  older estimate that temples held about a third of Egypt's cultivated land is often quoted and disputed (*contested*: Janssen
  argued the Harris lists include royal land the temples only administered).
- **The Wilbour Papyrus** (year 4 of Ramesses V, about 1140 BC) is a register of fields in Middle Egypt with their holders and
  assessments, a land register kept for temple and crown dues (*checked* as a genuine official register). It shows the same field
  owing to a temple while farmed by soldiers, priests, women and herdsmen in small holdings: the temple was an owner of revenue
  rather than a farmer of everything.
- **The reversion of offerings.** Food offered to the god each day was taken back from the altar and shared out to priests and
  staff by fixed shares (*unchecked*): the offering list was the payroll. Priests served in rotating groups a month at a time and
  farmed or traded the rest of the year. The Deir el-Medina strikers (part 10) sat in at the temples, where the granaries were.

### 2.3 Greece: sanctuaries as treasuries and lenders

Greek sanctuaries collected **dedications** (a tenth, *dekate*, of war booty, a harvest or a profit, set up as a statue, tripod or
building), held the **treasuries** of cities, and some lent at interest.

- **Delos.** The temple of Apollo lent to cities of the Cyclades and to private persons from the fifth to the second century BC;
  the annual officials (*hieropoioi*) recorded each loan with principal, interest, security (usually houses or land), guarantors
  and witnesses, and carved the accounts on stone (*checked*). The usual rate was 10% a year (*unchecked*; Larsen, Reger). The
  Amphictyonic accounts of 377/6 to 374/3 BC list interest received from twelve or so island cities (*checked*, Rhodes and Osborne
  no. 28). Defaults were common, and the temple seized pledged houses: a lender with the same remedies as a private one, but with
  the god's name on the bond.
- **Athena's treasury.** Athens borrowed from the treasures of Athena and the "other gods" on the Acropolis during the
  Peloponnesian War; the accounts for 433 to 423 BC record several thousand talents (*memory*: about 5,600). The gold on Pheidias's
  statue of Athena was counted as removable reserve (Thucydides 2.13).
- **Delphi.** Cities kept their dedications in treasury buildings there (the Siphnian one paid for by a tithe of the island's mines,
  Herodotus 3.57). In the Third Sacred War (356 to 346 BC) the Phocians seized Delphi and melted its offerings to pay mercenaries
  (*contested*: about 10,000 talents is one estimate). The Artemision at Ephesus took deposits from kings and private persons
  (*memory*).

### 2.4 Rome and Jerusalem

- **Rome** kept its state treasury (the *aerarium*) in the temple of Saturn and its mint at the temple of **Juno Moneta**, whence
  "money" and "mint" (*unchecked*, standard).
- **The Jerusalem temple** took a half-shekel a year from every adult Jewish man of the diaspora, in Tyrian shekels for their
  fineness, hence the money changers in its courts; it also held private deposits (2 Maccabees 3) (*unchecked*). After AD 70 Rome
  diverted the half-shekel to Jupiter as the *fiscus Iudaicus*.
- **Sacrifice and meat.** Much of the meat eaten in Greek and Roman towns came from sacrifice, the surplus sold in the market (Paul
  discusses whether Christians may buy it) (*unchecked*): an abattoir with a calendar.

### 2.5 South India: temples as banks and employers

The great Chola temple at Thanjavur (the Rajarajesvara or Brihadisvara, completed 1010) carries inscriptions that read as a
balance sheet.

- **Loans to village assemblies.** Rajaraja I and his successors lent the temple's gold to village assemblies (*sabhas*) in the
  Kaveri delta, the interest due yearly to the temple treasury either in paddy, at three *kuruni* for each *kasu*, or in money at
  **12½% a year** (*checked*, the inscription as transcribed by IGNCA and the *South Indian Inscriptions*). The borrowed money was
  probably used to bring waste land under cultivation, and the principal seems never to have been returned: the loans were
  perpetual rents on the delta (*checked* as the editors' reading).
- **Livestock endowments.** Lamps were endowed with herds: for each perpetual lamp, **96 ewes, or 48 cows, or 16 she-buffaloes**,
  given to named shepherds who owed a fixed measure of ghee each day (*checked*). The animals' increase was the shepherd's; the
  ghee was the temple's. This is a perpetual sharecropping contract in herds, with the temple as owner of the capital.
- **The temple as employer.** The same inscriptions list hundreds paid in land shares, some 400 dancers among musicians,
  accountants, washermen, potters and goldsmiths (*memory*): a planned economy around a shrine.

### 2.6 Buddhist monasteries in China

- **Millers and lenders** (Gernet 1956). Tang monasteries owned water mills and oil presses and rented them out with land worked by
  tenants and bondsmen (*memory*): like the European church (part 05 §2.4), they held much of the capital that needed a stream. The
  Sanjie sect's "inexhaustible treasury" in Chang'an lent its gifts, free to the poor and at interest to others; monasteries ran
  **pawnshops** and lent seed grain (*contested* as the pawnshop's origin).
- **The Huichang suppression.** In 845 the Tang emperor Wuzong, short of revenue and copper, closed the monasteries: the Board of
  Worship counted about **4,600 monasteries, 40,000 hermitages and 260,500 monks and nuns**, who were returned to lay life and the
  tax registers, with **150,000 serfs** (*checked*: the edict's figures as reported; a later count gives about 265,000 laicised).
  Monastery land was confiscated and bronze images and bells were melted for coin. It is the first great dissolution for which there
  are numbers, and it shows the same motives as England's seven centuries later: land, metal and taxpayers.

### 2.7 The Americas

The **Inca** divided land into the state's, the Sun's and the communities', all worked by the communities in turn (part 11 §6);
the Sun's produce and herds fed priests, offerings and storehouses. **Aztec** temples lived on tribute and temple lands (part 11);
the monthly festivals were feasts, markets and sacrifices at once.

### 2.8 Why the temple was the first bank

| What a bank needs | What the temple had |
|---|---|
| a safe store | a sacred building; theft as sacrilege, punished by gods and people; guards; often a fortress precinct |
| permanence | an owner (the god) that does not die, flee or split an inheritance |
| records | literate staff; archives kept for centuries; the habit of counting offerings |
| a central place on a calendar | people came anyway at festivals; deposits and repayments could be timed to them |
| enforcement | oaths sworn before the god; the shame and fear of defaulting on a god; and the temple's own power to seize pledges |
| capital | gifts and tithes accumulated over generations, with nothing else to spend them on |

What it lacked was usually liquidity and the will to take risk: temple treasure was often plate and statues, lent to the state in
war and melted when the state was desperate. The same features recur in the medieval monastery as a place of deposit (§5) and in
the Templars (§10).

## 3. Sacrifice, offerings and votive demand

Ritual created whole industries by asking for particular goods at particular times. A few examples across the map, each joined to
a chain in another part:

| Ritual demand | Good | Chain it fed | Where and when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sacrifice of beasts** | cattle, sheep, goats, pigs | herding (part 02); the meat market in classical towns | Mesopotamia to Rome; Hindu, Jewish (to AD 70), Islamic Eid al-Adha today (millions of animals a year, *rough*) |
| **incense** | frankincense, myrrh | the South Arabian incense road; Nabataean caravans; Roman imports (part 11) | first millennium BC to late Roman times; Pliny complains of the drain of coin east (*unchecked*) |
| **lamps and candles** | olive oil, ghee, beeswax, later tallow | olives (part 11); dairy; the Baltic and Russian wax trade through Novgorod (*memory*); bees | the Church burned beeswax by rule at the altar (part 01: wax candles "for the Church and the rich") |
| **fasting** | salt herring, stockfish, salt cod; eels and pond fish | the herring fisheries, Scania, the Hanse and the Lofoten stockfish trade (parts 03, 04); fishponds (part 03) | medieval Europe: meat forbidden on perhaps 40% of days, in thirteenth-century England nearly half (*checked*, secondary summaries) |
| **the Eucharist** | wheat bread, wine | monastic and episcopal vineyards north of the Alps (Burgundy, the Rhine, the Moselle) | from the early middle ages |
| **church building** | stone, lime, timber, lead, glass, bronze for bells | quarries, lime kilns, glass (part 01), lead mines, bell founding | eleventh to fifteenth centuries; Gimpel's claim that France quarried more stone in 1050 to 1350 than ancient Egypt in any period (*contested*, popular) |
| **liturgical books** | parchment, ink, pigments | parchment from calf and sheep skins (part 03, `parchment`); lapis for blue | monastic scriptoria to the fifteenth century |
| **pilgrimage, prayers for the dead** | badges, lodging; masses and chantry priests | lead and tin casting, inns (§6); endowments (§9) | late medieval Europe |
| **spirit money** | paper burned for the dead | paper and printing (part 11) | China from the Tang (*unchecked*); still a large industry |

**Fish and the fast** shaped a whole sector. Meat was forbidden on Fridays, often Wednesdays and Saturdays, in Lent, Advent and on
vigils (*checked*, secondary), and northern Europe met the demand with salt herring from Scania and stockfish through Bergen and the
Hanse (part 04), later Newfoundland cod. How far the rules drove the trade is argued (*contested*). Protestant England kept fish days
by statute for the fishermen and the navy, adding Wednesday in 1563 (*unchecked*): a ritual rule turned industrial policy.

## 4. Tithe and church taxation

### 4.1 Origins

The tenth as a share owed to a god or a king is old and widespread: the Neo-Babylonian temples' *ešrû* (§2.1); Greek dedications
of a tenth (§2.3); the Hebrew Bible's tithes of produce and herds to the Levites, for feasts and for the poor (Leviticus 27,
Deuteronomy 14); the Islamic *ʿushr* on land (below). Christian tithe began as an exhortation and became a law.

- **The church** urged tithes from the fourth century; the Council of Mâcon (585) ordered them on pain of excommunication
  (*unchecked*; the web's `tithe` node, part 05, already uses 585). **Charlemagne** made tithe a legal obligation enforced by the
  state across his empire in the Capitulary of Herstal (779) (*unchecked*, standard). From then on tithe was a tax collected under
  royal law for the church.
- **The division.** Canon law (from Pope Gelasius, late fifth century) split church income into four: the bishop, the clergy, the
  poor and the fabric of the church; in practice the poor's share shrank as tithes were appropriated (*unchecked*).

### 4.2 How it worked in the village

Part 05 (§3.3) gives the rules: great tithes (grain, hay, wood) to the rector, small tithes (lambs, wool, milk, eggs, garden
produce) to the vicar, collection in the field by counting stooks. Part 02 puts it at a tenth of the sheaves after reaping. A few
details bear on the village as an economy.

- **In kind, in the field, before the household's share.** The farmer left every tenth sheaf standing in the field, and the
  rector's men carted them to the **tithe barn**. The tithe owner thus held a store of grain, straw, hay and wool, and had to thresh,
  sell or use it. Some of the largest medieval barns (Bradford-on-Avon, built for Shaftesbury Abbey) were tithe and demesne barns at
  once (*unchecked*).
- **Disputes.** Every new crop, new land and new practice raised a tithe dispute: was hay titheable, was a mill, were turnips,
  were wages, was land newly reclaimed from waste ("novalia")? Church courts heard tithe cases in large numbers; they were among
  the commonest suits in early modern England (*memory*). Tithe was a tax on innovation as well as output, because new crops were
  usually brought under it after argument.
- **Appropriation.** A monastery or college that took over a parish (appropriated it) took the great tithes and paid a vicar a
  stipend from the small tithes. By the early sixteenth century a large share of English parishes were appropriated, so that the
  tithe of a village often fed a distant abbey (*rough*: about a third of parishes is often given, *memory*). After the
  dissolution these tithes passed to lay owners ("impropriators"), so lay landlords collected tithe for three more centuries.
- **The tithe as a statistic.** Because it was a fixed share of the crop, a tithe series is an output series. Joseph Goy and
  Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie used French tithe records to estimate grain output from the fourteenth to the eighteenth century
  (*memory*). Tithe farms (the right to collect a parish's tithe, auctioned each year) give its expected value in money.
- **Burden.** A tenth of gross output, with seed a quarter, meant a household kept about two-thirds of its harvest (part 02) and
  then paid rent and dues (part 05 §3.4: rent and services a tenth to a quarter). Tithe fell on gross produce, so it took a larger
  share of the net income of poor land than of good land, and of tillage than of pasture (the costs of ploughing are not deducted)
  (*unchecked*, standard reasoning; an old complaint by economists from Adam Smith to Ricardo).

### 4.3 Papal and clerical taxation, and the bankers

- **Peter's pence** (an annual payment from England and elsewhere to Rome, from the Anglo-Saxon period), **annates** (the first
  year's income of a benefice, paid by a new holder), **crusading tenths** and **clerical subsidies** made the papacy a fiscal
  power across Latin Christendom (*unchecked*).
- **The Saladin tithe** (1188), a tenth of movable goods and income levied by Henry II and Philip II for the Third Crusade, was the
  first general tax on personal property in England and a model for later lay subsidies (part 05, `lay_subsidy`) (*unchecked*).
- **Moving the money to Rome** was the papacy's great practical problem, and its solution made international banking. From the
  thirteenth century Florentine, Sienese and Lucchese companies (the Bardi, the Peruzzi, the Spini, later the Medici, who were the
  papal bankers) collected papal taxes in England, France and Germany and paid them out in Rome or Avignon by bills of exchange
  (part 04 §9.1) (*unchecked*). In England they also bought the monasteries' wool with the money (§5.2), so tax and trade cleared
  against each other: papal revenue in England became wool shipped to Florence and florins paid in Italy. The papacy was the
  largest single customer of the bill of exchange in its first century.

### 4.4 The end of compulsory tithe

- **France** abolished tithe on the night of 4 August 1789 (*unchecked*, standard), and the National Assembly nationalised church
  land in November 1789 (§5.6).
- **England** commuted tithe to a money rent charge on land in 1836 (*unchecked*, part 05), assessed by the Tithe Commission's
  parish maps, which are one of the best surveys of English land use ever made (*unchecked*). The rent charge was converted into
  annuities in 1936 and ended in 1977 (*unchecked*).
- **Islamic law** has its own levies: **zakat**, an annual alms on wealth above a threshold, usually 2.5% on money and trade goods
  and set rates on herds; and **ʿushr**, a tenth of the produce of rain-fed land and a twentieth of irrigated land, the lower rate
  allowing for the cost of watering (*unchecked*, standard). Early Islamic states collected zakat as a tax; most now leave it
  voluntary, with a few (Saudi Arabia, Malaysia's states, Pakistan for some decades) collecting it by law.
- **Church taxes today.** Germany, Austria, Switzerland and the Nordic countries collect a church tax with the income tax from
  registered members (in Germany 8 or 9% of income tax). German Catholic dioceses received **€6.51 billion in 2023** (*checked*),
  down from a record €6.84 billion in 2022 as members left; both churches together took about €13 billion (*checked*, IW Köln for
  2022). Tithing survives voluntarily among Latter-day Saints, many evangelical and Pentecostal churches, and others.

## 5. Monasteries as landlords, farmers and makers

### 5.1 Benedictines: the self-sufficient house

The Rule of St Benedict (about 530) required monks to work with their hands and the monastery to hold within its walls "water, a
mill, a garden and the various crafts" so that monks need not wander outside (chapter 66; *unchecked*, standard). The ideal
**Plan of St Gall** (about 820), a drawing for a model monastery, shows a mill, a mortar house, a kiln for drying grain, three
breweries (for monks, guests and pilgrims, and the poor), bakeries, a cooper's and a turner's shop, a smithy, a goldsmith, a
fuller, a shoemaker and saddler, a tannery, granaries, stables for horses, oxen, cows, goats, pigs and sheep, a poultry yard, a
herb garden and an infirmary (*unchecked*). It is the medieval village's catalogue of trades (part 05 §1.4) gathered inside one
institution.

Large Carolingian monasteries were the greatest estates of their age. The **polyptych of Irminon** (about 823 to 829) lists the
lands of Saint-Germain-des-Prés near Paris: thousands of tenant families on its manors (*mansi*), each owing labour weeks,
ploughing services, payments in chickens, eggs, wood, wine and coin (*memory*: on the order of 2,000 to 2,800 households). It is
the founding document of the "bipartite" manor of demesne and tenant holdings (part 05 §3.1): the classical manor is known first
from monastic estates because monasteries kept records.

**Cluny** (founded 910) built the richest religious network of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with over a thousand dependent
houses, living on rents, gifts for prayers and an annual cash census from the kings of Castile paid out of the tribute (*parias*)
taken from Muslim kingdoms (*memory*: 2,000 gold pieces a year under Alfonso VI). Its great church (Cluny III, begun 1088) was the
largest in Christendom until St Peter's was rebuilt. Cluny's economy was liturgy: it sold intercession and spent on building.

### 5.2 Cistercians: granges, lay brothers, wool and water

The Cistercians (Cîteaux, founded 1098) began as a reaction against Cluny's wealth: they refused tithes, mills, serfs, manorial
dues and churches as income and resolved to live from their own labour on land given to them, usually waste or marginal land "far
from the habitations of men" (*unchecked*). Within a century these rules had made them among Europe's most efficient landholders,
and by the fourteenth century they had taken on most of the incomes they first refused.

- **Spread.** About 500 abbeys of men by 1200 and over 700 by 1300 across Europe (*memory*); in England and Wales 77 abbeys,
  most founded by 1153 (*checked*).
- **The grange.** Instead of tenant manors, a Cistercian abbey worked its land directly through **granges**: consolidated farms,
  ideally within a day's journey of the abbey, each with a barn, a chapel and buildings for workers, specialised as arable,
  sheep, cattle, horse-breeding, fisheries, mines or salt (*unchecked*). Granges were often made by buying out or removing the
  peasants already there; the order's clearances of villages for sheep runs are an early case of what part 05 (§2.5) calls
  enclosure.
- **Lay brothers (*conversi*).** Illiterate men who took vows and did the farm work, living at the granges with a shorter round of
  prayer; in the twelfth century they outnumbered the choir monks in many houses, and they declined steeply before 1350, after
  which the granges were let to tenants (*checked*, Cambridge companion; Donkin). Recent work stresses that hired servants
  (*familia*) did much of the work even early (*checked* as a reading).
- **Wool.** English Cistercian houses (Fountains, Rievaulx, Meaux, Tintern and many in Yorkshire and Wales) were among the
  largest producers of export wool. Italian and Flemish merchants contracted for the clip years ahead and paid in advance; the
  Florentine merchant Pegolotti's manual (about 1320s to 1340) lists English monasteries by name with the price of their wool
  (*memory*). Advance sales were credit, and several abbeys fell deep into debt to the merchants when murrain killed their flocks
  (*memory*: Meaux's chronicle). This is the chain of part 03 (`english_wool_export`) and part 04 (`wool_staple`) seen from the
  supplier: a religious order as the export producer, Italian bankers as its buyer and creditor, papal taxes cleared against it
  (§4.3).
- **Iron.** Cistercian houses ran forges in Burgundy, Champagne, Yorkshire and Sweden. The forge building at **Fontenay** (twelfth
  century) used a diverted stream to drive hammers (*memory*); Rievaulx worked iron at Laskill and elsewhere, and excavation at
  **Bordesley** abbey found a water-powered metalworking mill of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries (*memory*, Astill). Part 03's
  water-powered bloomery and hammer (`water_bloomery_hammer`) lists the site as "the lord's or a monastery's".
- **Water.** A twelfth-century description of Clairvaux follows the river Aube, led through the abbey in a channel, as it
  drives the mill for grain, then the brewery, then the fulling stocks, then the tannery, then flushes the drains (*memory*: the
  *Descriptio positionis seu situationis monasterii Clarae-Vallensis*). It is the river with many claims of 02zzk §2, inside one
  wall and under one owner, which removes the quarrel. Cistercians also drained marsh (Ter Duinen and Ter Doest in Flanders, the
  Fens) and built fishponds (part 03, `fishponds`).
- **Vineyards and salt.** The Clos de Vougeot in Burgundy was a Cistercian vineyard from the twelfth century; the order also held
  salt works (Salins, Lüneburg shares; *memory*).
- **Was it an industrial revolution?** Jean Gimpel's *The Medieval Machine* (1976) called the twelfth and thirteenth centuries an
  industrial revolution, with the Cistercians as its factories (*contested*). Part 05 (§2.4) gives a sober figure: about 43% of
  English powered mills in the fourteenth century belonged to the church (*checked* there, Adam Lucas). The church owned so much of
  the water-powered capital because it owned so much land and had the long horizon and the records that capital needs; whether
  monks invented much is doubtful, but they spread and kept what worked.

### 5.3 Monasteries as lenders and hosts

- **Lenders.** Before the rise of Italian banks, monasteries were among the few holders of coin and plate in northern Europe.
  Crusaders and indebted knights pledged land to abbeys for cash, often in the *mort-gage* form in which the lender took the land's
  revenue without reducing the debt, an evasion of the usury rule that let the abbey earn a return (*unchecked*; the origin of the
  word "mortgage" is often traced here). From the thirteenth century monasteries more often borrowed than lent.
- **Hosts.** The Rule told monks to receive guests as Christ; great abbeys fed travellers, pilgrims and the poor at the gate, and
  their guesthouses were inns before there were many inns (part 04, `inns`). The cost could be large: kings and lords claimed
  hospitality for their retinues (*corrodies*, pensions of food and lodging, were sold or granted, sometimes as an annuity bought for
  cash, a hidden loan; *unchecked*).
- **Records and archives.** Monasteries kept charters, cartularies, chronicles and accounts. Their archives are where most medieval
  economic history comes from, and they were also a service: lords and towns deposited documents with them.

### 5.4 The friars, the towns and the pawn bank

The mendicant orders (Franciscans 1209, Dominicans 1216) lived in towns on alms rather than land. Their economic thinking was
sharp: Peter John Olivi (about 1295) wrote on price, value and the merchant's legitimate profit, and Franciscans founded the
**monti di pietà** from 1462 at Perugia, charitable pawn banks lending to the poor on pledges at low interest to drive out
moneylenders (part 04, `pawnbroking`) (*unchecked*). Bernardino of Siena preached against usury and on just price. The friars were
the first economists of the market town.

### 5.5 Elsewhere

Tibetan monasteries held land, herds and serfs and lent grain and money; before 1950 a large share of Tibet's arable was held by
monasteries (*contested*). Theravada monasteries in Sri Lanka and South-East Asia held irrigated land and tanks granted by kings
(*memory*). Coptic and Ethiopian monasteries, Orthodox monasteries in Russia (the Trinity-Sergius Lavra held over 100,000 serfs in
the eighteenth century before Catherine II's secularisation of 1764; *memory*) repeat the pattern.

### 5.6 Dissolution as a land market

| Dissolution | When | What was taken | What became of it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tang China (Huichang)** | 842 to 845 | about 4,600 monasteries, 40,000 hermitages; 260,500 monks and nuns, 150,000 serfs to the tax registers (*checked*) | land to the state and buyers; bronze to the mints; partly reversed by the next emperor |
| **England** | 1536 to 1541; chantries 1545 to 1547 | about 800 or more religious houses (*memory*), with net income about £136,000 a year (*checked*, one study of the Valor; other summaries give about £160,000 gross, *contested*) | the Court of Augmentations (1536) took the land; about two-thirds sold or granted by 1547 and most of the rest by 1554 (*checked*, Clay via NBER w21450) |
| **Russia** | 1764 | monastic estates and their serfs (about a million male peasants, *memory*) | transferred to the state's "economic peasants" |
| **Habsburg lands (Joseph II)** | 1782 on | contemplative monasteries (several hundred, *memory*) | a religious fund paying parish clergy |
| **France** | 1789 to 1790 | all church property (*biens nationaux*), perhaps 6 to 10% of land (*rough*) | sold at auction; the **assignats** were first issued as bonds against it and became paper money that collapsed by 1796 (*unchecked*) |
| **Spain (Mendizábal)** | 1836 on | monastic land, later (Madoz, 1855) municipal commons too | auctions to pay the public debt; bought largely by urban and noble buyers (*contested* who gained) |
| **Mexico (Lerdo law)** | 1856 | corporate land of the church and of Indian communities | sales to tenants and buyers; a cause of the Reform War |

**England in detail.** The **Valor Ecclesiasticus** (1535) was a national survey of church income, made to assess a new tax of a
tenth; it doubled as the inventory for the dissolution. The **act of 1536** dissolved houses with income under £200 a year; the
greater houses surrendered "voluntarily" from 1538 and an **act of 1539** confirmed it. The Crown's income from the estates and
sales was large: estimates of the receipts from 1536 to 1547 run from about £800,000 from sales to about £1.3 million in all
(*checked* as the range of estimates found; *contested*). Most went to war with France and Scotland in the 1540s. The land passed
mainly to existing landowners, courtiers, officials and lawyers, who resold much of it, so that a market in land on a scale
England had not seen opened within a decade (*unchecked*). Tawney's "rise of the gentry" (1941) made the transfer the root of a
new class and so of the Civil War (*contested*, the "storm over the gentry"). Heldring, Robinson and Vollmer (NBER w21450, 2015 and
revisions) argue that parishes where monastic land entered the market later had more enclosure, more agricultural innovation and
more textile mills, because the land left the church's fixed, customary tenures for owners who could reorganise it (*memory* for
the findings; *contested*). The dissolution also ended most monastic alms and hospitality (part 05 §6.5), and within sixty years
England had a compulsory poor rate (§12).

The pattern across all of these: a religious institution accumulates land over centuries through gifts it may not sell (the "dead
hand", §9); its land is managed for stability, not profit; a state short of money takes it; it is sold fast, cheaply, to people
with capital; and the new owners manage it for rent. Each dissolution was a one-off transfer of capital from an endowment economy
to a market one.

## 6. Pilgrimage as trade and travel infrastructure

Pilgrimage moved large numbers of people over long distances on a calendar, and the roads, bridges, inns, hospitals, markets and
credit that served them served trade too.

- **Santiago de Compostela.** From the tenth century the shrine of St James drew pilgrims across France and northern Spain. The
  **Codex Calixtinus** (about 1140) includes a guide to the four French routes, the stages, the towns, the rivers that are safe to
  drink and the tolls that are unjust (*unchecked*). Along the camino the kings of León and Castile chartered towns with privileges
  for settlers (many of them French, *francos*), built bridges and founded hospitals; Puente la Reina and Estella are towns made by
  the road (*memory*). Pilgrims wore the scallop shell, sold at the shrine.
- **Rome.** Boniface VIII's first jubilee (1300) offered a plenary indulgence to those who came; the chronicler Giovanni Villani
  wrote of 200,000 pilgrims in the city at any time (*contested*, a chronicler's number). Later jubilees were fixed every 50, then 25
  years, with large flows of coin to Rome.
- **English shrines.** Canterbury (Becket, from 1170), Walsingham, Hailes and others drew pilgrims whose offerings paid for building
  and whose spending made the shrine towns. Pilgrim badges in lead and tin were cast in moulds by the hundred thousand (*rough*)
  and are found across Europe: a small metal trade made by devotion.
- **The hajj.** The pilgrimage to Mecca, owed once in a lifetime by those able, has run since the seventh century. It moved people
  and goods across the Islamic world each year (part 11 §4.1) and joined a fair: pilgrims traded at Mecca and on the way, and the
  great caravans from Cairo and Damascus were trade caravans as well (*unchecked*). Mansa Musa's hajj of 1324 (part 11) spent so
  much gold in Cairo that its price fell there for years. The Ottoman state paid large subsidies to the Bedouin tribes on the route
  and to the holy cities (*memory*). Today the hajj and umrah are a major part of the Saudi service economy (*rough*: about 1.8
  million hajj pilgrims in 2024, and many millions for umrah; *memory*).
- **India.** Hindu pilgrimage to tirthas (Varanasi, Puri, Tirupati, the Kumbh Mela) supported brahmin guides (*pandas*) who kept
  genealogical registers of pilgrim families for generations (*unchecked*), temple towns and fairs; the 2025 Kumbh Mela at
  Prayagraj was claimed by the state to have drawn over 600 million visits over six weeks (*contested*, an official count).
- **Infrastructure.** Pilgrimage paid for works that a market would not have built on its own:
  - **bridges**: the Frères Pontifes are traditionally linked with the bridge at Avignon (twelfth century; *contested*); bishops
    granted indulgences to those who gave to bridge building, and bridges carried chapels and were kept by hermits (part 04,
    `bridges_pontage`) (*unchecked*);
  - **hospices and hospitals** along the routes, endowed and run by orders (the Hospitallers began as a hospital for pilgrims in
    Jerusalem, about 1070s to 1113);
  - **roads and protection**: the military orders were founded to protect pilgrims (§10);
  - **credit**: a pilgrim could deposit money with the Templars at home and draw on it in the East (§10).
- **Pilgrimage and disease.** Large gatherings carried epidemics: cholera spread from the hajj and the Kumbh in the nineteenth
  century and prompted the first international sanitary conferences (1851 on) and quarantine stations on the route (*unchecked*;
  part 05, `quarantine`).

## 7. Calendars, feast days, fairs and the rhythm of work

### 7.1 Days of rest

- **The Jewish Sabbath** forbade work on the seventh day for the household, its servants and its beasts; the **sabbatical year**
  (*shmita*) left land fallow every seventh year and released debts (Deuteronomy 15) (*unchecked*, standard). The debt release is
  the origin of one of the best-known workarounds (§8.1).
- **Sunday.** Constantine ordered rest on Sunday for townspeople in 321, exempting farm work (*unchecked*, standard). Church councils
  and Carolingian law extended the rule to field work. Medieval English law and custom kept Sunday free of servile work and, after
  long argument, of markets (§7.3).
- **Holy days.** On top of Sundays the medieval western calendar had many feast days of obligation, on which people were to hear mass
  and abstain from servile work: part 05 (§1.3) gives about **40 to 50 holy days** for the English village, and Voth about 53 lost
  from London working years between 1750 and 1800 (part 05 §1.9, *checked* there). With Sundays that is about 100 days a year
  without full field work, less in harvest, when dispensations and boon days put everyone in the fields (*rough*).
- **The Reformation cut holy days.** In England an act and injunctions of 1536 abolished feasts falling in harvest and in the law
  terms (*unchecked*), and Protestant churches generally reduced the calendar to Sundays and a few great feasts; Catholic countries
  kept more. Whether the fall in holy days raised output is part of the "industrious revolution" argument (part 05 §1.9: de Vries
  against Allen and Weisdorf) and of the Weber debate (§11). One estimate for early modern Europe puts the Protestant gain at a few
  weeks of work a year (*memory*; *contested*).
- **Secular calendars.** The French Revolutionary calendar replaced the week with a ten-day *décade*, one day of rest in ten, from
  1793 to 1805; the Soviet "continuous week" (1929 to 1940) staggered rest days so that factories never stopped. Both failed, partly
  because families could not rest together (*unchecked*). The rhythm of shared rest is an economic coordination device as much as a
  religious one.
- **Today.** Sunday trading laws in Europe (the UK Sunday Trading Act 1994; Germany's *Ladenschlussgesetz*); US "blue laws"; the
  Friday and Saturday weekend in most Muslim-majority states, with the UAE moving to Saturday and Sunday in 2022 partly to align with
  world markets (*unchecked*). Ramadan changes working hours and output: Campante and Yanagizawa-Drott (2015) found that longer daily
  fasting hours in Ramadan lowered output growth in Muslim countries while raising reported well-being (*memory*; *contested*).

### 7.2 Quarter days and the reckoning

The great feasts were the economy's calendar. In England the **quarter days**, Lady Day (25 March), Midsummer (24 June),
Michaelmas (29 September) and Christmas, were when rents fell due, servants were hired and paid, tenancies began and ended, and
debts were reckoned (part 04, `reckoning_days`; part 05, `hiring_fairs`). Michaelmas was the end of the farming year and the
start of the manorial accounting year (*unchecked*). Scotland used Candlemas, Whitsunday, Lammas and Martinmas. Rents in kind were
tied to feasts too: hens at Christmas, eggs at Easter (*unchecked*). A promise "to pay at Michaelmas" needed no date in a world
without calendars on the wall; the feast was the clock.

### 7.3 Fairs on feast days

- **The fair as a feast.** Medieval fairs were usually held on and around the feast of the saint of the local church or abbey, and
  the word *feria* means a holy day (*unchecked*). The lord of the fair was often a church: the bishop of Winchester held **St
  Giles's fair** (granted 1096) on the hill outside the city, one of the largest in England, with its own court and the bishop's
  right to close all other trade in Winchester while it ran (*memory*); the prior of St Bartholomew's held **Bartholomew Fair** in
  London from 1133; the abbey of Saint-Denis held the **Lendit** fair near Paris (*memory*). The fair's tolls and stall rents were
  church income; its court (the piepowder court, part 04 `fair_courts`) was a church franchise.
- **Markets and Sunday.** Many early markets met on Sundays at the church door, where people gathered anyway. From the late twelfth
  century the church pressed to move them; English charters moved many markets to weekdays in the thirteenth century, and the
  Statute of Winchester (1285) forbade fairs and markets in churchyards (*unchecked*). The weekly market day of part 04 was often the
  old Sunday gathering moved to a weekday.
- **Church ales.** Parishes raised money for the church fabric and the poor by brewing ale and selling it at a feast (Whitsun ales,
  church ales, bid-ales for a needy neighbour) (*unchecked*). Churchwardens' accounts show these feasts as the largest single source
  of parish income in many places before the Reformation (*memory*, Hutton). They were suppressed by Puritans in the late sixteenth
  and seventeenth centuries. A church ale is a work feast turned into a fundraiser: grain given by parishioners, brewed in the church
  house, sold back to them, the profit to a common store.
- **Harvest and boon.** The lord's harvest boons ended with a feast (part 05 boon days); the harvest home and the Lammas loaf
  (first bread of the new crop, offered in church on 1 August) marked the farming year (*unchecked*).

### 7.4 Calendars as knowledge

Fixing the date of Easter (the *computus*) needed astronomy and arithmetic, and monasteries kept the tables. The canonical hours
divided the day for monks, and the need to ring them at the right time is one of the arguments for why the mechanical clock appeared
in late thirteenth-century Europe, first in monasteries and cathedrals (*contested*: Landes, *Revolution in Time*; others stress
towns and astronomy). Church bells told the village the hours of work: the curfew, the angelus. A shared time signal is a public
good the church supplied.

## 8. Religious rules on lending and their workarounds

### 8.1 The old rules

- **The Hebrew Bible** forbade interest on loans to fellow Israelites but allowed it from foreigners (Deuteronomy 23:19 to 20),
  and the sabbatical year released debts. **Hillel's prozbul** (first century BC) let a creditor transfer a loan to a court before
  the sabbatical year so that it would not be released, because lenders had stopped lending as the year approached (*unchecked*,
  standard). It is the clearest early case of a rule that killed credit and a contract invented to save it.
- **Mesopotamia** capped interest rather than banning it (part 04 §3.2), and kings periodically cancelled debts (part 04,
  `debt_cancellation`): a periodic jubilee under royal rather than divine authority.
- **Hindu law** allowed interest with caps graded by caste and security; the *Arthashastra* gives 1¼% a month as the proper rate
  on secured loans and much higher rates for risky trade (*memory*). Buddhist monasteries lent at interest (§2.6).

### 8.2 The Christian ban and its contracts

| Date | Rule | Effect |
|---|---|---|

| 1545 | England allows interest up to 10% (repealed 1552, restored 1571) | statute replaces the ban with a cap |

| 1830 | Rome tells confessors not to trouble those who take the legal rate | ban in abeyance |

(All dates *unchecked*, standard; Noonan 1957 and Homer and Sylla are the references.)

What counted as usury was a **profit on a loan (*mutuum*) as such**. Scholastic theology accepted compensation for a loss the
lender suffered (*damnum emergens*), for a profit he gave up (*lucrum cessans*), for a penalty on late payment (*poena
conventionalis*), and, more grudgingly, for risk (*periculum sortis*). Each opened a door. The main contracts that carried credit
past the ban:

| Contract | How it hid or avoided interest | Part |
|---|---|---|
| **partnership** (*commenda*, *societas*, *compagnia*) | the investor shares profit and loss, so it is not a loan | 04, 11, 12 |
| **bill of exchange** | the interest sits in the difference between exchange rates at two places and dates; "dry exchange" (*cambio secco*), a bill with no real transfer, was condemned and widely used | 04 §9.1 |
| **census, rent charge** (*rente*, *Rentenkauf*) | the lender buys a perpetual annual rent secured on land or a town's revenues; redeemable by the seller only | 05; the basis of town and state debt (*Renten* in Germany and the Low Countries) |
| **mort-gage** | the lender takes the land's revenue without reducing the debt | §5.3 |
| **sale and resale** | sell goods on credit at a higher price, or buy and resell at once | |
| **late penalties** | a loan "free" if repaid on time, with a penalty both sides expect to be paid | |
| **triple contract** (*contractus trinus*) | a partnership plus insurance of the capital plus sale of uncertain profit for a fixed return: a loan at fixed interest in three legal parts; defended by Johann Eck (1514, with the Fuggers' support), condemned by Sixtus V (1586) | |
| **public debt shares** | Florence's *Monte* and Venice's *prestiti* paid "gifts" to forced lenders; tradeable, and argued over by theologians | 04 `funded_public_debt` |
| **monti di pietà** | charitable pawn banks charging a fee to cover costs | 04 `pawnbroking` |

The ban's real effects were on **form and cost**: credit went into contracts that were harder to write and enforce, priced risk
poorly, and were most available to those who could afford lawyers and notaries; open moneylending was left to outsiders and to
the poor's pawnbrokers; and interest was hidden in records, which makes historical interest rates hard to read. Usurers still
made restitution in their wills (*unchecked*), so the ban also moved money into the church at death. Calvin's letter on usury
(1545) and the English statute of the same year accepted moderate interest in principle; Catholic practice followed in fact.

### 8.3 Jewish moneylending in Christian Europe

Because the Christian ban did not bind Jews, and Deuteronomy allowed lending to non-Jews, and because Jews were excluded from guilds
and often from holding land, moneylending became one of the few trades open to them in parts of northern Europe from the twelfth
century (*unchecked*). Rulers licensed and taxed them.

- **England.** Jews came with the Normans. After the massacres of 1189 to 1190, in which debt records were burned, the Crown
  created the **Exchequer of the Jews** (1194) and required every loan to be recorded in a chest (*archa*) in one of the main towns,
  with a copy of the bond (the *chirograph*) kept under joint Christian and Jewish keys (*unchecked*). It was a public register of
  private debts, run because the Crown taxed and inherited the debts. Heavy tallages exhausted the community; the Statute of Jewry
  (1275) banned their lending; Edward I expelled the Jews in 1290 (*unchecked*), and Italian bankers took their place as the
  Crown's lenders.
- **Italy.** Towns invited Jewish bankers under a contract (*condotta*) that set the rate (often 20 to 40% a year on small pawn
  loans) and the term, as a regulated service for the poor (*memory*); the *monti di pietà* were founded partly to compete with
  them.
- **Heter iska.** Jewish law also bans interest between Jews; the *heter iska* (sixteenth century) turns a loan into a
  partnership in which the "investor" receives a fixed return unless the "manager" proves a loss under strict conditions, which in
  practice he cannot (*unchecked*). Israeli banks use a general *heter iska* today.
- **Botticini and Eckstein** (*The Chosen Few*, 2012) argue that a religious rule requiring men to read the Torah made Jews literate
  and so drew them into urban trades and finance before any exclusion did (*contested*).

### 8.4 Islamic rules and their contracts

The Qur'an forbids **riba** (increase, understood by most jurists as any fixed return on a loan) and **gharar** (excessive
uncertainty in a contract). Islamic commercial law developed partnership and sale forms to carry credit.

- **Partnership.** *Mudaraba* or *qirad* (an investor provides capital and a merchant labour, sharing profits by agreed ratio and the
  investor bearing loss) and *musharaka* (joint capital) (part 11, `qirad`). Udovitch argued the commenda came to Italy from the
  qirad (*contested*).
- **Sales on credit.** A higher price for deferred payment is a sale, not a loan, and so allowed by most schools. The legal
  devices (*hiyal*) included the double sale (*bayʿ al-ʿina*: sell an item on credit and buy it back at once for less in cash), which
  some schools accepted and others condemned (*unchecked*).
- **Transfer and bills.** The *hawala* and *suftaja* (part 11) moved money across distance; a fee for transfer is not riba.
- **The cash waqf.** From the fifteenth century Ottoman endowments of cash lent out their capital, often at 10 to 15% a year through
  sale-and-resale forms, to fund mosques and schools; the Ottoman chief jurist Ebussuud upheld them against critics in the 1540s
  (*memory*; Mandaville 1979). By the eighteenth century cash waqfs were the main lenders in Ottoman towns.
- **Kuran and Rubin.** Timur Kuran (*The Long Divergence*, 2011) argues that Islamic partnership law (dissolved at a partner's death),
  equal inheritance among heirs and the waqf's rigidity kept Middle Eastern firms small and blocked the corporation, while the
  ban on interest mattered less because it was evaded (*contested*). Jared Rubin (*Rulers, Religion, and Riches*, 2017) argues that
  where rulers drew legitimacy from religious authorities, as in the Ottoman empire, those authorities could block changes such as
  open interest and printing; where they did not, as in Protestant Europe, merchants' interests won (*contested*).
- **Modern Islamic finance.** The Mit Ghamr savings bank in Egypt (1963), the Dubai Islamic Bank and the Islamic Development Bank
  (1975) began a modern industry (*unchecked*). Its dominant contract is **murabaha**, a cost-plus sale on deferred payment, which
  critics say reproduces interest under another name (El-Gamal, *Islamic Finance: Law, Economics, and Practice*, 2006). Sukuk are
  bonds structured as shares in assets or leases. Size: about **$3.88 trillion** of assets in 2024 by the Islamic Financial Services
  Board, 72% in banking and over half in the Gulf states; other counts give about $5 to 6 trillion with wider scope (*checked*, both).

## 9. Waqf and endowments

### 9.1 The waqf

A **waqf** (part 11, `waqf`; the funduq and caravanserai of part 11 §4.1) is property set aside in perpetuity for a purpose named by
the founder, its income spent as the deed says, under a manager (*mutawalli*) supervised by the judge (*qadi*). It cannot be sold,
given or inherited. It spread from the eighth century and became the main way to provide public goods in Islamic societies.

- **What waqfs built and ran:** mosques, schools (madrasas), hospitals, soup kitchens (*imaret*), fountains, caravanserais, bridges,
  markets, baths. A great foundation like the **Süleymaniye** complex in Istanbul (1550s) was endowed with villages, shops, baths and
  mills whose rents paid for its mosque, schools, hospital, kitchen and staff (*unchecked*).
- **How large.** Estimates for the Ottoman empire before the nineteenth-century reforms range from a fifth to a half of land and
  property in some provinces (*contested*); Kuran estimates that waqf revenues in the eighteenth century were a large share of the
  state's own (*memory*). In some cities most commercial property was endowed.
- **Why so popular.** A waqf protected wealth from confiscation (rulers rarely seized endowed property), escaped the fixed shares of
  Islamic inheritance (a founder could name his descendants as beneficiaries, the "family waqf"), and gave the founder's family
  salaried posts as managers. It was as much an estate plan as a gift (*contested* in degree).
- **The cost.** Because the founder's terms bound forever, a waqf could not easily change use, merge or borrow; buildings decayed
  when rents fell and the purpose became obsolete; managers drew salaries from shrinking income. Kuran's thesis (§8.4) is that this
  rigidity froze capital that in Europe went through more flexible trusts and corporations (*contested*).
- **Reform.** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century states took waqfs under ministries (Ottoman Ministry of Pious Foundations, 1826; Egypt
  abolished family waqfs in 1952) (*unchecked*).

### 9.2 Western endowments: the dead hand

- **Mortmain.** Land given to a church passed into "the dead hand" (*mortmain*): it never died, married, came of age or forfeited, so
  the lord lost the feudal incidents (reliefs, wardships, escheats) he would have had from a lay tenant. The English **Statute of
  Mortmain** (1279) forbade gifts of land to the church without the lord's licence (*unchecked*, standard). It is an early case of a
  state limiting endowments to protect its revenue.
- **Chantries.** Late medieval people endowed chantries (priests to sing masses for their souls) with land or rents; there were some
  thousands in England when they were dissolved in 1547 (*memory*: about 2,400). Many also ran schools, which the Crown refounded in a
  few cases ("King Edward VI grammar schools").
- **Colleges and hospitals.** Oxford and Cambridge colleges, cathedral chapters, almshouses and hospitals (St Bartholomew's, London,
  1123; St Cross, Winchester, 1130s) were endowed corporations (part 05 §2.6, `corporation`). Several Oxford and Cambridge colleges
  are still among the larger landowners in England.
- **The trust.** The English use and trust developed from the thirteenth century, partly to give land to friars (who could not own
  it) and to evade feudal dues; Gaudiosi (1988) argued it borrowed from the waqf through returning crusaders (*contested*).
- **Modern foundations and endowments.** University endowments (Harvard's about $50 billion, *memory*), religious foundations and
  philanthropic foundations are the waqf's descendants: capital held in perpetuity for a purpose, managed by trustees. The Church of
  England's Church Commissioners and the Latter-day Saints' investment arm hold large portfolios (*memory*: both in the tens of
  billions; the latter reported at about $100 billion in 2019, *contested*).

## 10. Religious orders and networks as trust

### 10.1 The Templars

The **Knights Templar** (founded about 1119 to 1120 in Jerusalem to protect pilgrims) received land across Europe, built a chain of
fortified houses from Scotland to Syria and moved men, money and supplies to the East. These features made them bankers:
- **Safe deposit.** Lords, merchants and kings left money and documents in the Paris and London Temples. Joinville deposited 360
  livres of his pay with the Templars at Acre in 1250 (*checked*).
- **The royal treasury.** From the late twelfth century the Paris Temple held and managed the French king's treasury, with the
  treasurer a Templar; the Temple sent account statements to major clients several times a year (*checked*, secondary).
- **Transfer.** A pilgrim or crusader could deposit money in Europe and draw it in the East against a written document, and the
  order could settle between its houses without moving coin (*contested* in detail; the giro form is reported by popular sources and
  better attested for royal accounts).
- **Lending.** They lent to kings, including Louis VII and Louis IX for crusades (*unchecked*).
- **The end.** Philip IV, heavily indebted to them and short of money, had every Templar in France arrested on **13 October 1307**
  (*checked*); the pope suppressed the order in 1312 and gave most of its property to the Hospitallers (*unchecked*). It is the
  first case of a state destroying its creditor, and a lesson in the risk of lending to a sovereign that part 04's later bankers
  (the Bardi and Peruzzi under Edward III) repeated.
- **The Teutonic Order** ruled Prussia from the thirteenth century as a state, with a monopoly of Baltic amber (part 04,
  `amber_road`) and a large grain export from its estates through Danzig (*memory*). A religious order became a territorial monopolist.

### 10.2 The Jesuits

Founded 1540, the Society of Jesus was an international organisation of educated men with a central command in Rome, regular
written reports (the *litterae annuae*), and colleges across Europe, Asia and the Americas.
- **Colleges financed by estates.** In New Spain and Peru Jesuit colleges were endowed with **haciendas**: sugar plantations worked by
  enslaved Africans, cattle ranches, vineyards and textile workshops (*obrajes*), run with careful accounting to fund the colleges
  (Konrad's study of Santa Lucía, Mexico; *memory*). At the expulsion of 1767 they were among the largest landowners in Spanish
  America.
- **The Guaraní reductions.** Thirty mission towns in Paraguay, Argentina and Brazil (from 1609) reached a peak of about **141,000
  people in 1732** (*checked*), then fell by half in the epidemics of 1733 to 1740 (*checked*). They farmed communally and privately,
  ran cattle herds and sold **yerba mate** in the La Plata market (*checked*, qualitative). They were a planned economy under a
  religious order, closed to Spanish settlers, which made them rich in their region and resented.
- **Trade and debt.** The French Jesuit Antoine Lavalette's trading ventures in Martinique failed in the Seven Years' War, and the
  suit by his creditors helped bring the order's suppression in France in 1764 (*memory*). Portugal (1759), Spain (1767) and the
  pope (1773) followed; the confiscated estates were sold.
- **Knowledge.** Jesuits carried European astronomy and mathematics to China and reported Chinese crafts back (d'Entrecolles on
  Jingdezhen porcelain, part 11), and cinchona bark ("Jesuits' bark") to Europe (*unchecked*). An order was a channel for knowledge
  across civilisations, a link the web lacks (02zzk §3).

### 10.3 Quakers and the dissenters

The Society of Friends (from the 1650s) was excluded from universities, public office and the professions in England and turned to
trade, manufacturing and banking. Its members became disproportionately prominent in eighteenth-century industry and finance.

- **Trust.** Quaker meetings disciplined members for debt, bankruptcy and dishonest dealing, and could disown them; members dealt
  with each other across the Atlantic on that guarantee (*unchecked*, standard). Quakers were early to sell at **fixed prices**
  rather than haggling, on the principle of telling the truth (*unchecked*).
- **Iron.** Abraham Darby, a Quaker, smelted iron with coke at Coalbrookdale in **1709** (part 07, `coke`), and the Darbys and
  their Quaker partners and relations (the Reynoldses, the Lloyds of Birmingham) ran ironworks for a century; Benjamin Huntsman's
  crucible steel (part 03, `crucible_steel`, about 1740) was a Quaker's too (*unchecked*).
- **Banks.** Lloyds (Birmingham, 1765), Barclays (from Quaker goldsmith-bankers in London), the Gurneys of Norwich, Backhouse of
  Darlington (*unchecked*): part of the country banks of part 07.
- **Railways.** Edward Pease and other Quaker financiers of Darlington backed the **Stockton and Darlington Railway** (1825; part
  04 `railway`), sometimes called "the Quaker line" (*unchecked*).
- **Food.** Cadbury, Fry and Rowntree in chocolate, Huntley and Palmers in biscuits, Clarks in shoes, Bryant and May in matches
  (*unchecked*).
- **Why.** Exclusion from other careers, literacy, thrift, a network of kin and co-religionists that lent to each other and vouched
  for each other, and a reputation outsiders could rely on. The same explanation is given for other minorities: the **Huguenots**
  after 1685 (silk weaving in Spitalfields, banking in Geneva, Amsterdam and London), the **Old Believers** in Moscow's textile
  industry, the **Parsis** in Bombay's shipping and cotton, the **Jains and Marwaris** in Indian banking (the Jagat Seths of Bengal;
  part 11, `shroffs_hundi`), the **Armenians of New Julfa** in the silk trade between Persia, India and Europe (Aslanian 2011;
  *memory*), and the Maghribi traders of part 04 and 05.

### 10.4 Theories of religious networks

- **Middleman minorities.** A minority excluded from land and office, internally cohesive and trusted among its own, specialises in
  trade and credit between other groups (Sombart, then Bonacich 1973; *memory*). It gains from the trust, and is exposed to
  expulsion and confiscation, as the Jews of England in 1290 and the Templars in 1307 were.
- **Club goods** (Iannaccone 1992, "Sacrifice and stigma"): strict religious groups impose costly rules (dress, diet, rest) that
  screen out free riders, so members can trust each other with credit and mutual aid. Berman (2000) applied it to ultra-Orthodox
  communities and later to militant religious groups (*memory*).
- **Coalitions and community enforcement** (Greif; part 04 §4.4 on the Maghribis): a religious community is a ready coalition with
  shared courts, meetings where news spreads, and sanctions (disownment, excommunication) that cost a defaulter his whole network.

Three readings come out of the debate and bear on the map. First, the measured channels are **human capital** (reading, from
Bible reading and schooling), **reallocation** (land, buildings and people moved from church to secular uses) and **trust networks**
(§10), more than a work ethic as such. Second, religion often mattered by **who held what** (land, mills, tithes, the calendar)
rather than by belief. Third, the timing problem never goes away: commercial capitalism was well developed in Catholic Italy and
Flanders and in the Islamic and Chinese worlds (part 11) long before Calvin.

## 12. Charity and poor relief

Part 05 (§6.5) gives the means of insurance (stores, kin, neighbours, credit, charity, poor relief) and the English poor law's costs.
Religious institutions ran most of the charity before the state.

- **The church's share.** The canonical quarter of tithe for the poor (§4.1); monastic alms at the gate, doles at funerals and on
  anniversaries, hospitals for the sick, the old and travellers, leper houses, and almshouses. How much went to the poor is argued:
  Savine's reading of the Valor (1909) found monastic alms of only about 3% of income (*memory*), but the Valor counted only alms
  required by founders' deeds, and later historians (Harvey, Rushton) put the real share higher, with food given at the gate and in
  hospitality (*contested*).
- **Fraternities and gilds.** Parish and craft fraternities (part 05 `friendly_societies`; guilds) paid for funerals, masses, and
  aid to members in sickness and poverty from members' dues and endowments (*unchecked*). They were also drinking clubs and
  credit circles.
- **Islam.** Zakat (2.5% of wealth, §4.4) is owed to named classes (the poor, the needy, debtors, travellers, collectors and others),
  sadaqa is voluntary alms, and the waqf soup kitchen fed students, travellers and the poor daily (*unchecked*). The Ottoman
  *imaret* of a great mosque could feed hundreds to a thousand a day (*memory*).
- **Judaism.** The communal **kuppah** (charity fund) and **tamhui** (soup kitchen) were levied on members by the community
  (*unchecked*).
- **Buddhism.** *Dana* (giving) to monks, who in turn ran famine relief, hospitals and the inexhaustible treasuries (§2.6).
- **The Reformation and the common chest.** Protestant towns replaced monastic and fraternal alms with a **common chest** filled
  from confiscated church endowments and contributions and run by elected stewards: Wittenberg (1520 to 1521), **Leisnig** (1523,
  with Luther's preface), Nuremberg, Strasbourg, Zurich; Catholic Ypres (1525) did the same, and Juan Luis Vives's *De subventione
  pauperum* (1526) argued for municipal relief (*unchecked*). They banned begging, registered the poor and gave relief by need: the
  model for the English parish poor rate of 1597 to 1601 (part 05), administered by churchwardens and overseers in the parish, the
  church's unit made a civil one.
- **Modern.** Religious bodies still run a large share of charity: hospitals, schools, food banks, the Salvation Army, Caritas,
  Islamic Relief. Grim and Grim (2016) valued the US religious sector at about **$1.2 trillion a year** in their middle estimate,
  counting congregations' spending, religious institutions (hospitals, schools, charities) and religion-related businesses; their
  low estimate was about $378 billion (revenues only) and their high $4.8 trillion (*checked* as the study's figures; the method is
  argued).

## 13. Modern religious economies where they bear on the map

Most of the twentieth- and twenty-first-century religious economy is a nonprofit sector, but a few parts shape chains in the other
parts.

- **Rules on food as supply-chain standards.** **Halal** and **kosher** certification are standards applied at the slaughterhouse,
  the processor and the ingredient supplier. They run through the packers of part 02 and part 13 (`meatpacking`): Brazil and
  Australia export halal beef and chicken at scale, and certifiers (the Orthodox Union and others for kosher; national bodies such
  as Malaysia's JAKIM for halal) are chokepoints that a supplier must pass to sell into a market (*unchecked*). A ritual rule has
  become a market-access standard.
- **Rules on rest** (§7.1) set shop hours and the working week; **rules on lending** (§8.4) created Islamic finance; **church
  taxes** (§4.4) fund churches as employers (the German churches and their welfare arms, Caritas and Diakonie, are among the
  country's largest employers; *memory*: over a million employees together).
- **Pilgrimage and religious tourism** (§6) are large service industries: the hajj and umrah in Saudi Arabia's plans, Lourdes,
  Vatican City's museums, the Kumbh Mela.
- **Temple gold.** Indian households and temples hold a very large stock of gold (often put at over 20,000 tonnes in households and
  several thousand in temples, *contested*); the state's gold monetisation scheme (2015) tried to bring temple gold into the
  banking system (*unchecked*). Offerings as a store of value, still.
- **Spirit money and ritual goods.** Joss paper and funeral goods in China and diaspora communities; candles, flowers and incense
  everywhere; Christmas as the largest single season of retail demand in many countries, and Diwali, Eid and Lunar New Year in
  others (*unchecked*). The ritual calendar still sets the peaks of consumption.
- **Megachurches and the prosperity gospel.** Large congregations run schools, media and property; the prosperity gospel teaches
  that giving (tithing) brings wealth, and a literature argues it encourages saving and enterprise in Africa and Latin America and
  another that it extracts from the poor (*contested*).
