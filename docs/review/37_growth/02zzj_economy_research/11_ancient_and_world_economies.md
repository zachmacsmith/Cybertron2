# 11: Ancient and world economies before industry: Rome, China, India, the Islamic world, the Americas and Africa

Part of the 02zzj economy research folder (15 Nov 2026), for the designers of Cybertron 2. It balances the folder's European
material with the large economies of antiquity and of Asia, Africa and the Americas before about 1800: what they made, the supply
chains behind each industry, how the chains were organised (market, state, tribute, temple, monopoly), and how the regions joined
into one world economy. Chain shapes use the vocabulary of `docs/cybertron2/proposals/economy_web.md` §1 (shallow, accretive,
paradigm switch, substitutes, joint products, assembly, chokepoint, long skill, time capital, pulse, commons, increasing
returns); the frame of limits and society types is `02zzi_economies_scaling.md`. Nothing here is limited by what a simulator
could afford to run.

**Numbers.** Ancient and medieval figures are reconstructions. Each is cited; ones marked *(rough)* are orders of magnitude, and
ones marked *(contested)* have serious scholarly estimates far apart. Bracketed numbers point to the sources at the end.

## Summary

The pre-industrial world held several economies as large and as deep as Europe's, and for long stretches larger. Imperial Rome
fed a city of perhaps a million on wheat shipped from Egypt and Africa, ate olive oil from Spain in tens of millions of amphorae,
and smelted enough lead and silver that its output can be read in Greenland ice. Song China (960 to 1279) made iron in tens of
thousands of tonnes a year with coal and blast furnaces, moved rice up a canal more than 1,700 km long, and was the first state to
issue paper money. India dressed much of the Old World in cotton cloth with fast colours no one else could match; the Islamic
world ran a credit system of transferable bills across three continents and carried paper, sugar and irrigation west; the Inca
fed an empire of millions with no markets and no money at all, from storehouses on mountainsides; and West African gold minted
the coins of the Mediterranean for five hundred years. The lesson for a designer is that the same few problems (feeding a
capital, moving bulk, paying an army, trusting a stranger far away, controlling a chokepoint) were solved in very different ways,
and each way made a different society.

These regional systems were joined long before 1500 by relay trade along the Silk Roads, across the Sahara and around the Indian
Ocean, each segment run by its own merchants, institutions and seasons. After 1500 American silver, mined with Andean forced
labour and mercury from Spain and Peru, flowed both ways round the world and ended largely in China, which had made silver its
tax money; American crops (maize, potato, sweet potato, cassava) remade the food base of Europe, Africa and China; and sugar,
moving west from India through the Islamic world to the Atlantic, became the plantation and slave system. The comparison with
Europe's path is not one of backwardness: China and India reached high levels of output, specialisation and commerce on an
organic energy base. What they did not do (and what Europe did only late and partly by luck of coal and colonies) was switch to
mineral energy. For a simulator the material offers a catalogue of chain shapes and institutions not found in medieval Europe:
state procurement and monopoly, tribute and redistribution without markets, time shares in water, transferable credit, relay
trade, monsoon pulses, and proxies by which output can be measured from what a society leaves behind.

## 1. Classical antiquity

### 1.1 Before Greece: merchants on credit at Kanesh

Long before coins, the Old Assyrian merchants of Assur (about 1950 to 1750 BC) ran a trade in tin and woollen textiles to
Kanesh in Anatolia, by donkey caravans of tens of animals over about 1,000 to 1,200 km in six to eight weeks, selling for silver
and gold which went back to Assur to buy more [14]. More than 22,000 merchant letters and contracts survive from one level of the
site: family firms with branch offices, partnerships with pooled capital, interest-bearing loans, transport costs and tolls paid
to the city-states along the road. Larsen's reading of the archives puts the traffic at the order of a hundred tonnes of tin and
a hundred thousand textiles over a few decades *(rough)*. It is a reminder that merchant credit, partnership and branch agency
are Bronze Age institutions, not medieval Italian ones.

### 1.2 Grain to Rome: the annona

Rome under Augustus had perhaps 800,000 to a million people *(rough)*: far more than the land around it could feed. Grain came by
sea from Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa and Egypt.

| Item | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Rome's yearly grain need | about 150,000 t (Erdkamp, a minimum) to 200,000 t (Rickman) to 400,000 t (Casson) *(contested)* | [1] |
| Egypt's contribution | 20 million modii (about 135,000 t) a year, said of Augustus's time | Epitome de Caesaribus 1.6 [1] |
| Africa against Egypt | Africa fed Rome "eight months of the year", Egypt four | Josephus, Jewish War 2.383–386 [2] |
| Free grain (frumentationes) | 5 modii a month to about 200,000 male citizens under Augustus (about 80,000 t a year) | Res Gestae 15; Dio 55.10 [1] |
| Large grain ships | 340 t was common; the Isis in Lucian's Navigium perhaps 1,000 t or more *(rough)* | Casson [1] |

The state did not own the fleet. The **cura annonae**, under a prefect, bought or levied grain (much of Egypt's was tax in kind),
contracted private shipowners (**navicularii**) to carry it, and stored it in the great warehouses (horrea) at Ostia and Portus.
To get enough ships, emperors bought them in with privileges: Claudius insured shippers against storm losses and gave legal
privileges to those who built ships for the grain trade (Suetonius, Claudius 18–19); a Junian Latin who built a ship of at least
10,000 modii and carried grain to Rome for six years earned citizenship (Gaius, Institutes 1.32c). Claudius and Trajan built the
artificial harbour at Portus because the Tiber mouth could not take the big ships. Later emperors added olive oil, pork and wine
to the dole, and Aurelian (270s) replaced grain with bread.

The free grain was less than half of what the city ate; the rest was sold, by private dealers and from state stocks. So the
annona was a **hybrid**: state demand and state guarantees calling a private shipping and trading industry into being, which also
carried private cargo.

```
Bread in Rome  (assembly; a pulse at harvest; chokepoint at the harbour)
├── wheat
│   ├── Egyptian Nile farms ← the flood (one crop a year) ← tax in kind to the state granaries at Alexandria
│   ├── African estates (Tunisia, Algeria) ← rent and tax; private estates of senators and emperor
│   └── Sicily, Sardinia (earlier the main source)
├── shipping (navicularii, private) ← timber, pitch, sailcloth, crews; state privileges and insurance
│   └── sailing season (about April to October) → the whole year's supply in six months: a pulse
├── harbours: Puteoli, then Ostia and Portus ← state works; dredging; lighthouses
├── transfer: river barges up the Tiber (towed by men and oxen)
├── storage: horrea (warehouses), state and private ← stone, brick, concrete
├── milling and baking: mills turned by donkeys or slaves; bakers' guild (pistores), later state-regulated
└── distribution: the frumentatio lists (citizens, by tickets) + the market
```

Shape: an **assembly** with a **pulse** (the sailing season and the Nile flood) and a **chokepoint** (Egypt; Ostia). Whoever held
Egypt held Rome's bread, which is why Augustus kept Egypt under a prefect of his own and barred senators from entering it.

### 1.3 Oil and wine in amphorae

Amphorae, the standard clay jars for oil, wine and fish sauce, are the best evidence of ancient bulk trade because they survive
and can be traced to kilns by shape and stamp.

- **Monte Testaccio** in Rome is a hill about 35 m high made entirely of broken oil amphorae, mostly the globular **Dressel 20**
  from Baetica (the Guadalquivir valley in Spain), dumped from about AD 140 to 260 because oil-soaked sherds could not be reused.
  Estimates of how many it holds run from 20 to 25 million (a gravimetric study) to 53 million (the older, widely repeated figure)
  *(contested)*; at about 70 litres each that is 1.4 to 6 billion litres of oil over a century or so [3]. Rates at the peak are
  put at about 130,000 amphorae a year *(rough)*. It holds only one route's state-handled oil; private trade and other routes
  are on top.
- The amphora stamps and painted labels (tituli picti) name the estate, the shipper and the officials who checked weight, so the
  hill is in effect a customs archive.
- **Wine** moved from Italy to Gaul in huge volume in the late Republic (Dressel 1 amphorae, found by the hundred thousand in
  Gaul), then from Gaul, Spain and the eastern Mediterranean to Italy under the Empire, as provincial vineyards matured.
- **Fish sauce (garum)** from Spain and North Africa: salt (salt pans), fish (a seasonal tuna run: a pulse), vats, amphorae.

```
Olive oil to Rome and the army (time capital; assembly)
├── olive groves (15 to 20 years to full bearing: time capital) ← land, labour (tenants, slaves), peace
├── presses (lever and screw presses, stone weights) ← stone, timber, a mill house
├── amphora kilns on the river (Dressel 20) ← clay, fuel (wood), potters; estate stamps
├── river barges down the Guadalquivir → Hispalis (Seville) → sea shipping
├── state purchase for the annona and the frontier armies (Rhine, Britain)
└── Monte Testaccio: the dump for jars that cannot be cleaned
```

Shipwrecks give a second index. A. J. Parker's 1992 catalogue of 1,189 Mediterranean wrecks peaks in the 1st century BC; Andrew
Wilson's probability-weighted reworking moves the peak to the 1st century AD and falls sharply after [9]. Most historians read the
curve as a rough index of seaborne trade, with caveats (organic cargoes leave nothing, and sailing habits changed).

### 1.4 Mining, metals and the record in the ice

Rome's money and much of its building rested on Iberian mines: silver and lead at Carthago Nova (Cartagena) and in the Sierra
Morena, copper and silver at **Rio Tinto**, gold in the north-west (Las Médulas, worked by hydraulic mining: aqueducts brought
water to wash whole hillsides away, as Pliny describes in Natural History 33.70–78).

- Polybius, quoted by Strabo (3.2.10), says the silver mines near Carthago Nova employed **40,000 workers** and yielded the Roman
  people **25,000 drachmae a day** [7].
- At Rio Tinto a company survey in 1924 estimated about 15 million tonnes of lead-silver slag and about 1 million tonnes of copper
  slag from ancient working; other estimates of Roman slag range from 6 to 16 million tonnes depending on what is counted
  *(contested)* [6].
- **Lead in Greenland ice.** Lead smelted with silver goes into the air, and some of it reaches the Arctic. Hong, Candelone,
  Patterson and Boutron (1994) first showed Roman-era lead in Greenland ice four times the natural level [5]. McConnell and
  colleagues (2018) measured 423 m of the NGRIP core year by year from 1100 BC to AD 800: emissions rose with Carthaginian and
  Roman mining in Iberia, reached a maximum under the early Empire, tracked wars and plagues (falling with the civil wars of the
  late Republic and with the Antonine plague from AD 165), and moved with the silver content of the denarius [4]. They read it as
  evidence of **sustained growth during the first two centuries of the Empire**.

```
Silver coin (assembly; chokepoint; long skill)
├── ore: argentiferous galena (lead-silver), jarosite at Rio Tinto ← a seam (place-bound)
├── labour: slaves and convicts underground; free contractors on leased shafts (the Vipasca tablets regulate leases)
├── drainage: Archimedean screws and wheels of water-lifting worked in series by men ← timber, bronze, carpentry
├── fuel: charcoal in huge volume ← woods around the mines (deforestation shows in pollen)
├── smelting → lead bullion → cupellation (lead oxidised away, silver left) → litharge, lead (pipes, sheeting, ballast)
├── state ownership of mines, leased to contractors (publicani, then imperial procurators)
└── mints (Rome, Lugdunum) → denarii → army pay → taxes → back to the state
```

Shape: a **chokepoint** (the seam), an **assembly** (ore, fuel, drainage, labour), and **joint products** (silver and lead; lead
was a by-product the empire then used for water pipes).

### 1.5 Coin, tax and trade

The silver **denarius** (about 3.9 g, nearly pure, under Augustus) was the unit of a monetary economy that reached peasants in
many provinces. Nero cut its weight and fineness (AD 64); by the 260s the successor antoninianus had a few per cent silver
*(rough)*, and prices rose steeply. Keith Hopkins's model of "taxes and trade" [10] is the most useful mechanism for a designer:
the state taxed the rich inner provinces in money and spent it in Rome and on the frontier armies; to get coin to pay next year's
tax, the provinces had to sell goods to the places where the coin was spent; so **taxation in money forced trade** in a circuit
from the inner provinces to Rome and the frontiers and back. The same mechanism recurs in Mughal India (§3.4) and Ming China
(§6.3).

Land transport was dear. Duncan-Jones's reading of Diocletian's Price Edict (301) puts the cost of carrying a load by sea, river
and road at roughly **1 : 5 : 30 to 60** *(rough)*. Wheat carried 300 Roman miles by wagon roughly doubled in price; from
Alexandria to Rome by sea it rose by a fraction. Hence the empire's economy was a sea economy with coastal cities, and inland
grain moved only where rivers ran.

### 1.6 Slavery's role

Slavery was large in Roman Italy and smaller elsewhere. Walter Scheidel puts the slave population of Italy at its height (late
Republic, Augustus) at about **1 to 1.5 million** in a population of perhaps 6 to 7.5 million, against older figures of 2 to 3
million; for the empire as a whole he suggests about **10 per cent** as a lower bound [8] *(contested)*. Slaves worked in mines (the
worst lot), on the big estates of Italy and Sicily (the villa system the agronomists Cato, Varro and Columella write for),
in households, and in skilled trades and business, where they often managed shops, ships and money for their owners as agents
(the peculium, a fund a slave managed and could use to buy freedom). Freed slaves (liberti) were a large part of the urban
commercial class.

Its economic role cuts two ways. Slaves let the rich run estates and businesses at scale with agents they could control, and
war captives made labour cheap after the great conquests. But slave supply after the conquests depended on breeding and
frontier capture, the mines show how slavery and the most dangerous work went together, and the Italian slave estate gave way
over the 2nd to 4th centuries to tenancy (coloni), which bound free farmers to land.

### 1.7 Building: Roman concrete

Roman **opus caementicium** combined lime with volcanic ash (pozzolana, from Puteoli on the Bay of Naples) and broken stone. The
ash reacts with lime to set hard, even under water, which allowed harbour moles (Caesarea, Portus) to be cast in the sea. Its
durability comes from minerals that keep growing in the set concrete (aluminous tobermorite and phillipsite in marine concrete,
Jackson and others 2017) and from lime clasts left by mixing with hot quicklime that let cracks heal (Seymour and others 2023)
[11]. It let Rome build vaults and domes cheaply with semi-skilled labour inside brick or stone facing: the Pantheon's unreinforced
dome (about AD 126) is 43.3 m across and was the largest in the world for over a thousand years.

```
Concrete vaults (assembly; long skill at the top; increasing returns in the brickyards)
├── lime ← limestone + kilns + fuel (wood) ← quarries and woods
├── pozzolana ← volcanic ash pits near Naples (a regional chokepoint; elsewhere crushed brick as a substitute)
├── aggregate (caementa) ← rubble, tufa, light pumice for upper dome courses
├── brick facing ← brickyards (many owned by senators and the emperor; stamped) ← clay, fuel
├── timber centring (formwork) ← carpenters, large timber
└── water; labour: gangs of free workers and slaves under contractors (redemptores)
```

### 1.8 Greece: silver, grain and the invention of coin

- **Coin** was first struck in Lydia and Ionia in the late 7th century BC, in electrum (a gold-silver alloy), then in pure gold
  and silver under Croesus. Greek cities adopted it fast; by the 5th century the Athenian silver **tetradrachm** ("owl") was the
  common currency of the Aegean.
- **Laurion.** Athens's silver came from the mines of Laurion in Attica. A rich strike in 483/2 BC was spent, at Themistocles's
  urging, on a fleet: the navy grew to about 200 triremes by 480 and won at Salamis [12] (Herodotus 7.144). The mines were
  leased by the state to contractors and worked by slaves; Thucydides (7.27) says more than 20,000 slaves, most of them
  craftsmen, deserted Athens when the Spartans fortified Decelea in 413. A fleet of rowers was paid in coin from a mine dug by
  slaves: a single loop of silver, labour and power.
- **Grain.** Athens imported grain from the Black Sea (the Bosporan kingdom in Crimea), Egypt and Sicily. Demosthenes (20.31–32)
  says some 400,000 medimnoi (roughly 12,000 to 16,000 t) a year came from the Bosporus [13]; Athens controlled the Hellespont
  route, laws forbade Athenians to ship grain anywhere but Athens, and grain wardens policed the market. How much Athens depended
  on imports is debated (Garnsey argued less than once thought).

### 1.9 What kind of economy was Rome's?

Historians have argued for a century between a "primitivist" view (Moses Finley: an economy of status, not markets; cities
consumed and did not produce) and a "modernist" view. The current consensus sits between: markets for grain, oil, wine, labour and
credit were real and integrated across the Mediterranean (Peter Temin argues for a market economy), with some per capita growth in
the late Republic and early Empire, read from shipwrecks, ice-core lead, animal bones growing larger, and building, but on an
organic energy base with no sustained rise in productivity. **The proxies themselves are a lesson:** Rome's output is measured from
what it threw away and what drifted into the air.

| Proxy | Measures | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Greenland ice lead [4][5] | smelting of lead and silver | Iberian mines dominate; one industry |
| Shipwreck counts [9] | seaborne trade | dating spread; organic cargoes vanish |
| Monte Testaccio, amphora finds [3] | oil and wine flows | one route; counting method |
| Coin hoards and fineness | money in use; state finance | hoards mark trouble, not wealth |
| Animal bone size, building inscriptions | diet, investment | regional |

## 2. China

### 2.1 The Han salt and iron monopolies

China smelted **cast iron** in blast furnaces from about the 5th century BC, more than 1,500 years before Europe did at any scale,
and learned to make steel by decarburising cast iron ("stir-frying", chaogang, a puddling process) in the Han. In 117 BC Emperor
Wu, needing revenue for wars against the Xiongnu, approved a **state monopoly of salt and iron** run by Sang Hongyang, a
merchant's son turned minister [15]. The Han shu lists about four dozen iron offices in the commanderies *(rough)*; archaeology
shows big state ironworks a few hundred metres outside Han city walls, worked by conscripts and convicts (Wagner [15]). A wine
monopoly followed.

In 81 BC the court held a debate, written up by Huan Kuan as the **Discourses on Salt and Iron** (Yantie lun): Confucian
"worthies" argued that the monopolies competed with the people for profit, made poor, brittle tools that peasants were forced to
buy at fixed prices, and corrupted officials; the ministers replied that they paid for defence and kept great merchants from
dominating the market [15]. The wine monopoly was dropped; salt and iron stayed, with interruptions, and the salt monopoly became
a fiscal pillar of Chinese states for two thousand years. It is the clearest early case of **a state taking a chokepoint
(salt, the edge-tool metal) as revenue**, and of the arguments that follow.

```
Han iron tools (assembly; increasing returns at the state works; chokepoint by law)
├── ore + fuel (charcoal, later coal in the north) ← mines and woods near the works
├── blast furnaces (cast iron) with bellows driven by men, horses, then water (Du Shi's water-powered bellows, AD 31)
├── moulds (clay, then iron moulds for mass casting of ploughshares, hoes)
├── malleablising cast iron by annealing; steel by chaogang ← skilled iron masters
├── labour: conscripts, convicts, hired workers
└── sale at fixed prices through state offices → peasants' ploughs, hoes, sickles; the army's weapons
```

### 2.2 The Song "economic revolution"

Between about 900 and 1200 China's economy changed so much that historians (Shiba Yoshinobu, Mark Elvin, Robert Hartwell) call it
a revolution. Its parts reinforced each other:

| Part | What changed | Figures |
|---|---|---|
| **Rice** | The south (Yangzi delta, Fujian, Jiangxi) overtook the north; wet-rice paddies with transplanting, irrigation by treadle and noria pumps, fertiliser of night soil and river mud. | China's population passed about 100 million around 1100 (household registers) *(rough)* |
| **Champa rice** | Early-ripening, drought-tolerant rice from Champa (central Vietnam), via Fujian. In 1012, after drought in the lower Yangzi and Huai, Emperor Zhenzong had 30,000 bushels of seed sent for distribution [20]. It allowed a second crop or a crop on higher, drier land. | Ho Ping-ti (1956) made it central to the doubling of population; genomic studies now debate its origin [20] |
| **Canals** | The Grand Canal, begun by the Sui (from 605), joined the Yangzi to the Yellow River and the northern capitals; the Yuan extended it to Beijing. | about 1,776 km; in Ming and Qing it carried 3 to 4 million shi of tribute grain north a year [19] *(rough)* |
| **Coal and iron** | Coal replaced charcoal in northern ironworks and heated the capital, Kaifeng. | iron output in 1078: 75,000 t (Hartwell 1962) to 125,000 t (his later figure); Wagner calls any total unknowable [16][17] *(contested)* |
| **Money** | Copper cash minted in billions; iron cash in Sichuan; then paper money. | mint output peaked at about 5 to 6 million strings (5 to 6 billion coins) a year around 1080 *(rough)* [18] |
| **Commerce** | The walled ward-and-market system of Tang cities gave way to open streets of shops; night markets; guilds (hang); merchant shipping to Southeast Asia and India from Quanzhou and Guangzhou. | Kaifeng and later Hangzhou near a million people *(rough)* |
| **Print** | Woodblock printing made books, almanacs, agricultural manuals and the examinations' texts cheap. | |

**Iron and coal (Hartwell's estimates).** Hartwell built his figures from Song tax records of iron delivered to the state, assumed
the deliveries were a 10 per cent tax on part of the industry, and multiplied: 75,000 tons in his 1962 article, about 125,000 in
later work, a sixfold rise since 800 and comparable to (or above) all of Britain's output in 1788, which he put at about 76,000
tons. Donald Wagner accepts the qualitative picture (a large, partly private, coal-using iron industry in Hebei and Henan, two
prefectures of which supplied over 75 per cent of iron delivered to the state in 1078) but argues that the multiplier is
unsupported and **no reliable total can be calculated** from the sources [17]. Wagner's per capita conversion of the 125,000 t
figure is about 1.2 kg a head. The iron went into weapons and armour for huge standing armies, farm tools, salt-boiling pans,
nails, chains for bridges, Sichuan's iron coins, and building. Hartwell's own later work shows output fell after the Jurchen
conquest of the north (1126), the Mongol wars, and the Yellow River's change of course (1194) which ruined the region.

```
Northern Song iron (assembly; paradigm switch from charcoal to coal; increasing returns)
├── ore ← Cizhou and Xingzhou (Hebei) and other prefectures
├── fuel: coal (mined in shafts) replacing charcoal as northern woods ran out ← mining labour; carriage by cart and canal
├── blast furnaces; bellows driven by water wheels (described by the Yuan writer Wang Zhen, 1313)
├── refining to wrought iron and steel ← skilled masters; private works under state licence and quota
├── markets: the state (armies of over a million soldiers), Kaifeng's builders and shops, farmers
└── transport to Kaifeng by the canal network (Bian canal)
```

**Paper money.** In Sichuan the coins were of iron and so heavy that carrying them for trade was absurd (a string of iron cash
weighed several kilograms *(rough)*). Merchant houses there began issuing deposit receipts, **jiaozi**, around 1000; the state
suppressed the private issuers and took the business over in 1023 to 1024 with an official Jiaozi office at Chengdu [18]. Notes
had a term (at first three years), a fixed issue and a cash reserve. Over-issue to pay for war led to depreciation; Huizong
replaced jiaozi with qianyin in 1105; the Southern Song issued **huizi** from the 1160s, which depreciated badly under the
pressure of war with the Jin and the Mongols; the Yuan made paper the main currency, and then over-issued it too. The Ming
returned in the 15th century to copper and, above all, to **uncoined silver by weight**, which set up the great silver demand of
§6.3. The sequence is a complete arc: heavy money → private receipts → state monopoly of issue → fiscal over-issue → loss of
trust → a return to metal.

### 2.3 Porcelain: Jingdezhen

Jingdezhen in Jiangxi became the world's porcelain city from the Song on, and in the Ming and Qing it held the imperial kilns
(the Yuqichang, from 1369 or 1402) beside hundreds of private ones. Porcelain needs two raw materials found near Jingdezhen:
**kaolin** (china clay, named after Gaoling hill nearby) and **petuntse** (baidunzi, china stone, a feldspathic rock), crushed in
water-powered trip hammers along the streams and shipped to town in white bricks. Blue-and-white ware, which began under the
Yuan, used **cobalt** imported from Persia (mined at Qamsar near Kashan), "Mohammedan blue"; when the supply was cut, Chinese
potters used duller local cobalt [21]. Song Yingxing's technical encyclopedia (Tiangong kaiwu, 1637) says a single cup passes
through seventy-two hands before it is done; the French Jesuit François Xavier d'Entrecolles, writing from the town in 1712,
reported some 3,000 kilns and a population near a million *(reported, probably exaggerated)*.

```
Jingdezhen blue-and-white (assembly; long skill split across many specialists; increasing returns; one import chokepoint)
├── body: kaolin (Gaoling) + petuntse (china stone) ← quarries in the hills; water-powered trip hammers; settling tanks; bricks
├── glaze: china stone + glaze ash (lime burned with fern) ← lime kilns
├── cobalt: Persian (Kashan) via the Mongol and maritime trade, or local cobalt (dull) ← a substitute with a trade-off
├── throwing, moulding, trimming, painting, glazing: each a separate workshop and trade
├── saggars (protective clay boxes) ← coarse clay; a trade of its own
├── firing: egg-shaped or dragon kilns ← pine wood in enormous quantity (fuel drawn from ever further away)
├── transport: the Chang river → Poyang lake → the Yangzi or overland to Guangzhou and Quanzhou ports
└── markets: the court (imperial orders), the empire, Japan, Southeast Asia, the Islamic world (made to Persian taste),
    after 1600 Europe through the Portuguese and the Dutch (millions of pieces in the 17th century, rough)
```

The imperial kiln shows a pattern seen again in India: the state as **a quality-setting customer** inside a private industry,
commissioning designs, drawing the best workers and passing failures to the private market.

### 2.4 Silk

Silk was China's oldest luxury export and a tax good (households paid tax in silk cloth for centuries).

```
Silk cloth (time capital; long skill; joint products in the mulberry-dike loop)
├── mulberry trees (years to grow; leaves picked several times a season) ← land, often on dikes and field edges
├── silkworms (Bombyx mori): egg cards kept through winter; rearing in a warm room for about a month; women's work
│   └── leaves fed by the hour in the last instars: a labour pulse
├── cocoons → boiling, reeling the filament (hours of skilled work; a reeling frame) → raw silk
├── throwing (twisting), dyeing ← dyes (safflower, indigo, sappanwood imported from Southeast Asia)
├── weaving: plain cloth in peasant homes; damasks, brocades on drawlooms (two operators) in Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing
│   └── imperial weaving bureaus (zhizao ju) in those cities under Ming and Qing
└── sale: tax silk; markets in Jiangnan; export by the overland routes, by sea, and after 1570 for American silver at Manila
```

In the Pearl River delta and around Lake Tai the **mulberry-dike fish-pond** system (sangji yutang) closed a loop: ponds dug for
fish, the spoil heaped into dikes planted with mulberry, mulberry leaves fed to silkworms, silkworm droppings and waste fed to the
fish, and the fertile pond mud dredged back onto the dikes. It is a working example of the **joint products** shape at landscape
scale (the Huzhou system is a FAO Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System).

### 2.5 Why no industrial revolution followed

Song China had coal-fired iron, water-powered machinery (including a water-powered spinning frame for hemp described by Wang Zhen
in 1313), paper money, printing and a national market. The question of why it did not go on to sustained, machine-driven growth
(Joseph Needham's question) has many answers [22]:

| Explanation | Who | The argument | Weak points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shocks and the loss of the north** | Hartwell; many | Jurchen and Mongol conquest, Yellow River floods and plague broke the northern iron and coal region; the economy's centre moved south, where coal was far from the core | explains Song decline, not the later Ming and Qing |
| **High-level equilibrium trap** | Mark Elvin (1973) | Population growth made labour so cheap and so many skills so refined that labour-saving machines never paid; surplus per head was too low to invest | Jiangnan wages and invention in the Ming–Qing are disputed |
| **Coal and colonies** | Kenneth Pomeranz (2000) | Jiangnan around 1750 looked like England in living standards and market development; England diverged because its coal lay near its industry and its colonies supplied land-hungry goods (cotton, sugar, timber) and a market | Jiangnan's productivity per worker was lower (Brenner and Isett; Broadberry, Guan and Li) |
| **Different political economy** | Rosenthal and Wong (2011) | China's big, peaceful, low-tax state favoured dispersed rural industry with cheap labour; Europe's wars drove capital and industry into walled cities where labour was dear and capital cheap | a model, hard to test |
| **Relative prices** | Robert Allen | Machines pay where labour is dear and fuel cheap; in China labour was cheap and fuel (in the south) dear | |
| **Knowledge and institutions** | Justin Lin (1995); Joel Mokyr | Examinations drew talent into classics, not experiment; no culture of open, cumulative useful knowledge like Europe's | China had a large technical literature |
| **Early divergence** | Broadberry, Guan and Li (2018) | China's GDP per head peaked in the Northern Song and fell; it was behind Italy by about 1300 and Britain by about 1400 | reconstructions are thin *(contested)* |

The common ground: high output and wide markets on an **organic** base (Wrigley's term, see 02zzi §1) are not enough; the switch
needs cheap concentrated energy where the industry is and a reason (dear labour) to replace people with machines.

## 3. India

### 3.1 Cotton textiles and the Indian Ocean

India was the world's great cloth maker from antiquity to about 1800: Gujarat (coarse and printed cottons, to the Red Sea, East
Africa and Southeast Asia), the Coromandel coast (painted chintz, dyed with chay root), Bengal (fine muslins of Dhaka, from a
local short-staple cotton, phuti karpas) and the Punjab and Deccan. Roman authors complain of the cost of Eastern luxuries: Pliny
says India, China and Arabia took 100 million sesterces a year from the empire (Natural History 12.84) [42].

In the 17th and 18th centuries Indian cottons became a global good. Textiles made up about two-thirds or more of the value of the
English East India Company's imports from Asia for about a century after the 1660s [23]; the fashion for calico in England led
wool and silk interests to win the **Calico Acts** of 1700 and 1721, which banned imports of printed calico and then most cotton
sales [23]. Indian cloth was also a currency of the Atlantic slave trade: it was among the main goods European traders exchanged
for captives in West Africa (Riello [23]). Maddison's reconstructions give India about a quarter of world output in 1700
*(contested; an output share, not an industrial one)* [24]; Broadberry and Gupta show Indian output per head falling behind
Britain's from 1600 [23].

```
Indian painted and printed cotton (assembly; long skill; dispersed household production under merchant advances)
├── raw cotton (many local varieties; tree and annual) ← black-soil fields (Deccan, Gujarat)
├── ginning on the churka (a roller gin) → bowing (carding with a bow) → spinning on the charkha (women at home)
│   └── yarn markets; thread counts set by spinners' skill (Dhaka muslin: extremely fine hand-spun yarn)
├── weaving: caste weavers on pit looms in villages and towns
├── bleaching: washermen (dhobi) in river water, with lye, sun ← river and fuel
├── dyeing and printing: mordants (alum, iron) fix colours; madder and chay root (red), indigo (blue), turmeric, pomegranate
│   └── resist dyeing and hand painting (kalamkari) ← long skill; water quality matters
├── finance: merchants advance cash to weavers before the season (dadni) ← brokers, bankers (shroffs) and bills (hundi)
├── ports: Surat, Masulipatnam, Pulicat, Hughli, Calicut
└── markets: Southeast Asia (for spices, at Melaka and Aceh), East Africa (for gold and ivory), the Red Sea and Gulf, Europe,
    West Africa (for captives)
```

The **dadni** system is proto-industry in the 02zzi §3.6 sense, centuries old: merchants advanced capital to dispersed household
producers and collected the product. It also shows a credit network (shroffs, hundis) carrying the whole industry. The
monsoon set its rhythm: ships sailed east and west on the seasonal winds, so cloth had to be ready by a fixed date (a **pulse**).

### 3.2 Indigo and dyes

Indigo (from the plant Indigofera tinctoria) gave India's blue: the leaves were steeped and fermented in a vat, the liquor beaten
in a second vat to oxidise it, and the blue sediment settled, pressed and dried into cakes, a light, high-value product ideal for
long-distance trade. The best qualities came from Bayana near Agra and from Sarkhej near Ahmedabad. In Europe indigo competed with
**woad**, made from a local plant by a slow, smelly process; woad growers won bans and heavy duties on indigo in several German
states and France in the 16th and 17th centuries, which a stronger dye eventually broke. It is the **substitutes** shape across
continents, decided as much by politics as by chemistry. Under the British, indigo planting moved to Bengal and Bihar plantations
with forced contracts, which ended in the Indigo Revolt of 1859 to 1860; synthetic indigo (BASF, 1897) ended the trade.

### 3.3 Wootz steel

South India and Sri Lanka made **crucible steel**: iron and a carbon source (wood, leaves) sealed in small clay crucibles, held at
heat for hours and slowly cooled, giving a cake of high-carbon steel (about 1 to 2 per cent carbon) with a fine banded structure
[26]. Crucible remains at Kodumanal in Tamil Nadu date to about the 3rd century BC to early centuries AD; Sri Lankan sites
(Anuradhapura, Samanalawewa, where Gillian Juleff found wind-blown furnaces on hill ridges driven by the monsoon) date to the
first millennium AD. The cakes were traded across the Arabian Sea to Persia and Syria and forged at low heat into the patterned
blades Europeans called "Damascus steel". Golconda (in the Deccan) was a famous source in the 17th century. The secret was the
whole process, not one step: the right ore with traces of vanadium and other elements, the crucible recipe, slow cooling, and
forging at a low temperature (Verhoeven and Pendray); when the supply of the particular ores and the knowledge broke in the 18th
and 19th centuries, the blades could not be made [26]. It is a pure **long skill** good with a **chokepoint** in an ore.

The Delhi iron pillar (Gupta, about AD 400, over 6 tonnes of forge-welded wrought iron that has barely rusted) shows the scale
Indian smiths could forge without blast furnaces.

### 3.4 The Mughal economy

The Mughal empire (1526 to the 18th century) at its height ruled perhaps 110 to 150 million people *(contested)* [25].

- **Land revenue in cash.** Under Akbar the revenue was assessed on measured land by crop and average prices (the zabt or dahsala
  system), at about a third to a half of the produce, and collected mostly **in money** (Irfan Habib [25]). Peasants had to sell
  grain to pay, which pulled the countryside into markets and supported a dense network of market towns (qasbas) and grain
  merchants. Moosvi's statistical reconstruction of the Ain-i Akbari puts the assessed revenue (jama) around 1595 at roughly 90 to
  100 million rupees *(rough)* [25].
- **Assignments.** Revenue was assigned to officers (mansabdars) as jagirs, rotated every few years so no one could root himself in
  a region; the officers kept cavalry and spent in the cities. The system pulled the surplus to the court and the camps, which
  consumed luxuries made in imperial workshops (karkhanas) and by city artisans.
- **Carrying.** Grain moved over land by the **banjaras**, caste caravanners with pack bullocks in herds of thousands; the
  Englishman Peter Mundy reported a banjara train of about 14,000 laden oxen in 1632 *(reported)*. By river, the Ganges and Indus
  carried bulk.
- **Money.** The silver rupee (Sher Shah, 1540s; about 11 g) and copper dam. India exported cloth and imported silver and gold:
  it was a "sink" for the world's precious metals, and Mughal mint output swelled with American silver in the 17th century.
- **Bankers.** Shroffs tested and exchanged coins, remitted money by **hundi** (bills of exchange drawn on correspondents in
  other cities), lent to officers against revenue assignments and to merchants. Great banking houses (the Jagat Seths of Bengal,
  18th century) financed governments.

The Mughal economy is a direct test of Hopkins's mechanism: a state that taxes in money forces sales, sustains markets and draws
in silver. It also shows **redistribution through a court** (02zzi §3.3) at the scale of a subcontinent, with markets everywhere
beneath it.

## 4. The Islamic world

### 4.1 Networks, camels and the funduq

From the 8th century a single cultural and legal zone ran from al-Andalus to Central Asia and the Indian Ocean coasts, with a
common language of trade (Arabic), common law on contracts (with school differences), gold dinars and silver dirhams, and a
pilgrimage that moved people and goods every year to Mecca. Baghdad, Basra and Siraf (on the Gulf) were the hubs under the
Abbasids; after the 10th century Cairo, the Red Sea and Aden took over the India trade (the Karimi merchants of Egypt carried
spices and pepper from Aden to Cairo from the 12th to the 15th century).

- **The camel and the wheel.** Richard Bulliet argued that from about the 3rd to the 6th century the camel, with a better
  saddle, displaced wheeled carts across the Middle East and North Africa because it was cheaper per load and needed no roads
  [31]. The region's land transport became pack caravans: long strings of camels (hundreds to thousands on the big routes) moving
  in stages between watered stops.
- **The caravanserai, khan and funduq** were walled inns on the routes and in the cities: a courtyard for animals and goods,
  rooms above for merchants, often a single guarded gate. In cities the funduq was also a warehouse, a customs point and a
  residence for foreign merchants of one nation (the Venetians had theirs at Alexandria; Venice in turn copied the form in the
  Fondaco dei Tedeschi for German merchants). Many were built and maintained by **waqf** (a perpetual charitable endowment), so
  that a road's inns, markets and fountains were financed by rents from endowed property.
- **Market law.** The **muhtasib** (market inspector) enforced weights, measures, prices of necessities and honest dealing
  (hisba): an official who made a market's rules visible and enforced them on the spot.

### 4.2 Paper

The Chinese invented paper (Cai Lun's improvement is dated AD 105); it reached Samarkand by the 8th century (the story that
Chinese prisoners taken at the Battle of Talas in 751 taught it there is a legend). A paper mill worked in Baghdad by about 794 to
795 under the Barmakid viziers; Damascus, Cairo and Fez followed; al-Idrisi in the 12th century praises the paper of Xàtiva in
al-Andalus; Christian Europe's first major centre was Fabriano in Italy by the 1270s [27]. Jonathan Bloom (Paper Before Print,
2001) argues that paper transformed the Islamic world before printing: cheap writing material made bureaucracy, accounting,
commercial letters, books in quantity (Baghdad had a market of stationers and booksellers) and new kinds of mathematics practical.

```
Paper, Islamic method (assembly; increasing returns with water power; a substitute for papyrus and parchment)
├── fibre: linen and hemp rags (the Islamic and European method; China used mulberry bark, bamboo, hemp) ← a used-cloth trade
├── retting and rotting the rags in water → beating to pulp: mortars, then water- or animal-powered trip hammers ← a river
├── forming sheets: a mould dipped in the vat; couching; pressing; drying ← skilled vatmen
├── sizing with starch (Islamic) or gelatine (Fabriano) so ink does not run; burnishing with a stone
└── users: chanceries, merchants (letters, accounts, bills), scholars and copyists; later printers in Europe
```

Paper is an information good's material (02zzi §1): its spread is the spread of the ability to keep records and write bills.

### 4.3 Sugar

Cane sugar was first made into crystals in India (Sanskrit khanda, the root of "candy"), then in Sasanian Persia (Khuzestan). Under
Islamic rule cane spread to Iraq, the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, Cyprus, Sicily and al-Andalus. Andrew Watson's thesis of an
**"Arab agricultural revolution"** (1974) holds that Muslim rule spread a package of crops (sugar, rice, cotton, citrus,
sorghum, spinach, aubergine) and of summer irrigation, raising output and population; critics (Michael Decker 2009 and others)
argue that many of these crops were already in the Roman and Sasanian world and the change was smaller [29] *(contested)*.

Egypt under the Fatimids and Mamluks had a large sugar industry, with refineries in Fustat and sugar exported to Europe. Sugar is
the chain that most clearly passed from the Islamic world to the European colonial economy: Crusader states and Venetian Cyprus,
then Portuguese Madeira (1450s), São Tomé, Brazil (from the 1550s the world's leading producer) and the English and French
Caribbean (from the 1640s), where it became the plantation system run on enslaved African labour.

```
Sugar (assembly; increasing returns at the mill; a harvest pulse that cannot wait; fuel-hungry)
├── cane (12 to 18 months to mature; needs heat and water) ← irrigation (Egypt, Iraq) or rain (Brazil, Caribbean)
├── harvest: cane must be crushed within a day or two of cutting or the juice spoils → mill and field tied together
├── crushing: edge-runner stones or roller mills driven by animals or water ← timber, stone, iron, millwrights
├── boiling: a train of copper kettles over furnaces ← fuel in huge amounts (woods cleared; later bagasse burned)
├── purging and refining: clay cone moulds drain molasses → loaf sugar; molasses → rum (later)
├── labour: in Egypt peasants and wage workers; in the Atlantic, enslaved Africans in gangs
└── markets: the court and cities of the Islamic world, then Europe; sugar went from a medicine and luxury to a mass food
```

The cane's need to be crushed at once is why sugar cannot be made by dispersed peasants with distant mills: field, mill and
boiling house must be one unit under one command. That technical fact is why it became the plantation.

### 4.4 Qanats

A **qanat** is a gently sloping tunnel that taps groundwater under an alluvial fan and leads it out by gravity to fields
kilometres away, dug from a line of vertical shafts. It began in Iran in the early first millennium BC and spread to Oman (aflaj),
Central Asia (karez), North Africa (foggara), and al-Andalus. The Qasabeh qanat at Gonabad is about 2,500 years old, with a mother
well some 300 m deep and 427 shafts over about 33 km [28]. Iran has had tens of thousands of qanats: one count gives about 37,000
still in use in the 2000s, out of many more built [28] *(counts and total lengths vary widely)*. UNESCO inscribed eleven as "The
Persian Qanat" in 2016.

- **Water by time shares.** Water from a qanat was owned in shares of time: each owner had the flow for so many hours in a cycle
  of days, measured with a water clock (a bowl with a hole, sinking in a basin), administered by a water master. Shares were
  sold, inherited and pledged.
- **Specialists.** Digging and cleaning were done by a hereditary craft of diggers (muqannis), paid by the owners.
- **Shape:** **time capital** (years to dig), **increasing returns** (one tunnel serves a village), a **commons** within the
  owners, a **long skill** (surveying the gradient underground), and a **chokepoint** (the village dies if it collapses).

### 4.5 Credit and partnership

| Instrument | What it was | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| **suftaja** | a letter by which a merchant paid money in one city and had it repaid in another, by a correspondent of the issuer | moved value without moving coin across robber-ridden roads; jurists debated it because the lender gained the benefit of safe carriage |
| **hawala** | a legal transfer of a debt from one debtor to another (assignment) | let debts circulate between merchants; the root of the modern informal transfer system of that name |
| **sakk** | a written order to pay (the word gave Europe "cheque") | payment by paper in a city among known bankers |
| **qirad / mudaraba** | one partner supplies capital, the other travels and trades; profit shared, losses on capital | capital met skill without interest; very like the later Italian commenda (Udovitch [30]) |
| **shirka** | a partnership of several who all contribute | joint firms |

**Who enforced them?** Avner Greif's study of the 11th-century **Maghribi traders** of the Cairo Geniza argued that a coalition of
Jewish merchants enforced honesty among agents by collective reputation: cheat one, and none would employ you [30]. Edwards and
Ogilvie (2012) answered that no coalition can be shown, that the traders used courts and dealt with non-members; Greif replied,
partly conceding that legal enforcement was used too; Jessica Goldberg (2012) argues reputation worked, but through networks of
reciprocal agency rather than a closed coalition [30]. The debate is exactly the question of OPEN L-106 (who enforces a promise
when the engine does not): reputation, courts and kin all played a part, in a mix that changed with distance.

## 5. The Americas and Africa

### 5.1 The Inca: an empire without markets or money

The Inca state (Tawantinsuyu, about 1438 to 1533) ruled perhaps 6 to 14 million people *(contested)* along the Andes, from
Ecuador to central Chile, with no money, no writing in the usual sense, no draft animals but the llama, and little market exchange
at the imperial level [32].

- **Labour as tax (mit'a).** Households owed the state turns of labour, not goods: farming state and temple lands, herding state
  llamas, building, soldiering, carrying. In return the state fed the workers while they worked, with food and maize beer (chicha)
  at feasts: hospitality as the form of payment. Some groups were moved wholesale to new lands (mitmaqkuna).
- **Cloth as wealth.** Fine cloth (qompi), woven by "chosen women" (aqllakuna) and specialist men, was the state's main gift and
  store of value, given to soldiers, lords and allies; an army's loyalty was partly bought in cloth.
- **Storage.** The state built rows of storehouses (**qollqa**) on hillsides near its centres, where wind and cold kept the
  contents. Huánuco Pampa had 497 qollqa, mostly holding highland tubers (50 to 80 per cent of space) and some maize [32];
  Cotapachi in the Cochabamba valley had about 2,400 *(reported)* (D'Altroy and Earle 1985). Food kept for years as **chuño**
  (potatoes freeze-dried by night frost and day sun) and **ch'arki** (dried llama meat).
- **Roads.** The Qhapaq Ñan ran some 30,000 to 40,000 km (estimates range from 23,000 to 60,000) [32], with way stations
  (tambos) a day apart, stocked from the qollqa, and relay runners (chasqui). Records of labour owed and goods stored were kept on
  knotted cords (**khipu**).
- **Vertical archipelago.** John Murra showed how Andean communities held land in several ecological zones at different
  altitudes (tubers high, maize in valleys, coca and cotton lower) and moved goods between them within the kin group or state,
  instead of trading for them [32].

D'Altroy and Earle's distinction between **staple finance** (the state paid in food and cloth from its own stores) and **wealth
finance** (a state pays with valuables or money that people trade for what they need) names the choice. The Inca built an
empire entirely on staple finance and labour taxes, with local barter beneath. It is the strongest case that a large, complex
society can coordinate without markets, if it has storage, roads, records and a labour obligation. The Spanish conquest found the
qollqa full.

```
Inca staple finance (redistribution; time capital in terraces and stores; pulse at harvest)
├── land: state, temple and community fields (the community worked all three in turn)
├── labour: mit'a turns per household, recorded on khipu ← kurakas (local lords) organise
├── terraces and irrigation canals (andenes) ← mit'a labour over generations
├── processing: chuño, ch'arki, maize for chicha ← frost, sun, women's labour
├── storage: qollqa on hillsides ← stone, ventilation; at every provincial centre
├── carrying: people and llama caravans (a llama carries about 25 to 30 kg) along the Qhapaq Ñan
└── outflow: feasts for workers, rations for soldiers and builders, famine relief, cloth gifts
```

### 5.2 The Aztecs: tribute and markets

The Aztec Triple Alliance (Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, Tlacopan; about 1428 to 1521) combined **tribute** from conquered provinces with
some of the largest **markets** of their time.

- **Tribute.** The Codex Mendoza (about 1541) records the tribute of 38 or 39 provinces [33]: maize, beans, amaranth, chillies,
  cacao, cotton cloaks (by the tens of thousands, *rough*), warriors' costumes, feathers, jade, gold, copper axes, paper, lime,
  timber, by fixed schedules (yearly or twice yearly). Tribute fed the palaces, the warriors and the temples, and was in turn
  redistributed and sold.
- **Tlatelolco.** Cortés (second letter, 1520) described the market of Tlatelolco, the twin city of Tenochtitlan, as twice the
  size of Salamanca's square, with "more than sixty thousand" people buying and selling each day; other estimates put daily
  attendance at 20,000 to 25,000, with larger crowds on the five-day market day [33] *(the 60,000 is a conquistador's estimate)*.
  Goods were sold by count and measure, in streets by kind, under **judges who sat in the market** to settle disputes and punish
  fraud.
- **Money.** Cacao beans for small sums; cotton cloaks (**quachtli**) for large, worth 65 to 300 beans depending on quality [33];
  copper axe-shaped blanks and quills of gold dust as well.
- **Merchants.** The **pochteca**, a hereditary merchant guild, traded far beyond the empire's borders, acted as spies, had their
  own courts, and were watched with suspicion by the warrior nobility.
- **Carriers.** With no draft animals or wheels, all land carrying was on human backs: porters (tlamemeh) carried about 23 kg
  about 20 to 30 km a day (Hassig). Drennan (1984) showed this made it uneconomic to move staple maize more than a few hundred
  kilometres by land, so bulk food came from close by and by canoe across the lake system, while luxuries came from far.
- **Chinampas.** In the shallow freshwater lakes of Xochimilco and Chalco, farmers built long raised beds of mud and vegetation
  between canals, anchored by willows; the canals kept roots watered and their muck renewed fertility; seedlings were raised in
  nurseries and transplanted, giving several crops a year. At their height chinampas covered perhaps 9,000 to 9,500 hectares
  [33] *(rough)*; Tenochtitlan's population is estimated anywhere from about 40,000 to over 200,000 *(contested)*.

The Aztec economy shows that tribute, markets and specialised merchants can coexist and feed each other: tribute goods reached
the market, market cloaks served as money, and the state protected the market's peace.

### 5.3 Trans-Saharan gold and salt

West Africa held the Old World's richest gold fields: **Bambuk** (between the Senegal and Falémé), **Bure** (upper Niger) and
later the **Akan** forest (modern Ghana, from the 14th to 15th century). The Sahara's middle held rock salt, at **Taghaza** and
later **Taoudenni**. The savanna and forest to the south had no salt and the gold; North Africa and the Mediterranean had the
demand for gold. With the camel in common use from the early centuries AD and Muslim merchants from the 8th, a trade crossed the
desert for nearly a thousand years [34].

- **Scale.** At its height perhaps two-thirds of the gold circulating in the medieval Mediterranean came from West Africa
  *(rough)* [34]; output estimates range from about one to several tonnes a year *(contested)*. The gold was minted into dinars
  at Sijilmasa and elsewhere, and studies of Byzantine and later European coinage trace West African gold back to at least the
  9th century [34].
- **The chokepoint and its toll.** Al-Bakri (1068) says the king of Ghana took a dinar on every donkey-load of salt coming in and
  two on every load going out, and kept all gold **nuggets** for himself, leaving gold dust to the people, so that gold "would
  not become abundant and lose its value" (Levtzion and Hopkins, Corpus of Early Arabic Sources). A state controlling supply to
  keep the price up: an economist's cartel, described in the 11th century.
- **Salt.** Ibn Battuta reached Taghaza in 1352 and found a village with houses and a mosque built of blocks of salt roofed with
  camel skins, worked by the slaves of a Massufa lord; a camel carried two great slabs [34], each up to about 90 kg *(rough)*.
  Salt fetched its weight in gold at the southern end only in legend, but its price multiplied many times over the crossing.
- **Mansa Musa.** The Mali ruler's pilgrimage through Cairo in 1324 spent and gave away so much gold that al-Umari, writing about
  twelve years later, said the gold price in Cairo had still not recovered. The tonnage often quoted (12 to 18 tonnes) is a
  modern guess *(rough)*.
- **Other goods.** North to south: salt, copper, horses, cloth, glass beads, books, and **cowrie shells** from the Maldives,
  carried by way of Cairo, which became small change in West Africa. South to north: gold, captives, ivory, ostrich feathers,
  hides; within the south, kola nuts from the forest.
- **Towns.** Sijilmasa, Awdaghust, Koumbi Saleh, Walata, Timbuktu, Gao, Djenné; caravan merchants and local scholars; credit and
  partnerships on the Islamic model.

```
Gold for the Mediterranean (chokepoint; joint dependence; a pulse with the seasons)
├── gold: alluvial washing and shallow shafts in Bambuk, Bure, Akan, worked by farmers in the dry season ← local lords' rights
├── control: Ghana, then Mali, then Songhay taxing the trade at the savanna edge; Wangara (Mande) traders inland
├── salt: Taghaza / Taoudenni mines worked by slaves ← water and food brought in (no food grows there)
├── camels, guides, water: Saharan nomads (Sanhaja, Massufa, later Tuareg) as carriers and protectors (for a fee)
├── desert ports (Walata, Timbuktu) and northern termini (Sijilmasa, Tlemcen, Tripoli, Cairo)
├── mints: dinars (Almoravid, Fatimid), later European gold (Genoese and Venetian coins)
└── the Mediterranean's demand for gold coin; the Islamic and Christian states' wars and trade
```

From the 15th century the Portuguese sailed round to the Gold Coast (Elmina, 1482) and drew the gold south to the sea; from the
mid-16th century American gold and silver far outstripped West African supply [34].

### 5.4 Great Zimbabwe and the Swahili coast

**Great Zimbabwe** (about 1100 to 1450) was the capital of a state on the Zimbabwe plateau, with massive dry-stone enclosures
built without mortar. Its wealth was in **cattle**, herded seasonally under the court, and in control of the gold trade: gold
was won by many small-scale miners on the plateau and carried to the coast at **Sofala**, then north by sea to **Kilwa** [35].
Population estimates have fallen from up to 18,000 to under 10,000 in recent surveys *(contested)*. Excavations found Chinese
porcelain, Persian ceramics, Southeast Asian beads and copper ingots. It was abandoned about 1450, with explanations including
exhausted gold, shifting trade routes north (to Mutapa), overgrazing and drought [35]. It is preceded by Mapungubwe on the Limpopo
(about 1220 to 1290), which followed the same pattern of cattle, gold and coastal trade.

The **Swahili coast** (Mogadishu to Sofala, with Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, Zanzibar, Pate) was a string of coral-stone Muslim
trading towns. They exported **gold** (via Sofala), **ivory**, slaves, ambergris, tortoiseshell and **mangrove poles** (cut in the
coastal swamps, sent to treeless Arabia and the Gulf as roof beams); they imported Indian cloth, beads, Chinese porcelain and
Islamic ceramics. Kilwa minted copper coins from about the 11th century and built the palace of Husuni Kubwa in the 14th; Ibn
Battuta called it one of the most beautiful towns in the world (1331). The monsoon allowed one round trip a year, so foreign
merchants waited months in port as **guests of local hosts** who stored and sold their goods: a hosting institution like the
funduq, and the reason the coast's towns were brokers rather than carriers.

### 5.5 West African iron

Iron smelting in sub-Saharan Africa is old. At Taruga (Nok culture, Nigeria) furnaces date to the mid-first millennium BC (dates
from about 600 to 200 BC have been reported) [36]; there were early dates in the Great Lakes and in Niger too. Whether iron was
invented independently in Africa or came from the north (via Carthage or Meroë) is unsettled; the dissimilar furnaces make a
Meroitic origin for West Africa unlikely [36]. Meroë (Sudan) itself has large slag heaps from the 1st millennium BC on.

African smelters built a great variety of **bloomery** furnaces: small bowl furnaces with bellows, and tall natural-draft
furnaces that drew air through ports without bellows. Peter Schmidt and Donald Avery (1978) reported that the Haya of Tanzania
preheated the air blast in their furnaces, reaching higher temperatures than European bloomeries; the temperatures claimed have
since been disputed. Smiths were often a distinct, respected and feared caste (the Mande numu), combining iron with ritual power.
Iron hoes and axes opened the forest and savanna to farming during the Bantu expansion; iron bars and hoes served as money and
bridewealth in many regions. Brass and bronze were cast by lost wax at Igbo-Ukwu (9th to 10th century) and in Ife and Benin;
after 1500 Benin's bronzes used the brass **manillas** (bracelets) the Portuguese brought by the tens of thousands.

## 6. The connecting systems

### 6.1 The Silk Roads

The overland routes from China through Central Asia to Persia and the Mediterranean (the name is Ferdinand von Richthofen's,
1877) opened as a state matter: the Han envoy Zhang Qian's missions (from 138 BC) and the Han conquest of the Tarim basin. What
moved along them was **relay trade**: few merchants went end to end; goods passed from hand to hand through oasis cities
(Dunhuang, Turfan, Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv), each segment run by its own people. The **Sogdians** of Samarkand were the great
intermediaries from the 4th to the 8th century, with communities in Chinese cities and letters that survive [44]. Valerie Hansen
argues that most traffic was local and much of it carried by state demand (the Tang paid its Central Asian garrisons in silk,
which then entered trade) [44].

Goods westward: silk, later porcelain, paper, rhubarb; eastward: horses (the Han and Tang bought them in great numbers), glass,
silver, gold, Central Asian wool, and above all ideas (Buddhism, then Islam), crops (grapes, alfalfa) and techniques (paper
west; glass and the stirrup in various directions). Under the Mongols (13th to 14th century) a single power held the whole road,
issued safe-conduct tablets (paiza) and ran a relay post (yam); trade and travel (Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta) peaked, and the
plague of 1346 travelled the same routes.

### 6.2 The Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean was the largest trade zone before 1500, driven by the **monsoon**: winds blowing from the south-west in summer and
the north-east in winter made sailing across it predictable but seasonal [43]. K. N. Chaudhuri divides it into three segments,
the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea, each with its own sailing season, joined at **emporia** (Aden, Hormuz,
Cambay, Calicut, Melaka, Quanzhou) where merchants waited for the wind and exchanged cargoes. Janet Abu-Lughod's "world system
before European hegemony" (1250 to 1350) linked eight such circuits from Flanders to China [43].

- **Antiquity.** After Greek sailors learned the monsoon route in the 1st century BC, Roman Egypt traded directly with India from
  Red Sea ports (Berenike, Myos Hormos). The **Muziris papyrus** (2nd century AD) records one ship's cargo from Muziris in Kerala,
  the Hermapollon: Gangetic nard, ivory (valued about 7 million sesterces) and pepper, the whole worth near 9 million sesterces,
  of which the state took a quarter in customs [42].
- **Ships.** Dhows of planks sewn with coir cord; Chinese junks with watertight bulkheads (Song and later); the Ming admiral Zheng
  He's voyages (1405 to 1433) reached East Africa.
- **Goods.** Pepper and spices from Kerala and the Moluccas, cloth from India, porcelain and silk from China, horses from Arabia
  and Persia to India, gold and ivory from Africa, timber, rice, and captives.
- **Merchant communities** (Gujaratis, Chettiars, Arabs, Persians, Jews, Armenians, Chinese) lived as resident minorities in
  each port under their own headmen, a pattern the Portuguese broke by force after 1498 with their cartaz system (passes sold
  at gunpoint: selling protection, not goods).

### 6.3 The Columbian exchange and the silver that went to China

**Silver.** Potosí (in modern Bolivia) was found in 1545, and Zacatecas in Mexico in 1546. The Spanish learned to extract silver
from lower-grade ore by **amalgamation with mercury** (the patio process, Bartolomé de Medina at Pachuca, 1554; at Potosí from
about 1571 to 1572), with mercury from Huancavelica in Peru (found in the 1560s) and Almadén in Spain [38]. Viceroy Toledo
reorganised the Inca mit'a into the Potosí **mita**: about 13,000 to 14,000 men a year, drawn from seventeen provinces, about a
seventh of the eligible men, working a year in the mines and mills [38] (a forced-labour system whose effects on the regions
that supplied it can still be measured in household consumption today: Melissa Dell, 2010). Registered Peruvian output rose
from 6.4 million pesos in 1570–74 to 47 million in the 1570s and 64 million in the 1580s after amalgamation [38] *(rough)*.
Spanish America produced about 150,000 tonnes of silver from 1500 to 1800, perhaps 80 per cent of world output (Flynn and
Giráldez; Barrett) [37].

```
Spanish American silver to China (assembly with two chokepoints; forced labour; increasing returns at the mills)
├── ore: Potosí's Cerro Rico; Zacatecas, Guanajuato
├── labour: the mita (forced), plus wage workers (mingas) and, in Mexico, more free and enslaved labour
├── mercury: Huancavelica (Peru), Almadén (Spain) ← a crown monopoly; poisoning of the workers
├── amalgamation: patio process ← salt, copper sulphate, mules to tread the mix, stamp mills on dammed water (Potosí's lakes)
├── food and fuel: Potosí (perhaps 100,000+ people at 4,000 m, rough) fed from valleys hundreds of km away; llama caravans
├── minting: pieces of eight (the peso / Spanish dollar), the world's first global coin
├── routes west: Acapulco → Manila (galleons from 1571, about 2 million pesos, 50 t, a year) → Chinese junks [37]
├── routes east: Seville → Europe's wars and trade → the Baltic, the Levant, the Dutch and English companies → India, China
└── demand: China's Ming state taxed in silver (the single-whip reforms, 1570s to 1580s); gold:silver about 1:6 in China
    against about 1:12 in Spain around 1600, so silver flowed to China until the ratios met (Flynn and Giráldez) [37]
```

Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez date the birth of a global economy to **1571**, when Manila's founding linked the Americas
directly to Asia. China, they argue, was the "sink" that drew silver from Japan and the Americas because its monetary system and
taxes ran on silver after paper money failed; the price gap was the arbitrage that paid for the whole system [37]. How much of the
world's silver finally reached China is debated (a third to a half is often cited *(contested)*). Silver bought silk, porcelain
and tea; Europe could pay for Asian goods mainly because it had American silver.

**Crops, animals and disease.**

| From the Americas | Effect | From the Old World | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| maize | staple in Africa, southern Europe, China's uplands | wheat, barley, rice | colonial farming |
| potato | about a quarter of Old World population and urban growth 1700–1900 (Nunn and Qian, conservative estimate [39]); a sixth to a quarter in a critical review | sugar cane | the Atlantic plantation complex |
| sweet potato | upland settlement in south China from the 16th century (Ho Ping-ti) | cattle, sheep, pigs | ranching; land taken from native farmers |
| cassava | famine crop and staple across central Africa | horses | new mounted economies (the Plains peoples) |
| tomato, chilli, cacao, tobacco | cuisines and new habits (tobacco everywhere) | coffee (from Ethiopia via Yemen) | Brazilian and Caribbean plantations |
| | | **smallpox, measles, influenza** | about 56 million deaths in the Americas by 1600, roughly 90 per cent of a population of about 60 million (Koch and others 2019 [40]) *(contested in detail)* |

The collapse of the American population is the precondition of the colonial economy that followed: land without people,
filled with plantations, cattle and forced labour, and with about **12.5 million** Africans embarked across the Atlantic from
1501 to 1866, of whom about 10.7 million survived the crossing [41].

### 6.4 How the regional chains merged

| Date | Join | What it merged |
|---|---|---|
| ~1900 BC | Assur to Kanesh | tin (from the east) and textiles (Babylonia) with Anatolian silver |
| 5th c BC | Athens and the Black Sea | Crimean and Egyptian grain with Athenian silver and the fleet |
| 1st c BC–2nd c AD | Rome's sea; Red Sea to India | grain, oil, wine, metal across the Mediterranean; pepper and cloth from India for Roman silver |
| 2nd c BC on | the Silk Roads | Chinese silk and Central Asian horses; Buddhism; paper west |
| 8th–10th c | the Abbasid world | Indian sugar, cotton and rice west; paper west; dinars and credit across the zone |
| 9th–16th c | across the Sahara | West African gold into Mediterranean coin; Saharan salt south; Maldive cowries to West Africa |
| 11th–15th c | the Indian Ocean circuits | Chinese porcelain and iron, Indian cloth, Southeast Asian spices, African gold and ivory |
| 13th–14th c | the Mongol roads | the whole of Eurasia under one power, briefly; the plague |
| 1498–1571 | Portuguese and Spanish sea routes | Europe directly with India and the Spice Islands; Manila links the Americas to China |
| 16th–18th c | the Atlantic and the silver cycle | American silver to China and India; Indian cloth to Africa for captives; captives to American sugar; sugar to Europe |

The pattern is that **trade joined regions first through goods of high value per weight** (silk, spices, gold, silver, cloth,
porcelain), carried by relay through many hands and many institutions, and only later and locally through bulk staples (grain to
Rome and Athens, rice up the Grand Canal), which needed water and usually a state. When bulk did move far it was almost always on
a state's demand.

### 7.2 Compared with the European path

| Feature | Medieval and early modern Europe | Elsewhere | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Energy** | wood and charcoal, water and wind mills, animals; coal from the 16th century in England | the same organic base; Song coal in the north; human porters in Mesoamerica | output can be high on an organic base; the switch needs coal near industry |
| **Transport** | rivers, coasts, carts, pack horses | camels instead of wheels (Middle East); canals (China); llamas and porters (Americas); monsoon shipping | transport technology shapes where cities stand and what is traded far |
| **Money** | coin; bills of exchange from the 13th century; banks | coin from Lydia; paper money in Song China; cacao and cloth in Mexico; no money in the Inca state; cowries; silver by weight in Ming China | money is one solution among several; heavy coin invites paper; over-issue destroys paper |
| **Credit and enforcement** | Italian commenda, bills, notaries, courts, guilds | Assyrian partnerships, qirad, suftaja, hundi; reputation networks and qadi courts | the same instruments recur; enforcement is a mix of reputation and courts |
| **The state** | fiscal-military states, late | Han monopolies, Roman annona, Mughal cash revenue, Inca labour tax, Ghana's gold rule | states created demand, held chokepoints, and ran bulk supply far earlier and on a larger scale |
| **Labour** | serfdom, then wage labour; putting-out | slavery (Rome, Athens), corvée and mit'a (China, Inca), caste specialists (India, Africa), forced mining labour (Potosí), plantation slavery | labour's form follows the technology and the chokepoint (mines and sugar drew the worst) |
| **Specialisation** | towns and guilds | Jingdezhen's dozens of trades; India's weaving castes; Song city guilds | very fine divisions of labour existed without factories |

The European path was not the normal one, nor the best one before 1750. It was one of several high-output organic economies, and it
was distinctive mainly in its fragmented states competing in war, its Atlantic access to American land, silver and crops, and
English coal.

## 8. What a simulator could take from this

Not a build list: mechanisms and shapes the history offers that the medieval village frame does not, each with what it would
ask of a world.

1. **State demand calls an industry into being.** The annona did not own ships; it bought them in with privileges, insurance and
   citizenship. A world could let an institution post a standing demand (grain delivered to a capital at a price, with privileges
   to carriers) and see a private carrying trade form to meet it. It is the Roman answer to "who moves bulk far": almost always,
   a state's demand.
2. **Taxation in money forces markets** (Hopkins; the Mughal zabt; the Ming single whip). A tax payable only in coin makes
   households sell, so a market forms where none was. A strong lever for testing whether markets emerge (02zzi §7), and its
   opposite is the Inca: a tax in labour, paid back in food and cloth, needs no market at all.
3. **Staple finance against wealth finance.** Two ways to run a large polity: store food and cloth and pay in kind (Inca qollqa),
   or pay in money and let people buy. Both work; each needs different things (storage, roads and records against coin and
   markets). A world could offer both and watch which an agent-run state chooses.
4. **Chokepoints with tolls and price control.** Al-Bakri's Ghana taxed salt in and out and kept the nuggets to hold the gold
   price; the Han took salt and iron as monopolies; Potosí depended on mercury from two crown mines. A chokepoint invites not just
   tolls but monopoly and supply restriction, and the 81 BC debate shows the arguments a society has about it.
5. **Water by time shares.** The qanat's flow owned in hours of a cycle, measured with a water clock, sold and inherited: a precise
   and historical form for a commons that is also property, with a specialist craft to maintain it.
6. **Joint-product loops at landscape scale.** The mulberry-dike fish pond (leaves, worms, droppings, fish, mud, mulberry) is a
   closed loop in which each output is another's input. It shows what a well-designed web of the economy_web.md kind looks like
   when a society has had centuries to tune it.
7. **Pulses that cannot wait force organisation.** Sugar cane must be crushed in a day or two; Nile grain and Mediterranean
   shipping came in one season; monsoon cloth had to be ready by the sailing date. A pulse that spoils makes field and mill one
   unit under one command (the plantation) or makes merchants advance capital before the season (dadni).
8. **Heavy money breeds paper; over-issue kills it.** Sichuan's iron coins produced deposit receipts, then state notes, then
   inflation, then a return to silver. Money in a world could follow the same arc if both the weight of
   coin and the trust in an issuer are part of its state.
9. **Credit that moves value without moving coin.** The suftaja and hundi let a merchant pay in one city and be paid in another.
   They need correspondents who trust each other. Enforcement in history was a mix of reputation in networks, kin and courts (the
   Maghribi debate); OPEN L-106 is the same question.
10. **Relay trade and hosts.** Long routes were run in segments by different peoples (Sogdians, Saharan nomads, Swahili hosts),
    each taking a margin, with foreign merchants housed and policed in funduqs or by hosts. Long-distance trade in a world might
    be modelled as a chain of local intermediaries rather than one carrier going end to end.
11. **Transport technology sets what goes far.** Camels replaced carts where roads were poor; porters in Mexico limited staple
    trade to a few hundred kilometres; the sea was thirty or more times cheaper than the road in Rome. High value per weight goes
    far; bulk goes far only on water and usually on state demand.
12. **The same product can be made by very different social forms.** Iron by a state monopoly (Han), licensed private works (Song),
    a smith caste (West Africa); cloth by households under advances (India), imperial bureaus (China); silver by slaves (Laurion),
    leased contractors (Roman Spain), forced rotation (Potosí). The chain's technology does not fix the society; a world could hold
    the technology fixed and let the form vary.
13. **Proxies for output.** Rome is measured from lead in ice, shipwrecks and a hill of broken jars. A simulator could record
    the same kinds of indirect traces (slag, discarded containers, wrecked carts, pollution in a river) and test whether agents'
    economies can be read from them, as historians do; and it can check the proxies against the true state, which historians
    cannot.
14. **Scale is not the switch.** Song China had coal-fired iron, water-powered machines, paper money and national markets, and
    did not industrialise. A world that adds output and depth will still sit on an organic ceiling unless it adds a cheap,
    concentrated energy source near the industry and a reason (dear labour) to use it. This marks the edge noted in 02zzi §3.8.
15. **Collapse paths.** Great Zimbabwe when the gold ran out or the routes moved; the Song north after invasion and floods; the
    Andes after the epidemics; Rome's lead curve after the Antonine plague. A deep economy is fragile at its chokepoints, and the
    history offers a catalogue of how each one breaks.
