## Summary

War has been the largest single buyer in most economies that had a state, and force has been a way of organising production as
much as a cost on it. The goods of war are ordinary goods put to a harsh use, and their chains run through the rest of the map. A
composite bow is wood, horn, sinew and glue from the carcass, cured for months; a mail shirt is drawn wire from a bloomery;
gunpowder is three-quarters saltpetre scraped from dung heaps, stables and dovecotes, plus charcoal from the coppice and sulphur
from volcanoes; a 74-gun ship ate about 2,000 oaks; a warhorse ate the oats of several acres. So war competed with farming, tanning,
iron and building for the same few intermediates (dung, oak, charcoal, horses, horn), and its demand for precision and volume
moved those chains: the cannon-boring mill bored Watt's cylinders, the armouries made interchangeable parts, and 20th-century
procurement paid for radar, jet engines, the first computers, nuclear reactors, the first integrated circuits (the US government
bought all of them in 1962 and 72% in 1965), the ARPANET and GPS.

Force also organised economies directly. Feudal tenure was land paid out for armed service: the knight's fee was a holding big
enough to keep a mounted man, his horses and his kit, and the manor's labour dues and rents (part 05 §3) fed it. Castles held
crossings, ports and passes and took tolls. Tribute and plunder economies (Assyria, the Aztecs, the Mongols, the Vikings, whose
Danegeld reached £72,000 from England in 1018 plus £10,500 from London) moved surplus by threat; war made captives and so slaves.
Frederic Lane's "protection rent" names the profit of whoever produces security more cheaply than rivals: Venice's convoys,
Portugal's passes, the chartered companies with their own armies (the East India Company's forces approached a quarter of a million
men by the 1820s). War also made the state. The main lines are Tilly's "war made the state", the military-revolution debate and the
fiscal-military state (the Bank of England began with a £1.2 million war loan at 8%; British debt rose from £12 million in 1700 to
about £850 million in 1815). War finance drove most debasement and inflation, and mass income taxation.

Against that sit destruction (regions that lost a
third of their people), the standing cost of insecurity (walls, hidden stores, hill villages, hoards) and the peace dividends. The steppe nomads' edge (horse archers, grass for fuel,
many mounts a rider) shaped Eurasian trade for two thousand years, through tribute from China, protection of the Silk Roads and the
Mongol peace, until gunpowder and the agrarian empires closed the steppe in the 18th century. For the engine, the main point is that
force is already physics in Cybertron 2 (attacks, raids, guards, walls and gates, `spec/conflict/`; one crude weapon that serves
fighting and hunting, D2-23), but it is not yet an economy: no weapon has a chain beyond one timber, and nothing turns the threat of
a raid into a levy, a rent or a toll. That turn needs the social primitives of OPEN L-106 and part 09 §3 (P1, P4, P7), not more
goods.

**On the figures.** *Checked*: confirmed against a source this session (often a secondary summary of the standard work, named in
Sources). *Rough*: an order of magnitude or a range. *Contested*: scholars disagree materially. *Unchecked*: a standard figure
not re-verified this session (the shared search budget was small). *Memory*: recalled from a named standard work and less often
repeated; check before use.

## 1. Weapons and armour as goods: the chains

### 1.1 Spears, clubs and the sling

- **Hunting and war shared their gear** for most of history. The wooden spears from Schöningen and the stone-tipped spear, the
  spear-thrower and the bow are covered in part 10 §1 as hunting tools; they were the first weapons too. The engine already keeps
  this pairing (D2-23: weapons serve fighting and hunting).
- **The spear stayed the commonest weapon until the musket.** It needs a shaft (ash, hazel or a coppice pole; economy_web §2.4), a
  head (flint, then copper, bronze, iron) and a binding or a socket. A socketed iron head took a smith perhaps an hour or two
  (*rough*). It was the cheap arm of levies, militias and the pike squares.
- **The sling** (cord and stones or cast lead bullets) shows that a weapon can be nearly free while its skill is dear: Balearic
  slingers were hired for theirs (*unchecked*).

### 1.2 The bow: self bows, the longbow and the composite bow

The bow is the clearest case of a weapon as a meeting of chains (economy_web §2.10 lists five: stave, cord, shafts, feathers,
heads).

- **The self bow and the longbow.** One stave, ideally yew (heartwood on the belly, sapwood on the back), otherwise elm or ash; a
  string of flax or hemp; arrows of aspen, ash or birch with goose feathers and iron heads. English yew was poor and scarce, so
  staves were imported from the Alps, Italy and the Baltic through the Hanse; statutes from 1472 required merchants landing goods
  from Venice to bring four bowstaves with every ton (later ten) (*memory*, the standard cite is the 1472 statute). England
  ordered archery practice on Sundays and holidays (1363) and kept the trade to its own bowyers, fletchers and arrowsmiths, which
  were separate London guilds (*unchecked*). Feathers were requisitioned: in the 1410s the crown had sheriffs take six wing
  feathers from every goose in many counties (*memory*). The *Mary Rose* (sank 1545) held about 137 complete longbows and several
  thousand arrows (*memory*). The longbow's chain is cheap in materials and dear in skill: a strong archer took years of practice
  from boyhood, which is why it was replaced by the crossbow and the gun, which took weeks (*rough*).
- **The composite bow** (the Assyrian, Scythian, Hun, Turkish, Mongol, Chinese and Korean bow) packs more power into a short bow
  that can be shot from a horse. It is a thin wooden core with **horn** on the belly (water buffalo, cattle, ibex or goat), **sinew**
  on the back (shredded leg and back tendons of cattle or deer), bound with **glue** boiled from hides, sinew or fish swim bladders,
  and wrapped in bark or leather against damp. It needs the carcass's joint products (part 03 §6, horn and glue; economy_web §2.1)
  and, above all, **time**: each sinew layer must dry before the next, and the bow cures for months; a working week or two of a
  bowyer's labour spreads over a year or more from start to use (*checked*, several secondary sources; estimates run from three
  months to two years). It is **time capital** in economy_web's sense, and humid climates spoil it, which is one reason it stayed
  a dry-land weapon (*rough*).
- **The crossbow** (China from the Warring States, Europe from about the 10th century) moves the skill into the machine: a
  trigger, a stock, a prod and a winding gear. Qin and Han arsenals cast bronze triggers and arrowheads to standard sizes
  (*unchecked*).
- **Arrows are a consumable on a vast scale**, stockpiled by the hundred thousand in English campaigns (*rough*): the fletcher,
  the arrowsmith and the goose are as much the bow's chain as the bowyer.

### 1.3 Bronze arms and the chariot

- **Bronze put metal in the hand of an elite.** Copper with about 10% tin (part 03 §3; part 10 §4) made daggers, spearheads,
  axes, and from about 1600 BC long rapiers and later swords cast in two-piece moulds and hammered to harden the edge
  (*unchecked*). Because tin came from a few places a thousand kilometres away (the tin route, part 10 §4), bronze armies depended
  on long trade, and the Late Bronze Age collapse ran those dependencies backwards (README finding 9).
- **The chariot was a system, not a weapon.** It needed horses bred and trained (the Hittite horse-training text of Kikkuli,
  around 1400 BC, *unchecked*), spoked wheels and bent wood from wheelwrights, leather, bronze fittings, a composite bow and a
  trained archer and driver, and fodder. At Kadesh (1274 BC) Egypt and the Hittites are said to have fielded several thousand
  chariots between them (*contested*). Palace archives (Linear B at Knossos and Pylos) list
  chariots and wheels (*unchecked*); horse and spoked wheel came from the steppe edge (horse_riding_chariots, part 10).

### 1.4 Iron arms and the state arms works

- **Iron was the democratising metal.** Bloomery iron (part 03 §3) used local bog or hill ore and charcoal, so every region could
  arm more men, and iron tools and iron weapons came from the same smith and the same bloom. Assyrian armies were among the first
  equipped largely with iron (*rough*).
- **States built arms factories early.** Late Roman *fabricae* (the Notitia Dignitatum lists some 35, each for shields, armour,
  arrows, artillery or swords: *memory*) were staffed by hereditary workers. The Han iron
  offices (han_salt_iron_monopoly, part 11 §2) made tools and arms together. Song China had arsenals in the capital making
  bows, crossbows, armour and, from the 11th century, gunpowder weapons, with output counted by the million (*memory*).
- **Quality steel was a long-distance good.** Crucible steel from India and Central Asia (wootz, damascus_blades; part 11) and the
  Rhineland's Ulfberht blades, some of them of imported crucible steel (*contested*), show that the edge of a weapon could depend on
  a chain as long as bronze's. Pattern-welding made a good blade from poor
  metal at a high cost in labour and charcoal (*unchecked*).

- **Armour changed the arithmetic of a knight.** A mounted man in mail, with two or three horses, a squire and his keep, needed the
  surplus of a large holding (§2.4). When plate came, the price of a man-at-arms rose again, and so did the minimum size of the
  land that kept him.

### 1.6 The warhorse and its fodder

- **The warhorse was the costliest single item of medieval war.** A destrier in late 13th-century England could be valued at £80,
  and a horse lost by the Earl of Northampton in Flanders in 1339 at £100 (*checked*, secondary summary of R. H. C. Davis); most
  knights' horses were valued much lower, a few pounds to a few tens of pounds at English musters, where the crown recorded their
  value to compensate losses (*memory*, Prestwich). At 1.5d a day for a labourer, £80 was several decades of wages (my arithmetic,
  *rough*). Courser and hackney prices were a fraction of the destrier's (*checked*, secondary: 5 to 35 times dearer).
- **Breeding needed land and studs.** Kings and great lords kept studs (the English royal studs, the Arab and Turkoman horse trade
  to India by sea, part 11 §3; the Song's chronic shortage of horses, §5). Large horses need good grass and oats; poor agrarian
  states without grassland bought horses from the steppe (§5).
- **Fodder bound every army.** A hard-worked horse ate 12 to 16 lb of grain and 12 lb of hay a day, the oats and hay of 2 to 3 ha
  (part 01 §3, *checked* there). An army's horses ate more by weight than its men, and fodder could not be carried far, so
  pre-railway armies foraged, moved to fresh country and campaigned after the harvest when the fields stood full (Martin van
  Creveld, *Supplying War*, *unchecked*). Napoleon's army of 1812 took something like 150,000 to 200,000 horses into Russia
  (*memory*); most died. This is the organic economy's limit in its most violent form: land fed horses or people, and war made the
  horses' claim urgent (README finding 1).
- **Horse gear** (saddle, bit, horseshoe, stirrup) is its own chain; Lynn White's claim that the stirrup made feudalism is widely
  rejected (*contested*).

### 1.7 Siege engines, castles and walls

- **Siege engines were carpentry, rope and sinew.** Greek and Roman torsion artillery stored energy in skeins of sinew or hair
  (Carthage's women are said to have given their hair for the springs in 149 BC: *memory*, anecdote). The counterweight trebuchet
  (in use in the Mediterranean by the late 12th century, *unchecked*) needed timber in great lengths, iron fittings, rope and a
  crew; Edward I's Warwolf at Stirling (1304) is the famous case (*unchecked*). Mines needed miners, taken from the mining districts (*memory*).
- **Castles were building projects on the scale of cathedrals.** The Normans built several hundred earth-and-timber castles in
  England within a generation of 1066 (*rough*), from earth and timber the conquered provided. Edward I's castles in Wales cost about
  £80,000 between 1277 and 1304, several years of royal revenue (*checked*, secondary, after A. J. Taylor); Beaumaris alone cost
  about £14,500, over £6,000 of it in its first six months (*checked*, secondary). In 1295 its master, James of St George,
  reported some 400 masons, about 2,000 less skilled workers, 100 carts, 60 wagons and 30 boats bringing stone and sea coal
  (*memory*; secondary sources give 400 masons, 1,000 labourers, 200 carters). The workers were impressed from across England;
  stone, lime, timber, iron and coal came by sea (part 03 §4 for lime and masonry).
- **Town walls were paid by tolls.** In England the crown granted towns *murage*, the right to take a toll on goods entering for a
  number of years to build or repair the walls, from the early 13th century (*unchecked*). A wall is the joint capital of a town,
  built by a crew and kept by a levy (part 09 P8 and P7 together).
- **The artillery fortress.** From about 1500 the angled bastion (the *trace italienne*) of low, thick earth-backed walls beat
  cannon, but it cost so much that it could ruin a city: Parker's thesis is that these forts lengthened sieges, which needed bigger
  armies, which needed bigger states (§3.2; *contested*).

### 1.8 Gunpowder and saltpetre

**The recipe.** Black powder is roughly 75 parts saltpetre (potassium nitrate), 15 charcoal and 10 sulphur (*unchecked*,
standard). The earliest written formula is Chinese (the *Wujing Zongyao*, 1044, *unchecked*); guns appear in Europe in the 1320s
(*unchecked*). Each ingredient is a chain.

- **Saltpetre** forms where nitrogen from dung and urine rots in soil with lime or ash and dries: under stables, sheep folds,
  dovecotes, privies and cellars, and on old village earth in the dry season in India.
  - **Gathering**: earth is dug, leached through wood ash (potash, part 03), boiled down in copper pans and recrystallised: fuel,
    ash, pans and a skilled boiler (*rough*).
  - **Nitre beds** (plantations) made it on purpose: trenches of dung, urine, rotting vegetation, lime and ash, watered for months
    or years and turned (*checked*, secondary; the beer drinkers' urine was prized). It is the dung that farming needed for
    nitrogen (part 02 §2, the manure gap): **gunpowder and the fields competed for the same nitrogen**.
  - **Saltpetre men**: European states made saltpetre a royal right. In England licensed saltpetre men could enter barns,
    dovecotes and houses to dig, a hated grievance under Elizabeth and Charles I and one of the complaints of 1640 (*memory*, David
    Cressy, *Saltpeter*, 2013). Sweden from Gustav Vasa required peasants and landowners to deliver saltpetre or pay its value, and
    ran saltpetre boilers' districts into the 19th century (*checked*, the 1914 Swedish handbook). France's *salpêtriers* had the
    same rights; the Régie des poudres (1775), with Lavoisier in charge, raised French output and quality sharply (*memory*).
  - **India took the trade**: the dry plains of Bihar produced saltpetre from village earth worked by specialist castes (*memory*),
    and the English East India Company shipped it as ballast-cum-cargo from the mid-17th century. Its 1693 charter obliged it to
    supply the government 500 tons a year (*checked*, secondary), and by 1814 it shipped thousands of tons a year (*rough*). The
    English saltpetre men lost their role. **A chokepoint passed from the farmyard to a chartered company's monopoly.**
  - **Chile and synthesis**: in the 19th century Chilean sodium nitrate (chilean_nitrate) replaced both for explosives and
    fertiliser; Chile took the nitrate fields from Bolivia and Peru in the War of the Pacific (1879 to 1884, *unchecked*). In 1914
    the British blockade cut Germany off; the Haber–Bosch process (synthetic_nitrogen, part 08 §3) and Ostwald's nitric acid made
    explosives and fertiliser from air (*unchecked*). **The same molecule fed people and killed them, and each switch freed one
    from the other's supply.**
- **Charcoal** came from chosen woods (alder buckthorn, willow) in the mills' own coppices; **sulphur** from Sicily and Iceland,
  later pyrites (*unchecked*).
- **Making**: the three were ground and mixed wet in stamp mills or edge-runner mills driven by water; from the 15th century powder
  was *corned* (pressed wet and broken into grains), which burned faster and more evenly and stored better (*rough*). Powder mills
  stood apart for their explosions; England's Chilworth (from 1626 for the East India Company, *unchecked*) and Faversham are the
  type. It is a water-power trade on the same streams as fulling and grain mills (README finding 3).

### 1.9 Cannon and the boring mill

- **Bronze guns were cast by bell founders.** The skills, moulds (loam on a pattern) and furnaces were the bell-founder's; in
  crises bells were melted for guns (Peter the Great after Narva, 1700, *memory*). Bronze was dear (copper and tin) but safe: it
  bulged before it burst.
- **Wrought-iron bombards** were built like barrels, from iron staves hooped with rings (Mons Meg, 1449, *unchecked*); the great
  Ottoman bombards at Constantinople (1453) were cast in bronze on site (*unchecked*).
- **Cast-iron guns** came from the blast furnace (part 03 §3): the Sussex Weald from 1543 (*unchecked*), costing perhaps a third
  or a quarter of bronze (*memory*). England exported them; then Sweden, with Walloon smiths brought by Louis De Geer (Finspång,
  from the 1620s), became Europe's gun founder for the Dutch and others (*memory*). The Carron Company (1759) made the carronade
  (*unchecked*). The cast-iron gun tied cannon to the ironworks, the charcoal woods and later coke.
- **Boring**: guns were first cast hollow on a core; in the 18th century founders cast them solid and bored them out, which gave a
  truer bore and a sounder metal (Jean Maritz, from about 1713; Jan Verbruggen at Woolwich in the 1770s: *memory*). John
  Wilkinson's boring mill (patented 1774) bored the barrel by turning the gun against a fixed cutter; it then bored the cylinders of
  Boulton and Watt's engines (cannon_boring_mill, part 07 §1). **A military precision demand became the steam engine's enabler.**

### 1.10 Handguns and muskets

- **From the arquebus to the flintlock** (late 15th to late 17th century) and the socket bayonet (about 1700), the musket became
  the infantry's single weapon (*unchecked*).
- **A musket was a putting-out product.** Barrels (forged from skelp around a mandrel, then bored and proved), locks (a dozen small
  forged and filed parts), stocks (walnut), furniture (brass), bayonets and ramrods were made by different trades and set up by a
  gunmaker. The great centres were Liège, Brescia and the Gardone valley, Suhl, Birmingham, Tula (state works from 1712) and Sakai
  and Kunitomo in Japan after the *tanegashima* arrived in 1543 (*unchecked*). Birmingham supplied millions of muskets and barrels in
  the Napoleonic wars (*rough*). **Proof houses** (London Gunmakers' Company, 1637; Birmingham, 1813: *unchecked*) fired every barrel
  with an overload and stamped it: a quality institution that made the parts of a putting-out chain tradeable.
- **The musket fitted mass armies**: a recruit trained in weeks, so the cost of an army moved from the man's skill to the state's
  purse (guns, powder, uniforms from the cloth trades, barracks, pay).

### 1.11 Arsenals and dockyards

- **The Venice Arsenal** (venice_arsenal, part 12 §2) built, fitted and stored galleys for the state on something like a flow line:
  1,500 to 2,000 workers in peace, 4,000 to 5,000 in war (part 12; the 16,000 often repeated is overstated). It kept up to a
  hundred galleys in stages of building and storage so a war fleet could be fitted fast; in 1574 it is said to have assembled and
  launched a galley while Henri III of France dined (*checked* that the story is told; its timing varies). Its
  limits showed in the 1470s, when it could not quickly supply thirty galleys (*checked*, secondary).
- **Other state works**: the Ottoman Tophane (cannon foundry) and Tersane (dockyard) in Istanbul; Spain's Seville foundry; the
  Woolwich Arsenal (from 1671, a gun foundry from 1716: *unchecked*); and the royal dockyards (Chatham, Portsmouth, Plymouth), which
  were among the largest industrial establishments in 18th-century Britain, with thousands of workers each in war (*memory*,
  Coleman; Coad). The Portsmouth block mills (1803; portsmouth_block_mills, part 07 §4), where Brunel, Bentham and Maudslay made
  pulley blocks by machine for the navy, were the first mass production with machine tools.
- **Victualling**: the navy's
  Victualling Board (1683, *unchecked*) ran bakeries, breweries and salting works, the largest buyer of cattle, grain and casks
  (*rough*; salt_preservation, brewing, part 02 §5).

### 1.12 Naval timber, naval stores and navies

- **A ship of the line was a forest.** A 74-gun ship took about 3,000 loads of timber, mainly oak with elm, beech, ash and fir
  (*checked*, Naval Miscellany summary, 1803 to 1830), of the order of 2,000 oaks or about 57 acres of mature oak wood (*checked*,
  an early 19th-century Quarterly Review estimate; *rough*). Compass timber (naturally curved oak for knees and frames) was the
  scarcest kind; it grew best in open hedgerow trees, not close woods (*unchecked*). Navies regulated and reserved oak
  (naval_oak_regulation, part 03), and the oak they took was the oak tanners wanted for bark and ironmasters for charcoal (README
  finding 3): **three industries on one tree**.
- **Masts and stores came from far away**: masts from Riga and
  New England (where the crown marked large pines with the broad arrow from 1691, *memory*), hemp from Russia, tar from Sweden
  through the Stockholm Tar Company's monopoly (1648, *memory*), which English bounties on American tar (1705) set out to break. Copper sheathing from the 1770s and 1780s, against shipworm and
  fouling, pulled Anglesey's Parys Mountain copper into the navy (*memory*).
- **Navies were the first standing industrial customers** (ropewalks, sailcloth, anchors, guns), and naval stores became
  diplomacy (the Sound, part 04). **Sea power was paid for by trade and protected trade** (§2.6).

### 1.13 Industrial arms

- **Interchangeable parts came from the armouries** (interchangeable_parts, part 07 §4.3; part 13 §1). French artillery was
  standardised under Gribeauval (from 1765) and Honoré Blanc showed musket locks with interchangeable parts in the 1780s
  (*unchecked*). In the United States the federal armouries at Springfield (1794) and Harpers Ferry (1798) and the Ordnance
  Department paid for it: Eli Whitney's 1798 contract for 10,000 muskets was late and not interchangeable (*unchecked*), but John
  Hall's rifles at Harpers Ferry, made with jigs, gauges and special machines, were judged interchangeable in 1827 (*memory*, Merritt
  Roe Smith). Thomas Blanchard's copying lathe (1818) turned gunstocks. The "armoury practice" then passed to sewing machines,
  typewriters, bicycles and the car (Hounshell, *From the American System to Mass Production*, *unchecked*). In 1854
  Britain bought American machines for the new Enfield works (*unchecked*).
- **Steel guns and the private arms firm.** Alfred Krupp made crucible cast steel at Essen and sold steel guns to Prussia and to
  dozens of other states; Krupp's guns outclassed French bronze in 1870 (*checked*, secondary). Krupp employed some 12,000 to
  16,000 people in the early 1870s and about 71,000 at the centenary of 1912 (*checked*, a 1912 newspaper and two secondary
  sources). Armstrong (Elswick), Vickers, Schneider (Le Creusot) and Škoda made the same shift: an arms firm was a steel firm, a
  forging and machining firm and an exporter, selling to many governments.
- **The naval race drove heavy industry**: ironclads (from 1859), face-hardened nickel-steel armour plate, great forging
  presses, steam turbines, mechanical fire control, and oil fuel, which took the British state into Persian oil in 1914
  (*unchecked*).
- **Explosives** (guncotton 1846, dynamite 1867, smokeless powders from 1884, TNT) tied war to coal tar and acids
  (coal_tar_chemicals, sulphuric_acid; parts 07 §6, 08 §3). Britain's 1915 shell crisis produced the Ministry of Munitions and
  about a million munitions workers (*rough*).

  to 2001 (*checked*, several secondary series). Eisenhower named the "military-industrial complex" in 1961.
- **Total war**: in the Second World War the US built some 300,000 aircraft and about 2,700 welded Liberty ships (*unchecked*).

## 2. Force as economic organisation

### 2.1 Raids, stores and the first walls

- **Violence among foragers and early farmers** was common; the share of adult deaths by violence in archaeological and
  ethnographic samples is put at around a seventh by some (Keeley, *War before Civilization*, 1996; Bowles 2009) and much lower by
  others (*contested*). Massacres such as Talheim (about 5000 BC) show raids on early farming villages (*unchecked*).
- **Stores invite raiding.** Grain in pits and granaries, herds and metal could be taken in a night (part 10's tree: "stores ->
  property, theft, defence"). The answers were walls and ditches round villages, nucleated settlement on defensible sites,
  hidden stores, guards, and alliances. Jericho's wall and tower (about 8000 BC) may be the first (its purpose, flood or war, is
  *contested*).
- **Herds are the most raidable wealth.** Cattle raiding runs from Irish sagas to the border reivers: herds move
  on their own feet (§5).
- **The engine already has this** as physics: raids on buildings and nodes, guards, walls that multiply defenders, and wild raiders
  on fields and stores (§7).

### 2.2 Tribute and plunder economies

| **Rome** (2nd c. BC to 1st c. AD) | booty, indemnities, slaves, then provincial taxes | after the Macedonian booty of 167 BC citizens stopped paying direct tax (*unchecked*); Caesar's Gaul was said to yield a million captives (*contested*) | |

- **The pattern.** Plunder is a one-off, tribute is
  plunder made regular, taxation is tribute administered. In Olson's "roving and stationary bandits" (1993) a bandit who stays has a
  stake in his victims' output, so takes less and protects them (*unchecked*). Tribute needs a count, a schedule, collectors and the
  credible threat of the raid.
- **Plunder economies made little of their own** (Viking silver was mostly Islamic dirhams traded for slaves and furs, *rough*);
  when conquest stopped they became tax economies or shrank.

### 2.3 Slaving from war captives

- **War was the main source of slaves in most slave societies** (part 05 §1.5): Greek and Roman wars, Assyrian deportations,
  African wars feeding the Atlantic trade (atlantic_slave_trade), the Crimean Khanate's raids into Poland, Ukraine and Russia
  (estimates in the low millions over two centuries, *contested*), and Barbary corsairs (Robert Davis's estimate of 1 to 1.25
  million Europeans enslaved between 1530 and 1780, *contested*).
- **The gun-slave cycle.** In West Africa, European guns and powder were traded for captives, and states that raided for captives
  could buy more guns; hundreds of thousands of guns a year reached West Africa by the late 18th century (*memory*, Inikori). How far
  this drove the trade, rather than following it, is *contested*.
- **Ransom was a business**: John II of France's ransom (1360) was 3 million écus (*unchecked*); religious orders ransomed
  captives from North Africa. A captive was capital.
- **Captives went where hands mattered more than skill**: mines (Laurion, laurion_silver), galleys, estates, households. The
  galley fleets of Mediterranean states rowed with slaves and convicts from the 16th century (*unchecked*).

### 2.4 Feudal tenure: land for armed service

- **The idea.** Where money was scarce and armoured horsemen dear, a king paid for cavalry with land: a holding, worked by its
  peasants (part 05 §3), whose rents and dues kept a mounted man. The Carolingian benefice and vassalage (8th and 9th centuries)
  are the usual starting point (*unchecked*).
- **The knight's fee** was the unit: land enough to support one knight, often reckoned in England at about five hides, later at
  about £20 a year of income (*rough*; it varied a great deal). After 1066 William I set each tenant-in-chief a quota of knights
  (*servitium debitum*); the Cartae Baronum of 1166 asked each baron how many knights he had enfeoffed and how many he owed
  (*checked*: the survey and its purpose). Totals of about 5,000 to 7,000 knights' fees are given for England (*contested*,
  unchecked). Service was owed for about forty days a year (*unchecked*), plus **castle-guard**.
- **The fee carried a chain.** A knight with mail, a destrier and two lesser horses, a squire, a groom and keep for them all needed,
  by one rough estimate, the surplus of a few hundred acres and the peasants who worked it (*rough*). Feudal tenure is therefore the
  manor read from the other end: the labour dues, mill tolls and rents of part 05 §3 paid for the man in armour.
- **From service to money.** Scutage (shield money: cash instead of service) appears in the 12th century; John levied it often
  (*unchecked*), and kings used it to hire soldiers who would serve longer than forty days. The money fief (*fief-rente*) paid a
  vassal an annual sum instead of land (*unchecked*). Distraint of knighthood (from 1224) forced holders of £20 of land to become
  knights or pay (*memory*). Over the 13th and 14th centuries English armies became mostly paid by indenture (*unchecked*). **The
  military tenure turned into a tax**, and the land into ordinary property.
- **Parallels elsewhere.** The Islamic *iqta* (Buyids, Seljuks, Mamluks: the right to collect a district's taxes in return for
  keeping cavalry), the Ottoman *timar* (revenue grants to cavalry; tens of thousands of timariots in the 16th century, *memory*),
  the Byzantine military lands of the themes, the Mughal *mansab* rank with its *jagir* revenue assignment (mughal_zabt, part 11 §3),
  and Japanese samurai paid stipends in rice (koku) (*unchecked*). They differ in whether the
  holder had rights over people or only a revenue stream; all pay soldiers with a share of the peasant
  surplus collected locally, because the centre could not collect and move it.

### 2.5 Castles as chokepoints

- **Where castles stood.** On river crossings, mountain passes, harbours, river mouths and the junctions of roads: the places a
  traveller or a cargo had to pass (part 04 §5). A castle could tax what passed and keep out what it did not admit, which made it
  both a protector and a predator.
- **The Rhine tolls.** By the 14th century the Rhine carried dozens of toll stations, many at castles, each charging boats as
  they passed (*memory*; more than sixty is the figure often given). The "robber baron" is the extreme: a castle whose income was
  the trade it threatened. Leagues of towns (the Rhenish League of 1254, the Hanse, part 04) formed partly against them
  (*unchecked*).
- **Castle and town.** Conquest castles came with planned towns: Edward I's boroughs in Wales and the *bastides* of Gascony, with
  walls, a market and settlers given burgage plots (*unchecked*). The castle protected the market, and the market paid the castle.
- **Incastellamento.** In 10th- and 11th-century central Italy, lords gathered scattered peasants into fortified hill villages
  (Toubert's *incastellamento*, *unchecked*).
- **Sieges were usually won by hunger**, so a castle's granary and well were its real defences (*rough*).

### 2.6 Protection rents: Frederic Lane

- **The argument** ("Economic consequences of organized violence", *Journal of Economic History*, 1958, *checked*): governments are
  firms that produce protection, using violence to control violence. Merchants pay for it as a cost of trade (taxes, tributes, tolls, convoy fees, losses). A merchant or a city that obtains protection more cheaply than its rivals earns a **protection rent**: the difference is
  profit, as a Ricardian rent is on good land (*checked*, secondary summaries).
- **Cases.** Venice's state galley convoys (venice_mude, part 12), auctioned to merchant syndicates, sold protected passage at a
  lower cost than an unprotected voyage would bear in losses. The Portuguese *cartaz* (portuguese_cartaz, part 11) sold passes to
  Indian Ocean shippers under threat: selling protection, not goods. The Hanse used embargo and war (part 04). The Dutch and English
  companies (§2.9) bundled trade and force.
- **The theory's descendants.** Tilly's "War making and state making as organized crime" (1985) took Lane's view to its end: the
  state as a protection racket that sometimes delivers real protection. Olson's stationary bandit (§2.2) and North, Wallis and
  Weingast's "limited access orders" (part 06 §5), in which elites share rents to keep the peace, follow the same line.

### 2.7 Mercenaries and military entrepreneurs

- **Contract soldiers** were normal in late medieval and early modern Europe. Italian city-states hired *condottieri* by contract
  (the *condotta*); the Swiss cantons exported pike infantry by treaty, and Swiss pay ("no money, no Swiss") became proverbial; the
  German *Landsknechte* copied them (*unchecked*). Hiring was the way rich, small states (Venice, Florence, the Dutch) turned money
  into force, and it made credit as important as courage.
- **The military entrepreneur.** Colonels raised and owned regiments; Albrecht von Wallenstein raised an army for the Emperor in
  1625 on his own credit and kept it by levying **contributions** on occupied territory, reaching perhaps 100,000 men by 1630
  (*memory*; Redlich, *The German Military Enterpriser*). "War feeds war": the occupied land paid. It was taxation by the army
  itself, without a state's officials.
- **Renting armies.** Hesse-Kassel and other German states rented regiments to Britain; about 30,000 Germans fought for Britain in
  the American war (*unchecked*). Soldiers were an export good for poor states with men to spare.

### 2.8 Privateering, piracy and convoys

- **Privateering** licensed private ships, by letters of marque, to seize enemy shipping and keep the prize after a court ruled on
  it (prize courts: an institution that turned seizure into property). Elizabethan privateering against Spain brought in prizes of
  the order of £100,000 to £200,000 a year in the 1580s and 1590s (*memory*, K. R. Andrews). The Dutch West India Company's capture
  of the Spanish silver fleet at Matanzas (1628) paid shareholders a large dividend (*memory*: about 11.5 million guilders taken).
  Privateering was abolished among most European powers by the Declaration of Paris (1856) (*unchecked*).
- **Convoys** were the defence: Venice's *mude*; Spain's *flota* system, formalised in the 1560s, with two armed fleets a year
  between Seville and the Americas through Havana (*unchecked*); British convoys in the wars of 1793 to 1815 and in both world wars.
  A convoy trades speed and the timing of markets for safety: goods arrive in pulses (economy_web's "pulse"), and the fair or the
  price jumps with the fleet.
- **Insurance prices war**: marine war-risk premiums (insurance, part 04) are the market's price of insecurity, back again with
  Somali piracy and the Red Sea attacks of 2023 to 2024 (*memory*).

### 2.9 Chartered companies with armies

- **The charter bundled trade, monopoly and war.** The Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602) was licensed to make treaties, build
  forts, keep soldiers and wage war in Asia (*unchecked*). Under Jan Pieterszoon Coen it conquered the Banda Islands in 1621 and
  killed or enslaved most of their people to control nutmeg (*unchecked*): force making a monopoly of a spice.
- **The English East India Company** went further. After Plassey (1757) and the grant of the *diwani* of Bengal (1765) it collected
  Bengal's land revenue and used it to pay its armies, which conquered more revenue (*unchecked*). In 1823 its three presidency
  armies held about 237,000 men (Bengal 129,473, Madras 71,423, Bombay 36,475: *checked*, secondary); at their peak they are put at
  250,000 to 280,000 (*rough*), larger than the British Army. **A joint-stock company (joint_stock_company) became a
  fiscal-military state.**
- **Others** (the Hudson's Bay, Royal African and British South Africa companies) each held a chokepoint by force and traded
  through it.

## 3. War and the state

### 3.1 Tilly: war made the state

- **The claim.** Charles Tilly's "war made the state, and the state made war" (1975) and *Coercion, Capital, and European States,
  AD 990–1990* (1990): rulers who wanted to make war had to extract resources, and extraction built the bureaucracy, the tax system
  and the courts; states that could not keep up were absorbed (*unchecked*). Part 05 §5.5 gives Dincecco's evidence on revenue.
- **Three paths.** *Coercion-intensive* states (Russia, Brandenburg-Prussia) squeezed land and peasants through nobles;
  *capital-intensive* city-states (Venice, Genoa, the Dutch) bought force with commercial wealth and credit; *capitalised
  coercion* (England, France) combined both, and won because they could field mass armies paid from commercial economies.
- **Critiques.** Latin American states fought few large wars and stayed fiscally weak (Centeno, *Blood and Debt*, 2002); African
  states with low population densities and fixed colonial borders were not made by war (Herbst, 2000); war breaks states as often as
  it makes them; and the link only appears once war became dear enough that fiscal capacity decided it (Gennaioli and Voth, 2015)
  (*unchecked*, all).

### 3.2 The military revolution debate

- **Michael Roberts** (lecture 1955, published 1956): between 1560 and 1660, Maurice of Nassau's drill and Gustavus Adolphus's
  tactics made smaller, linear units of musketeers; armies grew, war got dearer, and states grew to pay (*unchecked*).
- **Geoffrey Parker** (*The Military Revolution*, 1988) moved it to about 1500 and to the bastion fort, which needed bigger
  armies for longer sieges; he saw armies growing about tenfold and the revolution as the basis of Western dominance overseas
  (*checked*, secondary). David Eltis and others argued his army figures are inconsistent (*checked*, secondary).
- **Others.** Black put the change after 1660, Rogers saw punctuated revolutions from the Hundred Years' War, and Lynn showed
  French paper strengths of the 1690s overstate the men present (*unchecked*). Andrade (*The Gunpowder Age*, 2016) found China's own military revolutions and a divergence only after about 1750
  (*unchecked*).
- **For this map** the mechanism is one chain whatever the date: a
  technique raises the minimum cost of war, which needs revenue and credit, which need officials, records and consent or coercion.

### 3.3 The fiscal-military state, public debt and the Bank of England

- **Britain after 1688** (part 05 §5.3; part 04 §6 on the financial revolution): parliamentary control of taxes, a professional
  excise (excise), and long-term funded debt (funded_public_debt) let Britain outspend France with a third of its population
  (*rough*).
- **The Bank of England** was founded in 1694 to lend the state £1.2 million at 8% for the war with France; the subscription was
  raised in eleven days from 1,268 subscribers (*checked*, Bank of England). The debt grew from about £12 million in 1700 to about
  £850 million in 1815 (*checked*, UK Debt Management Office), close to 180% of GDP after Waterloo (*checked*, OBR; other series
  give 200%). Military spending and debt service together took most of the state's spending in war years (*rough*).
- **The Dutch Republic** pioneered it (provincial bonds, low rates; part 04); **Spain** under Philip II borrowed from Genoese
  bankers on short contracts (*asientos*) and suspended payments several times (1557, 1560, 1575, 1596, *unchecked*; Drelichman and
  Voth argue these were renegotiations built into the contracts, *contested*); **France** relied on tax farmers and sold offices,
  and the debt of the American war brought on the crisis of 1788 to 1789 (*unchecked*).

### 3.4 War finance and inflation

- **Debasement** was the old way to pay for war: the Roman denarius, Henry VIII's Great Debasement of 1544 to 1551 (coin_debasement,
  part 04 §2).
- **Paper money and war**: the Continental dollar, the assignats, Britain's suspension of gold payments (1797 to 1821), the
  greenbacks, the suspensions of 1914 and Germany's 1923 hyperinflation (*unchecked*): war was the commonest cause of high
  inflation before the 1970s (*rough*).
- **Taxes that stayed.** Pitt's income tax (1799) for the French war, repealed in 1816; the US Civil War income tax; the
  Sixteenth Amendment (1913) and the First World War; in the Second, US income tax by withholding (1943) and a jump from a few
  million to over forty million taxpayers (*memory*). Keynes's *How to Pay for the War* (1940) proposed deferred pay to keep
  demand down. Peacock and Wiseman's "displacement effect" (1961): spending rises in war and does not fall back to its old level
  (part 05 §5.6 for the welfare state that followed) (*unchecked*).

## 4. Destruction, insecurity and the peace dividend

### 4.1 What war destroys

- **People.** The Thirty Years' War is the classic case: the textbook loss for Germany is 15 to 20% (Britannica), Günther Franz's
  older estimate was about 40%, and Quentin Outram's work supports a figure of that order, with some regions losing over half
  (*checked*, *contested*). Much of the death was disease and hunger from armies living off the land, not battle.
- **Capital and land.** Medieval armies devastated deliberately: the English *chevauchées* of the Hundred Years' War burned
  villages, mills and crops to cut the French crown's revenue (*unchecked*). The Mongol conquests broke irrigation works in Iraq
  and Iran (*contested* how far). Scorched earth was policy in Russia in 1812 and in 1941 (*unchecked*).
- **Recovery is often fast**: bombed Japanese cities regained
  their relative sizes within about fifteen years (Davis and Weinstein, 2002, *unchecked*). Know-how survives the loss of
  buildings; lost people, institutions and routes mend slowly (README finding 9).

### 4.2 Insecurity as a standing tax: walls and storage as insurance

Insecurity costs most when nothing is happening, because people arrange their lives against it.

- **Walls and stone.** Building in stone, behind walls, with vaults; the engine's stone multiplier and vault are this choice
  (§7). Town walls were the largest public work of most medieval towns (§1.7).
- **Stores as insurance and as targets.** Granaries, hidden pits, and goods spread over several places hedge against both bad
  harvests and raids. The Inca's storehouses and China's ever-normal granaries (part 11; ever_normal_granary) fed armies and the
  hungry from the same store.
- **Settlement shape.** Nucleated, hilltop or walled villages (incastellamento, §2.5), longer walks to fields, fewer outlying farms
  (*rough*).
- **Portfolio choices**: hideable
  wealth (coin hoards cluster in wars, *rough*) and crops soldiers cannot easily seize: William McNeill argued that the potato,
  left in the ground until needed, protected northern European peasants from foraging armies (*memory*, *contested*).
- **Higher interest and shorter horizons.** Risk raises the rate on loans and the premium on insurance (§2.8), and shortens the
  horizon of investment: no one plants an orchard where armies pass (*rough*).

### 4.3 War as demand

- **Armies are concentrated markets.** The Roman army of the Principate, some 300,000 to 450,000 men on the frontiers (*rough*),
  drew grain, leather, metal and pottery to the Rhine, the Danube and Britain, and Keith Hopkins's model has taxes paid in the core
  spent on the frontier, which pulled trade along the roads (*unchecked*). Purveyance (part 05 §5.2) and victualling did the same.
- **Wartime booms.** War raised demand for iron, cloth, leather, ships, horses and food, and depressed trade that the war cut.
  Whether war spending creates growth (military Keynesianism) or crowds out other output is *contested*; for mobilisations that
  used idle labour, as in the US from 1940, output rose fast.

### 4.4 The peace dividend

- **Pax Romana, Pax Mongolica, Pax Britannica.** Long peace across a large area lowered protection costs and widened markets:
  Mediterranean shipping in the early empire (shipwreck counts peak in the first centuries BC and AD, part 11, *rough*); the Silk
  Roads under the Mongols (§5); the cheap sea protection of the 19th-century Royal Navy (*rough*).
- **The 1990s.** US defence spending fell from about 6% of GDP in 1986 to about 3% around 2000 (*checked*, secondary), and other
  NATO members cut similarly; the "peace dividend" went to deficit reduction and other spending (*rough*). After 2014 and 2022 the
  trend reversed in Europe (*memory*).
- **"Doux commerce"** (Montesquieu) and Angell's *The Great Illusion* (1910) held that trade makes war unprofitable; the idea
  survives, against today's export controls and chokepoint politics (export_controls, part 13 §6).

## 5. Steppe nomads: the military edge and Eurasian trade

- **Why the steppe could fight.** Horse archers with composite bows (§1.2); horses fed on grass, not oats, so cavalry cost no
  arable land; several horses a rider, so armies moved fast and far (a Mongol rider with three to five remounts is often given,
  *contested*); every man a hunter and rider from childhood, with the great hunt (the *nerge*) as drill (*unchecked*). The steppe
  had the one military input that farming states found hardest to make: horses in numbers.
- **Why it needed its neighbours.** Nomads lacked grain, metal and cloth (Khazanov, part 06 §3.1), and got them by trade, tribute or
  raid. Barfield's "outer frontier strategy" (part 06): steppe confederations (the Xiongnu, the Turks, the Uighurs) raided China
  to extract tribute and trading rights, then kept the peace while paid.
- **What China paid.** The Han's *heqin* treaties sent silk, grain and wine to the Xiongnu from 198 BC; the Song paid the Liao
  200,000 bolts of silk and 100,000 taels of silver a year after the Treaty of Chanyuan (1005) (*unchecked*). Silk paid to nomads
  went west along the Silk Roads (silk_roads), so **tribute fed long-distance trade**.
- **The horse as a chokepoint.** China could not breed enough warhorses on its farmland. The Song set up a Tea and Horse Agency
  (from the 1070s) to trade Sichuan tea for Tibetan and Inner Asian horses (*memory*, Paul Smith, *Taxing Heaven's Storehouse*);
  the Ming ran tea-horse markets on the frontier. Walls, garrison farms (*tuntian*) and horse markets were the farming state's
  answers (*unchecked*).
- **Protection of the routes.** The Sogdian merchants under the Turks, and above all the Mongols, who held the whole road: safe
  conducts (*paiza*), relay posts (mongol_yam), merchant partnerships financed by the nobility (*ortogh*), and a peace in which
  travel from Italy to China peaked in the early 14th century, and plague travelled the same way in the 1340s (part 11 §6).
- **The steppe closed.** Gunpowder and the agrarian empires' revenue ended the steppe's edge. Russia advanced with Cossack
  fortified lines; the Qing destroyed the Dzungars in the 1750s (Perdue, *China Marches West*, *unchecked*). After that, the
  steppe was a frontier of farmers, not a source of conquerors. (A late echo: the Comanche raiding and trading empire on Spanish horses, Hämäläinen, *unchecked*.)

## 6. How the chains of war joined the rest of the web

  for new techniques that then spread to civil use.
- **Force made the institutions other chains needed.** The knight's fee is the other face of the manor; castles made markets and
  tolls; navies paid for naval stores trades and convoys; war debt built the bond market and the central bank; chartered companies
  joined the joint-stock company to force.

The reading: **force is physics in the engine, but not yet an economy.** The weapon has no chain (one timber); there is no
armour, no horse for war and no siege. Nothing turns a threat into a flow: a raid takes goods once, but no rule turns the raid into
tribute, the guard into a lord, or the gate into a toll. Those are social steps (P1 parties beyond the household, P4 enforcement by
people, P7 rules that create obligations), the same ones part 09 ranks first.

## 8. What the border would take, and what stays map only

Read against 02zzk's tiers. Nothing here is proposed; this sorts the domain for when a research question asks.

**Inside the medieval village and market town (rows on existing handlers).**
- *The weapon as a chain:* a spear with a shaft from the coppice and a copper or iron head from the smith; the bow from a stave,
  cord (flax or sinew), shafts and feathers; the composite bow as horn, sinew and glue from the carcass, cured over many days (time
  capital). These connect to the carcass's joint products, the coppice and the smith that economy_web's stage 1 and 4 already name,
  and to D2-23's pairing of hunting and fighting.
- *Armour as a defender's good:* hide armour from the tanner (economy_later.md); it makes the tannery and the smith matter to
  defence.
- *Walls and stores as insurance:* the stone house, the vault, the walled yard and the granary already exist; the map adds only that
  insecurity shapes where and how people store and build (§4.2).
- *The horse for riding and war* with its oats: the same fodder row 02zzk already lists, with war as one more claim on land.

**Behind the social primitives (L-106; part 09 P1, P4, P7).**
- *Tribute as a regular raid:* a levy owed to a party under threat of the raid (Olson's stationary bandit, §2.2), which needs a rule
  that creates obligations and an act of enforcement.
- *Land for service:* a tenancy whose leg is armed service or guard duty when called (the knight's fee and castle-guard, §2.4), and
  its commutation into money (scutage).
- *The toll at a gate or ford:* the castle's chokepoint (§2.5), on gates.md's keeper and rule; murage as a toll for building walls.
- *Hired guards and the watch* paid by subscription (the agreements preset), as the first protection sold (§2.6).
- *Captives and ransom:* detention as an act, and a captive's release for payment (P4).

**At the border (the market town).** The town wall and its murage, the castle with a market and planned plots, the fair protected by
the lord's peace, purveyance by a lord's officers; the armourer, bowyer and fletcher as town trades.

**Past the border: map only.** Gunpowder and the saltpetre men, cannon and boring mills, muskets and proof houses, arsenals,
dockyards and navies, naval stores; mercenary armies and contributions; privateering and convoys; chartered companies with armies;
the fiscal-military state, public debt and war inflation; industrial arms and 20th-century procurement; steppe empires and their
tribute. These need populations that are not agents, a hierarchy of places and states as parties (part 09 additions 6 to 8), or eras
a run never reaches.
