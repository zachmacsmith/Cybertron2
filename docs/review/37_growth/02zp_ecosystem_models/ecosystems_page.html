Ecosystems on Generated Land

Seven models run on the terrain generator's own land: 18 configurations, 3 seeds, 144 villages. They ask what the ecology would have to do to serve the simulation's aims, calibrate it against real cases, and compare it with what the engine does now.

Full note: docs/review/37_growth/02zp_ecosystems_deep_dive.md · models: 02zp_ecosystem_models/

What the models found

  1. The engine's wild food is flat, uniform and too fast. Forage gives about 4× a village's need in every season and every place. Game regrows each night at the rate red deer manage in a year, about 25× a real population's flow. Real yields give a lean winter and spring, and big differences between fen, wood and farmland.

  2. The seasonal year is data only. The built periodic rule gives a spring gap with the same yearly take. One catch: gathering is scored against the resident stock, so a seasonal pulse multiplies the yield of each unit of work.

  3. Plants placed by conditions work. Forty kinds on Ellenberg-style scales reproduce 69% of the generator's settled map from soil, water and height alone. Most disagreements are the generator's simplifications: open fen and low moor would really be carr and wood.

  4. Succession is real and slow. Calibrated on Broadbalk, Geescroft, heath, downland and Wicken Fen, it takes 10 to 45 years: 300 to 1,500 engine days. It belongs in the generator's history and in long cheap runs, never inside an LLM run.

  5. The animal web behaves as described, on a decade scale. Felling half a big wood raises wolf kills of stock 4 to 5× for a decade. Open-access hunting empties the woods near villages while refuges keep the catch going. Wolves slow the woods' loss to browsing from 20% to 6% a century.

  6. Variety alone does not make villages need each other. Goods per region rise from 5 to 16, but dependence links only from 3 to 5. Links come from a few scarce needs (salt, hay, wood) and from seasonal surpluses that keep.

  7. Use decides who wins. Draining a fen doubles a village's grazing and cuts its eels, fowl and rushes about tenfold. The cattle-keeper gains; the fowler, eel-trapper and reed-cutter lose.

What to build, in order

  1. The seasonal wild year, by kind and ecosystem. Periodic stocks with real yields, and a yearly draw for mast, nuts and berries. Data, plus one small mechanism and the density cap. Visible in every run.
  2. Scarce needs that create links: hay through winter, salt for keeping, peat and furze where wood is short.
  3. Movement between places (living_populations step 1), with game on the year clock. Together only: the clock alone makes hunting useless.
  4. Conditions and kinds in the generator: split "grassland" by use, add wood pasture, hedges and water ecosystems, spread villages across systems, and check that day 0 is an equilibrium.
  5. Use that holds vegetation (grazing, mowing, cutting, burning, draining) as yearly parcel state, for long runs and the generator's history.
  6. Mast and boar: a yearly cycle a two-year run sees.
  7. Wolves, deer and the wood: decades, except the switch to herds after a felling, which a run can see.

Not worth it: competition dynamics between plant species, seed dispersal, soil formation, food webs deeper than three levels, succession inside runs.

The probe, prepared and not run

Does a hungry gap change what people do? Two arms of the Ferrybank world, 28 days from late summer. They differ only in when the wild food comes: flat, or seasonal through the periodic rule. The engine's capacity model gives both the same year: wild food feeds 1.67 against 1.85 people, worked land 1.67× against 1.83× need. Both arms run clean with bots.
